1. Money in and money out

1.1 A household

Start with your own household. Money comes in and money goes out.

Suppose a household earns A$6,000 a month after tax. The rent is A$2,200, food is A$900, transport is A$400, the phone, power and internet total A$500, and everything else is A$1,000. Add up what goes out:

2,200 + 900 + 400 + 500 + 1,000 = 5,000

Money in is 6,000. Money out is 5,000. The difference is 6,000 minus 5,000, which is 1,000 left over. That A$1,000 goes into savings.

Now ask a different question. It is the 28th of the month. The rent is due on the 1st. The household has A$1,500 in its bank account and A$20,000 in a term deposit that it cannot touch for another 10 months. Can it pay the rent of A$2,200 on the 1st?

The household is "wealthy" on paper: A$21,500 in total. But the rent is due in three days, and only A$1,500 is available now. The A$20,000 is locked away. Unless the household gets its next pay in time, it may not be able to pay the rent on the day it is due. It is not poor. It is short of cash at the right time.

That, in a sentence, is the whole subject of this pack. Liquidity is about having the right amount of cash available at the moment a payment is due. A household can be rich and still be short of cash this week. A bank can be the same, only with billions of dollars instead of thousands.

1.2 A shop

A shop adds a second idea: it buys things before it sells them.

A corner shop buys A$10,000 of goods from a supplier on the 1st. The supplier gives it 30 days to pay. The shop sells the goods slowly, over about 60 days, and collects A$14,000 from customers. Over 60 days the shop makes a profit of 14,000 minus 10,000, which is A$4,000.

But look at the timing. On day 30 the shop must pay the supplier A$10,000. By then, say, it has sold only half of the goods and collected A$7,000. It is short by 10,000 minus 7,000, which is A$3,000, on the day the bill is due. The shop is profitable. It will make A$4,000 in the end. But on day 30 it can be forced to close, or borrow, because it did not have the cash when the bill arrived.

Two lessons:

  • Profit and cash are different things. A business can be profitable and run out of cash.
  • Timing matters. What counts is not only how much you have, but when the money arrives against when the money must leave.

The shop might solve the problem by borrowing A$3,000 from its own bank for a few weeks. Keep that thought. Who does the bank borrow from when it has the same problem?

1.3 A bank

A bank is a business, too. Money comes in (people deposit their pay, the bank collects loan repayments, the bank borrows from investors). Money goes out (customers withdraw, the bank makes loans, the bank repays its own borrowing, the bank pays wages and tax).

The difference from the shop is the scale and the speed. A bank moves enormous sums every day. Customers can often take their money out the same day. The bank has promised to hand it over. Because so much of what the bank owes can be demanded at short notice, the bank has to think about cash every single day.

From the controller: You will hear people in the team say "we are long" or "we are short" of liquidity. Do not worry about the slang yet. Translate it to the household and the shop: it means "do we have enough cash at the right time?"

2. What a bank actually is

A bank does three simple things:

  1. It takes in money from people who have spare money and want it kept safe (savers).
  2. It lends money to people who need money now and will repay it later (borrowers), such as someone buying a home.
  3. It moves money for customers: pays bills, receives salaries, transfers funds to other banks.

The bank earns its living from the gap between what it pays for money and what it charges for it. If it pays savers 2% a year and charges home buyers 6% a year, the gap is 4 percentage points. On every A$100 it lends, it earns A$4 a year before costs. (This gap, as a share of the loans, is called the net interest margin. You will meet it again in the guide, Guide Ch 3 s3.7.)

In Australia, a bank is one of a group of institutions called authorised deposit-taking institutions, or ADIs. An ADI is a business that APRA has authorised to take deposits from the public under the Banking Act 1959. You will see "ADI" all through APS 210, because the standard applies to every ADI (APS 210 para 2). Banks, building societies and credit unions are all ADIs. In this pack, "the bank" means an ADI.

Why does the government watch ADIs so closely? Because the money in them is other people's savings. If a bank fails, ordinary families lose their savings, and the failure can spread to other banks. We come back to this in section 10.

3. Deposits are money the bank owes. Loans are money owed to the bank.

This is the single most important idea in the document. Read it twice.

3.1 A deposit is a debt of the bank to you

When you put A$1,000 in your bank account, it feels like your money sitting in a safe with your name on it. It is not. The bank takes your A$1,000, uses it (lends it to someone else, buys securities with it), and promises to give you A$1,000 back when you ask. Legally, you have lent the bank A$1,000. The bank owes it to you.

So a deposit is a liability of the bank. A liability is simply something the bank owes. Whenever you see the word "liability", think "money the bank owes to someone else".

Deposits come in kinds, and the differences matter a great deal for liquidity:

  • At-call deposits (also called demand deposits): the customer can take the money out any time, today if they wish. Your everyday transaction account is at call.
  • Term deposits: the customer agrees to leave the money for a fixed time, for example six months, in return for a higher interest rate. Taking it out early usually costs a penalty, or is not allowed.

3.2 A loan is a debt to the bank

When the bank lends A$500,000 to a home buyer, the bank has handed over A$500,000 now and is owed A$500,000 back, plus interest, over perhaps 25 years. That promise is an asset of the bank. An asset is something the bank owns or is owed that has value. Whenever you see "asset", think "something that has value to the bank", for example cash, a loan, or a bond.

Notice the mirror image:

Item What it is Whose debt? Name on the bank's books
Customer deposit Money the bank must give back The bank owes the customer Liability
Home loan Money the customer must pay back The customer owes the bank Asset

A deposit is a liability for the bank, and an asset for you. A loan is an asset for the bank, and a liability for the borrower. It always depends on whose books you are reading. In this pack, you are always reading the bank's books.

3.3 Securities and bonds

Banks also hold securities: tradeable financial instruments. The most common are bonds. A bond is a loan that has been cut into tradeable pieces. When the Australian Government wants to borrow, it issues bonds. An investor who buys a A$1,000 bond is lending the government A$1,000 and will receive interest and then the A$1,000 back on a set date. Because bonds can be sold to someone else before that date, a bond can be turned into cash much faster than a home loan. This is why banks hold government bonds as a cash reserve.

Banks also issue bonds themselves: they sell their own bonds to investors to borrow money. That borrowing is called wholesale funding: money borrowed from large investors and businesses rather than from ordinary retail customers. (Retail means individuals; wholesale means large organisations.) A bond the bank issues is a liability, because the bank owes the investor.

4. The balance sheet: two lists that must total the same

4.1 The idea

A balance sheet is a snapshot, on one date, of two lists:

  • Left list: what the bank owns or is owed (assets).
  • Right list: where the money came from (liabilities and equity).

The two lists must always total the same. Here is why, using a house.

You buy a house for A$800,000. You paid A$100,000 from your own savings and borrowed A$700,000 from a bank.

Assets (what you own)                       800,000   (the house)
Liabilities (what you owe)                  700,000   (the home loan)
Equity (what is left for you)               100,000   (your own savings put in)

Check: liabilities plus equity is 700,000 + 100,000 = 800,000. That equals the assets. Every dollar of asset was paid for by someone: either lenders (a liability) or owners (equity). So:

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

Equity is the owners' stake: what would be left for the owners if the bank sold all its assets for their stated value and repaid everything it owes. For a bank, equity is also called shareholders' equity because the bank's shareholders own it.

If the house price rises to A$900,000, assets rise to 900,000, liabilities stay at 700,000, so equity becomes 900,000 minus 700,000, which is A$200,000. If it falls to A$650,000, equity becomes 650,000 minus 700,000, which is minus A$50,000: you owe more than you own. Hold on to that last case. It is what "insolvent" means (section 8).

5. Why a bank cannot pay everyone at once

5.1 Borrowing short, lending long

Look at the balance sheet again from the point of view of time.

On the right side, a large share of what Example Bank owes can be demanded very soon:

Retail at-call deposits        40.0   (25.0 + 15.0)
SME deposits                    5.0
Corporate deposits             10.0   (4.0 + 6.0)

At-call deposits               59.0

A$59.0bn of deposits can legally be withdrawn on demand. (Strictly, the SME and corporate deposits may carry notice periods; for this picture we treat them as available quickly.) Plus A$1.0bn of wholesale debt falls due within 30 days.

On the left side, look at what the bank has lent:

Residential mortgages            50.0   (repaid over decades)
Business loans over 1 year       15.0
Other debt securities > 1 year    3.0
                                -----
Money committed for years        68.0

A$68.0bn of the bank's money is tied up for more than a year. Home loans may take 25 or 30 years to be repaid in full. The bank cannot call in a mortgage on demand just because it needs cash. The home buyer has a contract.

So Example Bank has A$59.0bn of promises to pay on demand, and only A$17.0bn (cash A$5.0bn plus government securities A$12.0bn) that can be turned into cash quickly. This mismatch, borrowing short and lending long, is the basic business of banking. It is called maturity transformation: the bank takes money that savers may want back at any time (short maturity) and turns it into long loans that people can repay slowly (long maturity). It is useful: it is how a family gets a 30-year mortgage without needing to find a lender who will lock up cash for 30 years.

5.2 The risk

The trick works because of statistics. On a normal day, only a small fraction of depositors withdraw. Money that goes out is mostly matched by money coming in (new deposits, loan repayments). The bank needs to keep only a modest amount of cash.

Suppose, in a normal week, 2% of the at-call deposits are withdrawn and nothing comes in:

2% of 59.0 = 59.0 × 0.02 = 1.18

A$1.18bn goes out. The bank has A$17.0bn of cash and government securities. No problem at all.

Now suppose, in a frightening week, 10% are withdrawn:

10% of 59.0 = 59.0 × 0.10 = 5.90

A$5.90bn goes out. The bank still has A$17.0bn of quick assets, so it can cope. Suppose 30% are withdrawn:

30% of 59.0 = 59.0 × 0.30 = 17.70

A$17.70bn goes out. Now the bank's quick assets of A$17.0bn are not enough: 17.70 minus 17.00 leaves it A$0.70bn short. It would have to sell loans or borrow, and in a panic it may not be able to. And if 100% were withdrawn, A$59.0bn, the bank could not pay: it only has A$17.0bn of quick assets, and the rest is lent out for years.

So the bank cannot pay everyone at once. This is not a sign of dishonesty. It is how every bank works. It is why a bank has to hold a buffer of quick assets, and why regulators set minimum buffers.

In the standard: APS 210 begins with the principle that an ADI "must at all times maintain sufficient liquidity to meet its obligations as they fall due and hold a minimum level of liquid assets to survive a severe liquidity stress" (APS 210 para 12).

6. What "liquidity" means

6.1 Two meanings, both important

The word "liquid" comes from water: something that flows. In finance, there are two related meanings, and APS 210 deals with both (APS 210 para 20).

Funding liquidity is the bank's ability to pay what it owes when it falls due. It is the household's question: can I pay the rent on the 1st? A bank with funding liquidity can meet withdrawals and repay its debts on time. A bank that cannot, has a funding liquidity problem.

Market liquidity is how easily an asset can be turned into cash without losing much value. A government bond can be sold in minutes at close to its stated price. A bespoke loan to a small factory cannot be sold quickly at all.

The two feed each other. If the bank must sell assets in a hurry because depositors are leaving (a funding problem), it may have to sell at bad prices (a market problem), which cuts its equity and makes depositors more nervous (a funding problem again). (Guide Ch 1, "Funding liquidity risk and market liquidity risk".)

Liquidity risk is the risk that a bank will not have enough cash at the right time to meet its obligations.

6.2 What is a liquid asset?

A liquid asset is one you can turn into cash quickly, in large amounts, at a price close to its stated value, even when markets are nervous. It is a spectrum. Think of what you could do to pay an urgent A$10,000 bill:

Your asset How fast to cash What you get How liquid?
A$10,000 in your wallet Instantly A$10,000 Perfectly liquid (it is cash)
A$10,000 in your savings account Same day A$10,000 Very liquid
Shares you can sell on the exchange Two days to settle Maybe A$9,500 to A$10,500, depending on the market Fairly liquid, but the price moves
A rental property you own Months Maybe A$20,000 less than the value if you must sell fast Illiquid
Your car, if you must sell today Days A discount Illiquid

A bank's version of the list is:

  • Notes and coin and money at the RBA: instantly cash.
  • Australian Government and state government bonds: can be sold, or lent against to borrow cash, in a day or less, even in a crisis.
  • Other bonds: can often be sold, but in a crisis buyers may disappear or demand a big discount.
  • Home loans and business loans: cannot be sold quickly in volume.

6.3 High-quality liquid assets (HQLA)

APS 210 defines a special list of assets that count as high-quality liquid assets, or HQLA. HQLA are assets that are easy and quick to turn into cash, even under severe stress, and hold their value. There are classes:

  • HQLA1 is the best. It includes notes and coin, balances at the RBA (to the extent they can be drawn in times of stress), and certain government securities (Att A para 9). HQLA1 counts at its full market value, with no discount.
  • HQLA2A and HQLA2B are the lesser classes. They count only after a discount (a "haircut") and are limited in size (Att A para 6, Att A para 10 and Att A para 12).

In the standard: In Australian dollars, APRA's liquidity FAQ says that, at present, only notes and coin, balances with the RBA, and Australian Government and semi-government securities are HQLA1, and no Australian dollar assets currently qualify as HQLA2. For Example Bank, which has one currency, everything in the liquid asset stock is therefore HQLA1.

A haircut is a percentage cut taken off the value of an asset to allow for the chance that it will sell for less than its stated value in a crisis. A 15% haircut on a A$100 bond means it counts as 100 × (1 − 0.15) = 100 × 0.85 = A$85. We will not need haircuts for Example Bank's numbers, but you will use them in your job (document 08).

An encumbered asset is one that is already pledged as security for something else (for example, handed over as collateral for a loan), so the bank cannot freely sell it. An unencumbered asset is free. HQLA must be unencumbered to count (Att A paras 22 to 23). A government bond locked away as collateral is a good asset, but it is not available to meet a surprise withdrawal.

To monetise an asset means to turn it into cash, either by selling it or by borrowing against it in a repurchase agreement ("repo": you hand over a bond for cash and promise to buy it back later at a slightly higher price).

Example: Example Bank's A$12.0bn of government and semi-government securities is HQLA1 if it is free (unencumbered) and under the control of the Treasury team. If A$2.0bn of it were pledged to another bank as security for a loan, only A$10.0bn would count.

7. What a bank run is

A bank run is when many depositors, fearing the bank may fail, rush to withdraw their money at the same time. It is the nightmare scenario for a bank, and it can happen even to a bank that is perfectly healthy, because of the arithmetic in section 5.

Here is how it unfolds, in stages.

  1. A rumour or a bad piece of news. For example, the bank reports a big loss, or social media says it is in trouble.
  2. A few depositors withdraw. They reason: "If I am first in the queue, I get my money; if I am last, there may be none left."
  3. Others notice and copy. The more people withdraw, the more worried everyone else becomes.
  4. The bank sells assets quickly to fund the withdrawals. It sells government bonds first (the easy ones). When those are gone, it must sell other assets at a discount.
  5. Losses on the forced sales eat into equity, which scares more depositors and investors.
  6. The bank runs out of liquid assets and cannot pay. Without outside help, it fails.

The key point: a run is self-fulfilling. Even if everyone's money was safe at the start, enough people leaving at once can make the bank unable to pay. This is why confidence matters so much, and why a bank's liquidity position is not only a number but a story that depositors and investors believe (Guide Ch 1, "Anatomy of a bank run").

Modern runs can be very fast, because customers can move money by phone in seconds. That speed is one reason a bank watches its liquidity every day, not once a month.

7.1 Three safety nets

Society puts three things in place to reduce the chance of a run:

  1. Deposit insurance. In Australia, the Financial Claims Scheme (FCS) guarantees deposits up to A$250,000 per account holder per ADI. If an ADI failed, the government would repay each depositor up to that limit. Most households are fully protected, and that is why a small saver does not usually need to run. Large deposits above A$250,000 are not protected, and so those depositors are much more likely to run.
  2. The lender of last resort. The RBA can lend to an ADI that is under severe stress and cannot get funds elsewhere. This is called Exceptional Liquidity Assistance (ELA) (APS 210 para 70). It is truly a last resort: a bank must notify APRA first (see document 03 and document 11).
  3. Rules that require buffers. This is APS 210 itself, and the main subject of this pack.

8. Solvency, liquidity, capital and profit: four different things

People mix these up all the time. Learn the difference now.

Idea The question it answers In one sentence A simple sign of trouble
Liquidity Can the bank pay what falls due in the next days and weeks? Having enough cash, or assets that quickly become cash, at the right time. Depositors are leaving and the bank has no quick assets left.
Solvency Does the bank own more than it owes? Assets are worth more than liabilities, so equity is positive. Equity falls below zero: the bank owes more than it owns.
Capital How big is the cushion that absorbs losses? The owners' money in the bank (equity and similar instruments) that can take losses before depositors are hurt. Capital falls below the level APRA requires.
Profit Is the bank earning more than it spends this year? Revenue minus costs over a period. The bank makes a loss for a period.

8.1 Three short cases

Case 1: solvent but illiquid. A bank owns A$100bn of assets and owes A$92bn, so equity is A$8bn and it is solvent. But nearly all the assets are long-term loans, and depositors suddenly demand A$30bn. It cannot sell loans fast enough. It runs out of cash and fails, despite being solvent. This is the classic bank run. Liquidity failed first.

Case 2: liquid but insolvent. A bank has plenty of cash this week because it has just borrowed a lot, but it has made huge losses on loans and now owes A$95bn against assets worth A$90bn. It is liquid for the moment, but insolvent, and it will not survive once lenders find out.

Case 3: profitable but short of cash. The shop in section 1.2. A bank can report a large profit for the year and still have a bad liquidity week if its profits are tied up in long loans.

The reverse can happen too: a bank can make a loss in a year (a poor profit result) and still hold plenty of capital and liquidity.

From the controller: Liquidity Reporting looks at liquidity, not at profit or capital. But the three are linked: a loss reduces capital, a fall in capital can scare depositors, and scared depositors create a liquidity problem. So when someone says "the bank is fine", always ask: "fine in which sense?" See Guide Ch 1, "Four different things: liquidity, solvency, capital and profitability".

9. What "stress" means

In normal times, a bank's cash flows are predictable. Stress means an unusual, adverse situation in which many things go wrong at once. APS 210 asks the bank to hold enough liquidity to survive a defined stress.

Stress can come from:

  • Inside the bank (idiosyncratic stress): a scandal, a big loss, a credit rating downgrade. Depositors lose confidence in this bank specifically.
  • The whole market (market-wide stress): a financial crisis in which funding dries up for every bank, and markets for bonds seize up.
  • Both together: the worst case.

APS 210 requires the bank's framework to cover stress events "including those involving the loss or impairment of both unsecured and secured funding sources" and notes that the source "could be specific to the ADI or market-wide or a combination of the two" (APS 210 para 26).

In a severe stress scenario, the LCR assumes, for the next 30 days:

  • some depositors withdraw (different groups at different percentages);
  • wholesale investors do not roll over (do not renew) their lending;
  • customers draw down credit lines the bank has promised;
  • the bank must hand over more collateral on derivatives if its credit rating falls;
  • but some money still comes in from loan repayments, although less than usual.

The regulator sets the percentages. That is the key point: you do not choose them. They are written into APS 210 (Att A Table 3 for outflows and Att A paras 54 to 66 for inflows).

10. Why regulators set rules

Why can't each bank just decide how much liquidity to hold? Three reasons.

  1. The cost of failure falls on others. If a bank fails, depositors, other banks and the government (taxpayers) can bear the cost. The bank's owners may take risks that look good for them (more loans, more profit) because they do not bear the full cost of a failure. Rules make the bank hold more safety than it might choose to alone.
  2. Banks are linked. Banks lend to each other and hold each other's bonds. One failure can spread (contagion).
  3. Confidence is fragile. Customers cannot easily judge a bank's safety. A clear, tough, public rulebook reassures them.

After the 2007 to 2009 global financial crisis, the international Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (the club of bank regulators, based in Basel, Switzerland) designed two measures of liquidity, the LCR and the NSFR. APRA adopted them for Australia in APS 210. (Guide Ch 2, "Basel Committee, Basel III and how APRA adapts it".)

APRA acts under the Banking Act 1959; APS 210 is made under section 11AF of that Act (APS 210 para 1). APRA can also require a bank to hold more than the minimum if it has concerns about the bank's liquidity profile or risk management: a higher LCR (APS 210 para 56) or a higher NSFR (APS 210 para 61).

11. The three ideas of APS 210 on one page

APS 210 is a long document, but it rests on three ideas.

Idea 1: A 30-day survival buffer (the LCR)

If the worst were to happen for the next 30 days, would we survive on the liquid assets we hold today, without outside help?

The liquidity coverage ratio (LCR) compares the bank's stock of high-quality liquid assets with the cash it would be likely to lose, net, over the next 30 calendar days in a severe stress.

LCR = Stock of HQLA ÷ Total net cash outflows over the next 30 calendar days

(Att A para 3)

A locally incorporated ADI must keep its LCR at 100% or more, in Australian dollars and in all currencies together (APS 210 para 55). 100% means: liquid assets at least equal to the expected 30-day net loss. A foreign ADI (a branch of an overseas bank) has a lower minimum of 40% on an all-currencies basis (APS 210 para 55). Example Bank is locally incorporated, so 100% applies to it.

Idea 2: Stable long-term funding (the NSFR)

Is the bank funding its long-term lending with long-term, stable money, rather than money that might disappear?

The net stable funding ratio (NSFR) compares the stable funding the bank has (available stable funding, ASF) with the stable funding its assets and commitments need (required stable funding, RSF), over a one-year horizon.

NSFR = Available stable funding ÷ Required stable funding

(APS 210 Att C para 3)

A locally incorporated LCR ADI must keep its NSFR at 100% or more at all times (APS 210 paras 59 to 60). If a bank funds 30-year mortgages with money that can leave overnight, its NSFR will be low.

Idea 3: Good management

The numbers are not enough. APS 210 requires the bank to run sound liquidity risk management: a framework approved by the Board, limits, early warning indicators, a contingency funding plan (a ready-made plan for a crisis), stress testing, a three-year funding strategy, and an independent oversight function (APS 210 paras 11 to 51; APS 210 paras 62 to 66). The key principles say that an ADI is "responsible for the sound management of its liquidity risk" (APS 210 para 11), must fund itself with stable sources (APS 210 para 13), and must tell APRA as soon as possible of any concern (APS 210 para 14).

APS 210 also has a third, simpler measure called minimum liquidity holdings (MLH), which is 9% of liabilities held in specified liquid assets (APS 210 para 57; Attachment B). APRA classifies each locally incorporated ADI as either an LCR ADI or an MLH ADI (APS 210 para 52). APRA decides which ADIs are MLH ADIs; in practice they are smaller, less complex ones. Confirm with your controller.

To find a percentage of an amount, multiply the amount by the decimal.

Example: Find 5% of A$25.0bn.

Step 1: Turn 5% into a decimal: 5 ÷ 100 = 0.05. Step 2: Multiply: 25.0 × 0.05. Step 3: Work it out: 25.0 × 5 = 125, and then divide by 100 to get 1.25. Answer: A$1.25bn.

12.3 Factors, rates and weighted amounts

The standard uses several names for "the percentage you multiply by". They all mean the same operation.

A weighted amount is a balance multiplied by its rate or factor. "Weighting" simply means that some items count fully and others only in part.

12.4 Reading a number like A$5.0bn

"A$" means Australian dollars. "bn" means billion, which here is a thousand million: 1,000,000,000. So:

A$5.0bn = A$5,000,000,000 = A$5,000 million (A$5,000m)

Another way: 1 billion is 1,000 millions. So A$1.25bn is 1.25 × 1,000 = A$1,250 million. A$0.02bn is 0.02 × 1,000 = A$20 million.

A useful habit: whenever you see a figure, say its unit, then read it. "A$5.0 billion." Not "five". If a colleague says "the surplus is 5.45", ask "5.45 what?" (here: A$5.45bn, which is A$5,450 million).

Decimals: A$0.30bn is 30 hundredths of a billion, or A$300 million. Remember that 0.3 and 0.30 are the same number.

12.5 A ratio compares two amounts by dividing

A ratio is one amount divided by another. In the LCR:

LCR = HQLA ÷ net cash outflows

Take 17.0 ÷ 11.55. Do the division in steps (or use a calculator and then check it with these steps).

  • How many times does 11.55 go into 17.0? Once, with 17.0 − 11.55 = 5.45 left over. So the answer is a bit over 1.
  • Try 1.4: 11.55 × 1.4 = 16.17. Remainder 17.0 − 16.17 = 0.83.
  • 0.83 ÷ 11.55 is about 0.0719. So the answer is about 1.4 + 0.0719 = 1.4719.
  • Turn into a percentage: 1.4719 × 100 = 147.19%, which rounds to 147.2%.

Reading the result: a ratio of 1.0 (or 100%) means the two amounts are equal. Above 100% means the top number is larger. Here, HQLA is 147.2% of net outflows: for every A$1.00 that may leave, the bank holds A$1.47 of liquid assets.

Watch out: A ratio can fall for two different reasons: the top falls, or the bottom rises. When you explain a movement (documents 08 and 09), always say which.

12.6 Minimum, maximum and caps

Two words from the standard that you will use often:

  • A cap is an upper limit. "Inflows are capped at 75% of outflows" (Att A para 30) means that, however large the inflows are, you may count no more than 75% of the outflows.
  • "Min" and "max" are the smaller and the larger of two numbers. Min {a; b} means "take whichever is smaller". Max {a; b} means "take whichever is larger".

The net outflow formula is:

Total net cash outflows = Total expected cash outflows
                          − Min { total expected cash inflows ; 75% of total expected cash outflows }

(Att A para 30)

Example: A bank has outflows of 100 and inflows of 90. The cap is 75% of 100 = 75. The smaller of 90 and 75 is 75. Net outflows are 100 − 75 = 25. The inflows above 75 are ignored.

If instead the inflows were 40, the smaller of 40 and 75 is 40. Net outflows would be 100 − 40 = 60. The cap would not bite.

The purpose of the cap is that a bank cannot rely wholly on inflows. At least 25% of the outflows must always be covered by liquid assets.

There is a second kind of cap on the numerator: lower-quality HQLA (HQLA2) may be no more than 40% of the total stock, and HQLA2B no more than 15% (Att A para 6). Example Bank holds only HQLA1, so the caps do not matter for its numbers, but they matter for the job. Document 08 shows how they are calculated.

12.7 Rounding

We show ratios to one decimal place: 147.2%, 136.3%. We show amounts to two decimal places in A$bn in working papers, because the individual lines are small (A$0.30bn). Round only at the end. If you round each line and then add, the answer can drift by a small amount. Keep the full decimals through the calculation, then round the final ratio.

From the controller: When I check your work, I look first at whether your lines add to your total, and whether your ratio recomputes from your totals. Show every line; do not type only the totals.

13. The 30-day window and the one-year horizon

Two clocks run through APS 210. Do not confuse them.

13.1 The 30-day window (LCR)

The LCR looks at the next 30 calendar days: "30 calendar day period under a severe stress scenario" (APS 210 para 55; Att A para 1). A calendar day is any day, including weekends and holidays, so the window is 30 days on a calendar, not 30 business days.

What goes in the window?

  • Outflows: money the bank might have to pay in the next 30 days, using the stress assumptions. A deposit is "at risk" in the window if the customer could ask for it within those 30 days. A wholesale bond due on day 25 is in the window. A bond due on day 45 is not.
  • Inflows: money the bank is contractually due to receive within the 30 days (for example loan repayments).
  • Assets: HQLA are those the bank holds on day one, "irrespective of their residual maturity" (Att A para 6). It does not matter when the government bond matures. What matters is that it is liquid today.

A rule of thumb for the 30-day window:

Item Inside the window? Counts toward
A customer account the customer can access at any time Yes Outflow, at the run-off rate
A wholesale bond maturing in 12 days Yes Outflow at 100%
A wholesale bond maturing in 45 days No Not in the LCR (but in the NSFR)
A term deposit with no early-withdrawal right, maturing in 90 days No Not in the LCR (Att A para 41)
A loan repayment due in 10 days Yes Inflow at the inflow rate
A loan repayment due in 60 days No Not in the LCR

The window is rolling. Tomorrow's window is a day further on. A bond that is outside today's window may be inside tomorrow's, or next week's. This is why we project forward, and why a surprise (see document 12) can happen: a large item rolls into the window and the ratio drops.

13.2 The one-year horizon (NSFR)

The NSFR looks at stability over one year (Att C para 1). Its question is not "what could leave within a month?" but "how much of this funding will still be here in a year?" and "how much of these assets will still need funding in a year?"

It uses a few time buckets, and residual maturity (the time left until an item falls due) puts each item in one:

  • under 6 months
  • 6 months to under 1 year
  • 1 year or more

Rules for the clock are different for liabilities and assets. For liabilities, the maturity date is "the earliest date at which the funds may be redeemed" (Att C para 8). For assets it is "the latest possible date at which the asset may mature" (Att C para 20). In both cases the standard is deliberately cautious: it assumes the less favourable date.

Check yourself

Try each question before reading the answer.

1. In a bank's books, is a customer's deposit an asset or a liability? Why?

Answer: A liability. The deposit is money the bank owes to the customer, who can ask for it back. For the customer it is an asset; for the bank it is a liability.

2. A bank has assets of A$250bn and liabilities of A$232bn. What is its equity? Is it solvent?

Answer: Equity = assets − liabilities = 250 − 232 = A$18bn. Equity is positive, so the bank owns more than it owes and is solvent. (The accounting equation is assets = liabilities + equity: 232 + 18 = 250.)

3. A bank has A$40bn of at-call deposits and A$9bn of cash and government bonds. In a sudden panic, 12% of the at-call deposits are withdrawn. How much leaves, and can the quick assets cover it?

Answer: 12% of 40 = 40 × 0.12 = A$4.8bn leaves. Quick assets are A$9bn, which is more than 4.8, so yes, with 9 − 4.8 = A$4.2bn to spare. (If 25% left, 40 × 0.25 = 10, which is more than 9, and the bank would be A$1bn short.)

4. Explain the difference between liquidity and solvency in two sentences.

Answer: Liquidity is having enough cash, or assets that become cash quickly, to pay what is due in the near term. Solvency is owning more than you owe overall. A bank can be solvent but illiquid (long loans, nervous depositors), or liquid for a time but insolvent.

5. A bank has HQLA of A$9bn, expected outflows of A$12bn and expected inflows of A$4bn. Calculate the LCR.

Answer: Cap on inflows = 75% × 12 = 9. The smaller of 4 and 9 is 4, so the cap does not bind. Net outflows = 12 − 4 = 8. LCR = 9 ÷ 8 = 1.125 = 112.5%. This is above 100%.

6. A bank has outflows of A$20bn and inflows of A$18bn. HQLA is A$7bn. Calculate the LCR, and say what the cap does.

Answer: Cap = 75% × 20 = 15. The smaller of 18 and 15 is 15, so the cap binds. Net outflows = 20 − 15 = 5. LCR = 7 ÷ 5 = 1.40 = 140%. Without the cap, net outflows would be 20 − 18 = 2 and the LCR would look like 350%, which overstates the position. The cap ensures that at least 25% of outflows (here 5) must be covered by HQLA.

7. A bank has A$30bn of funding with an ASF factor of 90%, A$10bn with 50% and A$5bn with 0%. Its RSF is A$34bn. Calculate its ASF and NSFR. Does it meet the minimum?

Answer: ASF = 30 × 0.90 + 10 × 0.50 + 5 × 0.00 = 27 + 5 + 0 = A$32bn. NSFR = 32 ÷ 34 = 0.941 = 94.1%. This is below 100%, so the bank does not meet the minimum (APS 210 para 60). To fix it, the bank would need to raise at least 34 − 32 = A$2bn more of weighted stable funding (for example, by issuing long-term debt with a 100% factor, so about A$2bn of new long-term funding, if its assets do not change).

8. In Example Bank, a new A$1.0bn of stable retail deposits arrives and is held as RBA balances. Recalculate the LCR. (Other items unchanged; the 5% stable run-off rate applies.)

Answer: HQLA rises by 1.0: 17.0 + 1.0 = 18.0. Extra outflow = 1.0 × 0.05 = 0.05, so outflows = 13.45 + 0.05 = 13.50. Cap = 0.75 × 13.50 = 10.125, higher than inflows of 1.90, so it does not bind. Net outflows = 13.50 − 1.90 = 11.60. LCR = 18.0 ÷ 11.60 = 1.5517 = 155.2%. The ratio rises because the HQLA grows by 1.0 but the outflow grows by only 0.05.

9. Why does the LCR look at 30 days and the NSFR at one year?

Answer: The LCR is about surviving an immediate severe shock without outside help, so it uses a short window of 30 calendar days (Att A para 1). The NSFR is about the structure of funding, whether stable funding supports long assets, so it looks over a year (Att C para 1).

10. What are the minimum levels of the LCR and NSFR for a locally incorporated LCR ADI, and where is each stated?

Answer: The LCR must be at least 100% on both an Australian dollar and an all-currencies basis (APS 210 para 55). The NSFR must be at least 100% at all times (APS 210 para 60).

What to remember

  • A deposit is money the bank owes (a liability); a loan is money owed to the bank (an asset). Assets always equal liabilities plus equity.
  • A bank borrows short and lends long. It cannot pay everyone at once, so it must hold a buffer of liquid assets.
  • Liquidity (cash at the right time), solvency (owning more than owing), capital (the loss-absorbing cushion) and profit (earnings in a period) are four different things.
  • The LCR is HQLA divided by 30-day net cash outflows, minimum 100% (APS 210 para 55). The NSFR is available stable funding divided by required stable funding over one year, minimum 100% (APS 210 para 60).
  • Weighted amount = balance × rate or factor. Inflows are capped at 75% of outflows (Att A para 30).
  • Who the money comes from matters more than how much: A$4.0bn from financial institutions produces four times the LCR outflow of A$25.0bn of stable retail deposits.
  • Example Bank: HQLA 17.0, outflows 13.45, inflows 1.90, net 11.55, LCR 147.2%; ASF 79.75, RSF 58.50, NSFR 136.3%.
  • Show every line, round only at the end, and always say the unit (A$bn).
  • Rates and factors come from APS 210. Internal limits, triggers and timings are the bank's own choice; confirm them with your controller.
