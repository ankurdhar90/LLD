14 Exercises and assessments

Purpose: To let you practise everything in this pack, from simple arithmetic to a full month-end LCR and NSFR, and to prove to yourself and to your controller that you can do the job on your own.

You will be able to:

  • Do the arithmetic of ratios, percentages and weighted amounts without a calculator error.
  • Classify a product or customer balance into its LCR category and rate and its NSFR category and factor, and quote the paragraph that says so.
  • Build a complete LCR and NSFR from a raw list of balances, the way you will at month-end.
  • Handle an incident, a movement analysis, a data error, a product change and the two caps (the HQLA2 cap and the inflow cap).
  • Pass the 40-question final assessment and complete the competency sign-off sheet.

Before you start: Work through the documents in this order and do each level straight after its documents. Level 1 after document 02 (Liquidity from zero). Level 2 after documents 04 to 07 (the three APS 210 companions and Data and classification). Level 3 after documents 08 and 09 (Producing the daily LCR; Producing the NSFR). Level 4 after documents 10 to 12 (Monitoring beyond the ratios; Returns to APRA; Controls, incidents and escalation). The final assessment and sign-off come last. Keep document 13 (Job aids and checklists) beside you for the rate cards.

Time: Level 1, 45 minutes. Level 2, 2 hours. Level 3, 3 to 4 hours. Level 4, 3 hours. Final assessment, 2 hours, closed book except for the standard itself. Sign-off sheet: completed over your first 90 days.

How to use this document

  1. Try each question yourself first. Write your answer down before you look at the ## Answers part at the end.
  2. Show your arithmetic. A right answer with no working earns no credit in the final assessment, and a wrong answer with good working shows us exactly where to help you.
  3. Always quote the paragraph. In this job, a number you cannot trace to the standard is a number you cannot defend to the reviewer, the auditor or APRA (the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority, the bank regulator).
  4. If you score below 80% on any level, re-read the documents for that level and redo the questions after a day.

The standard is APS 210 Liquidity (July 2025). Paragraphs are quoted like this: (APS 210 para 55) for the main body, (Att A para 37) for Attachment A (the liquidity coverage ratio), and (Att C para 12) for Attachment C (the net stable funding ratio).

Words you need before you begin

Term Meaning
ADI Authorised deposit-taking institution: a bank, building society or credit union licensed to take deposits. Example Bank is one.
LCR Liquidity coverage ratio: the stock of high-quality liquid assets divided by net cash outflows over the next 30 days. Minimum 100% (APS 210 para 55).
NSFR Net stable funding ratio: available stable funding divided by required stable funding. Minimum 100% (APS 210 para 60).
HQLA High-quality liquid assets: assets that can be turned into cash quickly in a stress. HQLA1 is the best class (Att A para 9).
Run-off rate The share of a liability or commitment assumed to leave or be drawn in the 30-day stress. Outflow = balance multiplied by run-off rate.
Inflow rate The share of a contractual receipt counted as cash coming in. Inflow = amount due multiplied by inflow rate.
ASF and RSF Available stable funding (the weighted funding the bank has) and required stable funding (the weighted funding its assets need). Each balance is multiplied by a factor.
FCS The Financial Claims Scheme: government cover of A$250,000 per account holder per ADI.
RBA Reserve Bank of Australia, the central bank.
LVR Loan-to-valuation ratio: the loan divided by the value of the property.
SME Small and medium enterprise; for the LCR, one with total funding from the ADI under A$2 million (Att A para 46).
A$bn, A$m Australian dollars in billions (1,000,000,000) and millions (1,000,000).

From the controller: The three numbers you will use most are the 100% minimums, the 30-day window and the 75% inflow cap. If you remember nothing else after Level 1, remember those.

Level 1: Basics and arithmetic (after document 02)

Fifteen short questions. No standard needed except where a paragraph is asked for. Take your time. A calculator is allowed, but show the sum.

1.1 A household has A$5,000 of money coming in each month and spends A$4,200. How much is left over? If the household instead had to spend A$5,300, what would happen to its savings?

1.2 When you put A$1,000 in a savings account at a bank, is that deposit an asset or a liability of the bank? Say why in one sentence.

1.3 A bank lends A$300,000 to a home buyer. Is that loan an asset or a liability of the bank? Say why in one sentence.

1.4 A bank has assets of A$100 billion and liabilities of A$92 billion. What is its equity (the owners' money)? Which side of the balance sheet does equity sit on?

1.5 In two or three sentences, explain why a bank cannot pay all its depositors at once, even if it is perfectly sound. Use the words "borrows short" and "lends long".

1.6 Which of these is the most liquid and which is the least liquid: (a) notes and coin, (b) a 25-year home loan, (c) a government bond that can be sold in minutes? Put them in order from most to least liquid.

1.7 A bank is solvent but cannot find the cash to pay a deposit that has been withdrawn today. Is this mainly a solvency problem or a liquidity problem? Explain in one sentence.

1.8 Work out: (a) 5% of A$24.0 billion; (b) 10% of A$12.0 billion; (c) 25% of A$3.0 billion; (d) 100% of A$1.5 billion.

1.9 A bank holds A$30 million of liquid assets and expects A$20 million of net cash outflows over the next 30 days. Divide the first by the second and write the answer as a percentage. Is it above or below 100%?

1.10 Example Bank's balance of stable retail deposits is A$25.0 billion and the run-off rate is 5%. Calculate the weighted outflow in A$ billions. (A weighted amount is a balance multiplied by its rate.)

1.11 Write A$5.0bn (A$5.0 billion) (a) in full in dollars and (b) in millions of dollars.

1.12 What are the minimum LCR and the minimum NSFR for a locally incorporated LCR ADI like Example Bank? Give the paragraph for each.

1.13 Which ratio looks at a 30-day window and which looks at a one-year horizon? Which one asks "can we survive a severe month?" and which asks "is our long-term lending funded by long-term money?"

1.14 Example Bank expects A$13.45 billion of cash outflows and A$1.90 billion of cash inflows in the next 30 days. Calculate the net cash outflows.

1.15 Example Bank holds A$17.0 billion of HQLA and has net cash outflows of A$11.55 billion. (a) Calculate the LCR to one decimal place. (b) Is it above the 100% minimum? (c) By how many A$ billions does the HQLA exceed the net outflows (the surplus)?

Case 1: The LCR falls below 100%

Use your Level 3 work. At 2:30 pm on the last business day of the month, Treasury tells you that the A$2.8bn of wholesale debt in item n has been called early by the investors, so it now falls inside the 30-day window. It was not in any forecast. Your task:

  1. Recalculate the LCR and say whether this is a breach, and by how much.
  2. Say who you must tell, how fast and under which paragraphs.
  3. Give one remedy, with its size, and show that it restores the LCR to at least 100%.
  4. Explain what the forward-looking LCR in document 08 would have shown, and when.
  5. List the evidence you would keep.

Case 2: A movement analysis

Yesterday's LCR was 147.2%. Today, two things have happened. (a) Customers have swapped A$2.0bn of financial institution deposits (at call) into retail term deposits with more than 30 days to run and no early withdrawal. (b) Separately, in a different scenario, Treasury has lent A$3.0bn of cash (RBA balances) as new standard mortgages (LVR 80% or below, over 1 year).

  1. For (a) alone, recalculate the LCR and explain the movement line by line. Give the effect on the NSFR as well.
  2. For (b) alone, recalculate the LCR and the NSFR.
  3. If both happen on the same day, what are the LCR and NSFR?
  4. Write the two-sentence explanation you would put in the daily commentary for ALCO (the asset and liability committee).

Case 3: A data error found after sign-off

Three days after you signed off the daily LCR, a reviewer finds that A$1.0bn of corporate non-operational at-call deposits (not fully insured) has been tagged operational since a customer-segment table was updated. The reported LCR for each of the three days was calculated on that basis. The base position above is the correct position; the reported position was wrong only because of that tag. The month-end return to APRA has not yet been submitted.

  1. Calculate the reported (wrong) LCR and the correct LCR, and the size of the error.
  2. Does the error change the NSFR? Why or why not?
  3. Set out your steps: immediate action, who to tell, correction, the root cause and the control to add, and what you do about the three days already distributed.
  4. Would your answer change if the return had already been submitted? What would you do?

Case 4: A deposit product redesign

The retail product team wants to change how A$5.0bn of Example Bank's existing less stable at-call retail deposits (part of the A$15.0bn in the base position, currently score 2 because they are on-line accounts) are offered. They ask what each option does to the LCR and the NSFR before they decide.

  • Option A: pay a promotional bonus rate to attract new customers, who would have no other relationship with Example Bank.
  • Option B: move the A$5.0bn into a 12-month fixed-term deposit with no early withdrawal, except for hardship.
  • Option C: the same 12-month fixed term, but customers may break it at any time at only the loss of interest.

For each option, state the new scorecard score or treatment and run-off rate, and calculate the LCR and NSFR (all other base items unchanged). State what you would warn the product team about for Option B.

Case 5: The HQLA2 cap

Example Bank's treasury function reports the following holdings to you at market value. None is involved in secured funding or lending that matures within 30 days.

  • (a) HQLA1: A$60 and HQLA2A: A$100 (market value). Calculate the counted HQLA.
  • (b) HQLA1: A$80, HQLA2A: A$20 (market value), and eligible residential mortgage-backed securities (HQLA2B): A$20 (market value). Calculate the counted HQLA and test both caps.
  • (c) HQLA1: A$80, HQLA2A: A$20 (market value) and eligible residential mortgage-backed securities (HQLA2B): A$60 (market value). Calculate the counted HQLA and say which cap bites.

Note: in the real Example Bank, no Australian-dollar assets currently qualify as HQLA2 (APRA's liquidity FAQ). This case is about the method, which you must still know.

Case 6: The inflow cap

In a test calculation, total cash outflows are 100 and total contractual inflows are 90 (all inflows are valid). HQLA is 40.

  2. Calculate what they would have been without the cap, and say why the standard has the cap.
  3. Explain what the cap means for the practice case in Level 3.

Level 1 answers

1.1 Left over: A$5,000 - A$4,200 = A$800. If spending is A$5,300, then A$5,000 - A$5,300 = -A$300: the household is A$300 short and must use savings or borrow. A bank faces the same arithmetic with much bigger numbers: cash in must cover cash out.

1.2 A liability. The bank owes the money back to you whenever you ask, so it is something the bank owes, not something it owns.

1.3 An asset. The home buyer owes the bank the money (plus interest), so it is something the bank is owed and owns the right to.

1.4 Equity = assets - liabilities = A$100bn - A$92bn = A$8bn. Equity sits on the liabilities and equity side (the right-hand list). The two lists must total the same: assets = liabilities + equity, so 100 = 92 + 8.

1.5 A bank takes deposits that customers can withdraw quickly (it borrows short) and uses the money to make loans such as 25-year mortgages that are repaid slowly (it lends long). Most depositors do not need their money on the same day, so this works in normal times. But if everyone asked for their money at once, the bank could not turn its long loans into cash fast enough, even if every loan is good.

1.6 Most liquid to least liquid: (a) notes and coin, (c) a government bond that can be sold in minutes, (b) a 25-year home loan. Notes and coin are cash already. The bond becomes cash in minutes. The loan can only become cash as the borrower repays over years.

1.7 A liquidity problem. The bank owns more than it owes (it is solvent) but cannot get cash at the moment it needs it. Solvency is about whether assets exceed liabilities in total; liquidity is about having cash on the day it is due.

1.8 (a) 5% x A$24.0bn = 0.05 x 24.0 = A$1.20bn. (b) 10% x A$12.0bn = 0.10 x 12.0 = A$1.20bn. (c) 25% x A$3.0bn = 0.25 x 3.0 = A$0.75bn. (d) 100% x A$1.5bn = A$1.50bn.

Notes on Level 2.

  • Product 11 and "other products". The standard says a deposit is stable if it is fully covered by the FCS and either the depositor has other established relationships that make withdrawal highly unlikely or it is in a transactional account (Att A para 37). It also says on-line accounts are more likely to be withdrawn in a stress (Att A para 39). So an on-line account held by a customer with a real relationship and full FCS cover can still be stable. What counts as an "established relationship" is a judgement the bank documents; confirm Example Bank's definition with your controller. Line b of the practice case avoids the question: there is no relationship and no transactional account, so it cannot be stable.
  • Product 3. The factor depends only on the time left in the encumbrance, (Att C para 23). Under 6 months left: same as unencumbered. 6 months to under 1 year: 50% (or higher if the asset's normal factor is higher). 1 year or more: 100%.
  • Product 4. The Example Bank convention, following APRA's FAQ, is to treat Australian-dollar corporate bonds as non-HQLA. Do not rely on a bond being HQLA2A merely because it meets the criteria in the standard.
  • Product 20a. In the NSFR the derivative position is built from replacement costs, not from the 30-day net cash flow (Att C para 9).

Case 6: The inflow cap

  1. Cap = 75% x 100 = 75. Inflows of 90 exceed 75, so only 75 count. Net cash outflows = 100 - 75 = 25. LCR = 40 / 25 = 160.0%.
  2. Without the cap, net = 100 - 90 = 10 and the LCR would be 40 / 10 = 400.0%. The cap stops the bank relying on inflows to cover its outflows: it must always hold HQLA for at least 25% of outflows. Inflows depend on borrowers paying and cannot all be counted on in a severe stress (Att A para 30). APRA may also limit inflows from one or a few wholesale counterparties (Att A para 54).
  3. In the practice case, outflows are 15.44, so the cap is 75% x 15.44 = 11.58, and the inflows of 2.40 are far below it, so the cap does not bite. It would bite only for a bank with large inflows relative to outflows. Always test it anyway, as the review checklist says.
