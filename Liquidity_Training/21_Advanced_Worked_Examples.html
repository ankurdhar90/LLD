21 Advanced worked examples: currencies, groups, caps and derivatives

Purpose: To take you beyond the single-currency, single-entity Example Bank of documents 08 and 09 and show you, with full arithmetic, the eleven situations where the liquidity rules get tricky: more than one currency, a banking group, caps that bite, derivatives, pledged assets, repos, intermediated deposits, unusual facilities and the old Committed Liquidity Facility.

You will be able to:

  • Build an Australian dollar (AUD) LCR and an all-currencies LCR from one position table, and explain how a foreign exchange swap moves each ratio.
  • Explain why the Level 2 (group) LCR can be lower than the Level 1 (single entity) LCR, and what "trapped liquidity" means.
  • Apply the HQLA caps and the 75% inflow cap, and show when each one actually binds.
  • Compute derivative outflows for the LCR and the derivative charges for the NSFR, step by step.
  • Classify pledged assets, repos, reverse repos, intermediated and superannuation deposits and committed facilities for both ratios, with the right paragraph for each.
  • Read the old Committed Liquidity Facility paragraphs and know why they no longer add anything to a ratio today.

Before you start: Read document 02 (Liquidity from zero), document 05 (APS 210 companion: Attachment A), document 06 (companion: Attachments B and C), document 08 (Producing the daily LCR) and document 09 (Producing the NSFR). You should already be comfortable with run-off rates, inflow rates, ASF and RSF factors. Document 07 (Data and classification) is the place to look when a classification question arises.

Time: About eight hours: one hour per case on average. Do not read all eleven in one sitting. Recompute each case by hand first, then check yourself against the script check21.py that goes with this document.

How to use this document

Each case follows the same pattern: (1) the idea from the very basics, (2) the data, (3) the working, step by step, (4) the result and what it teaches, and (5) what to watch out for. Ground rules for all cases

  • Amounts are in A$ billions (A$bn) unless a case says A$ millions (A$m). A$1.0bn = A$1,000m.
  • "Example Bank" is the fictional bank used throughout this pack: one legal entity, one currency, HQLA 17.0, outflows 13.45, inflows 1.90, LCR 147.2%, ASF 79.75, RSF 58.50, NSFR 136.3%. Several cases start from it and change it. Every other bank, counterparty, host country and exchange rate in this document is invented for training. They are not market data and not rules.
  • Rates are from APS 210 (July 2025) [S1]: Att A Table 3 (outflows), Att A Table 4 (secured lending inflows), Att A paras 61 to 66 (other inflows), Att C paras 11 to 37 (NSFR factors).
  • Percentages are rounded to one decimal place. Amounts are rounded to the figure shown; the script keeps full precision.
  • Where the standard does not tell us exactly how to do something, the working is labelled Interpretation and the assumptions are listed. In real work you would agree these with your controller and record them in the methodology note (document 12 explains why).
  • One fact that matters in several cases: in Australian dollars, only notes and coin, balances with the RBA, and Australian Government and semi-government securities are HQLA1, and no AUD assets currently qualify as HQLA2 [S3]. So any case that holds HQLA2 assets (Cases 3 and 8) describes a bank holding them in a foreign currency or an illustration. Example Bank itself holds none.

A word on two ideas you will need again and again.

Level 1 and Level 2. A bank can be one company, or a parent with subsidiaries. Level 1 means the ADI (authorised deposit-taking institution, a bank) on its own. Level 2 means the whole banking group added together (APS 210 para 3). The LCR must be met on both (Att A para 26).

Netting. If you owe a counterparty 10 and it owes you 6 under one legal agreement that allows the amounts to be set off, you can treat it as owing 4. That is netting. It matters wherever you see derivatives (Cases 1, 5 and 6).

Case 1: A two-currency bank, an AUD LCR and an all-currencies LCR

1.1 The idea from the basics

Money in a bank is held in currencies. A dollar of Australian cash cannot pay a US dollar (USD) bill without first being exchanged, and in a crisis the exchange market may not work. APS 210 therefore does two things.

  1. A locally incorporated ADI must keep an LCR of at least 100% on an AUD basis and on an all-currencies basis (APS 210 para 55). The AUD LCR uses the AUD liquid assets against the AUD cash flows. The all-currencies LCR converts everything to Australian dollars and adds it up.
  2. An ADI active in several currencies must hold liquid assets consistent with where its liquidity needs are by currency, decide how much currency mismatch is acceptable, and analyse each material currency separately, thinking about stress (APS 210 para 43). There is no published minimum for a "USD LCR" in these paragraphs. The USD figure below is an internal coverage ratio that the team calculates the same way, to see whether USD needs are covered by USD assets.

Interpretation: APS 210 para 55 does not spell out how the AUD LCR is assembled. I read it as: AUD-denominated HQLA over net cash outflows from AUD-denominated items. Foreign currency HQLA cannot be counted in the AUD ratio. The inflow cap (75% of outflows, Att A para 30) is applied separately to each basis. Confirm the treatment with your controller before you rely on it.

An FX swap is two trades in one. Today you swap one currency for another (the near leg). On a later date you swap back (the far leg). It is how a bank moves liquidity between currencies without taking currency risk.

1.4 Working: a three-month FX swap moves liquidity

Treasury wants more AUD liquidity. It swaps A$5.0bn of USD (US$3.25bn) for AUD today, and agrees to swap back in three months, which is outside the 30-day window.

  • Near leg today: USD HQLA falls by 5.0 (the Treasuries or the cash are sold or lent), AUD cash at the RBA rises by 5.0.
  • Far leg: due in three months. It is not a cash flow inside 30 days, so it adds no outflow or inflow.
Ratio Before After the 3-month swap

All currencies: HQLA 19.0 (unchanged), net outflows 12.1 157.0% 157.0%

The USD coverage is not a regulatory minimum, but para 43 expects the bank to have thought about it, and this is the kind of mismatch it must "determine an acceptable level" for (APS 210 para 43).

1.5 Working: a 20-day swap does not help

Now suppose the same swap comes back in 20 days. The far leg is inside the 30-day window: the bank pays AUD 5.0 and receives USD (A$5.0bn).

  • AUD LCR: HQLA is 14.0 as before, but there is now an AUD outflow of 5.0 on the far leg (100%, as a derivative payable: Att A para 53). Outflows 7.9 + 5.0 = 12.9; inflows 1.5; net 11.4; AUD LCR = 14.0 / 11.4 = 122.8%. The benefit has gone from 218.8% to 122.8% because the bank has to give the AUD back inside the window.
  • USD: the far leg is an inflow of 5.0 in USD. Raw inflows 1.1 + 5.0 = 6.1, but the cap is 0.75 x 6.8 = 5.1, so only 5.1 counts. Net outflows 6.8 - 5.1 = 1.7; USD coverage = 5.0 / 1.7 = 294.1%. This looks wonderful and is misleading: it is the inflow cap doing its job and the swap simply reversing.
  • All currencies: the two legs of one FX swap involve a full exchange of principal on the same day, so they may be shown as a net figure (Att A footnote 5). Converted to AUD at the same rate they cancel, so the all-currencies LCR stays at 157.0%. Even if you showed them gross (outflow 5.0 and inflow 5.0), the answer is also 157.0% because the inflow cap (14.775) does not bind.

Interpretation: Footnote 5 lets "cash flows arising from FX derivative transactions that involve a full exchange of principal amounts on a simultaneous basis (or within the same day)" be shown net. I apply it to the all-currencies basis, where both legs are converted to AUD and can be netted. On the single-currency AUD basis I show the AUD leg alone, because the USD leg does not exist in the AUD ratio. This is the conservative reading. Confirm it with your controller.

1.6 Result and what it teaches

Position AUD LCR USD coverage All-currencies LCR
Base 140.6% 175.4% 157.0%
After 3-month FX swap 218.8% 87.7% 157.0%
After 20-day FX swap 122.8% 294.1% 157.0%

An FX swap re-allocates liquidity between currencies; it does not create any. A swap that runs beyond 30 days improves the ratio of the currency you receive. A swap that comes back inside 30 days improves nothing in the currency you receive, and the inflow cap can distort the other currency.

Watch out: Always check the far leg date. A "liquidity management" swap that closes on day 29 is a liquidity illusion. Also check that the all-currencies and AUD ratios use the same cut-off date and exchange rate. A rate mismatch between the two ratios is a classic reconciliation break (document 08).

Case 2: A banking group, with an offshore subsidiary and a securitisation vehicle

2.1 The idea from the basics

Example Bank is one company. Now imagine "Group Example", whose parent is Example Bank, with two additions.

  • An offshore banking subsidiary ("Hostland Bank") in a country with its own bank regulator, the "host supervisor", which applies the Basel framework.
  • A securitisation special purpose vehicle (SPV). A securitisation is when a bank bundles loans (here, A$4.0bn of mortgages) and sells them to a separate company, the SPV, which pays for them by issuing notes to investors. The bank is the "originating ADI".

APS 210 says:

  • The LCR is met on both a Level 1 and a Level 2 basis (Att A para 26). The subsidiary's own LCR is calculated using APS 210 rules, with two exceptions: the host supervisor's outflow rates apply to retail and small and medium enterprise (SME) deposits, and alternative liquid assets allowed by the host may be included (Att A para 26).
  • If the host does not apply the Basel framework, APS 210's own rates apply instead (Att A para 27).
  • A material offshore banking subsidiary must keep an LCR of at least 100% (Att A para 28).
  • At Level 2, liquid assets held to meet the subsidiary's own legal entity requirements count, if the related risks (the subsidiary's net outflows) are also in the LCR. A surplus at the subsidiary counts only if it would be freely available to the parent in times of stress (Att A para 24).
  • Restrictions on moving liquid assets across borders must be taken into account. Excess liquidity is not recognised unless there is reasonable certainty it is available (Att A para 29). That unrecognised excess is called trapped liquidity.
  • The SPV: if the ADI is the originating ADI and meets the operational requirements for capital relief under APS 120, it may exclude the SPV's assets and liabilities from its minimum liquidity holdings and NSFR. For the LCR it must include the cash flows of the SPV's assets and liabilities (APS 210 para 5).

2.5 Working: Level 2, naive and correct

Naive (wrong) approach: add everything. HQLA 17.0 + 6.0 = 23.0. Outflows 13.75 + 3.20 = 16.95. Inflows 2.00 + 0.40 = 2.40 (cap 12.7125; not binding). Net = 14.55. LCR = 23.0 / 14.55 = 158.1%. This assumes the 3.20 surplus in Hostland can be moved to the parent.

Correct approach when the surplus is trapped. Suppose Hostland's rules stop it sending liquidity to the parent, so there is no reasonable certainty it is available (Att A para 29). Hostland's HQLA counts only up to its own net outflows (2.80).

Interpretation: Att A para 24 admits subsidiary liquid assets "held to meet legal entity requirements" and excludes a surplus unless it is freely available. I measure the legal entity requirement as the subsidiary's net outflows (a 100% LCR, Att A para 28). If the host sets a higher minimum, use that figure instead. The 3.20 surplus is then excluded.

Recognised HQLA = 17.0 + 2.80 = 19.80. Outflows and inflows are still all included (the subsidiary's risks stay in). Net 14.55. Level 2 LCR = 19.80 / 14.55 = 136.1%.

2.6 Working: the host rate and the host without Basel

If Hostland's supervisor did not apply Basel (Att A para 27), you would use APS 210's own rates for retail: 12.0 x 5% + 8.0 x 10% = 1.4. Hostland outflows would be 1.4 + 1.2 + 0.4 = 3.0, net 2.6, standalone LCR 230.8%. Under Att A para 26 you use the host rate even though it is higher: the group LCR is more cautious (the host knows its local depositors).

2.7 Working: the SPV in the NSFR

For the NSFR (not the LCR), the originating ADI may exclude the SPV if it meets the APS 120 conditions for capital relief (APS 210 para 5). Suppose the SPV holds A$4.0bn of standard mortgages (RSF 65%: Att C para 35) funded by A$3.7bn of notes with 12 months or more to run (ASF 100%: Att C para 11) and A$0.3bn of shorter notes (ASF 0%: Att C para 15).

  • Excluded (the conditions are met): ASF 79.75 / RSF 58.50 = 136.3%.
  • Wrongly included: ASF 79.75 + 3.70 = 83.45; RSF 58.50 + 2.60 = 61.10; 136.6%.

The difference is 0.3 percentage points here, but a bank with a large securitisation programme would see a bigger difference. The important point is that the LCR includes the SPV and the NSFR excludes it. If the capital relief conditions are not met, the assets and liabilities stay on the bank's balance sheet and the NSFR includes them.

2.8 Result and what it teaches

  • The Level 2 LCR (136.1%) is lower than Level 1 (144.7%) because a subsidiary's surplus liquidity is not available to the group, while its risks are.
  • A bank that satisfies the minimum at Level 1 has not necessarily satisfied it at Level 2, and the reverse. Both must be 100% or more.
  • Host supervisor rates replace APS 210 rates for retail and SME deposits only.
  • The SPV is in the LCR, out of the NSFR (when relief conditions are met).

Watch out: Intragroup balances (a deposit the parent holds at the subsidiary, say) must be eliminated at Level 2. Document 10 (Monitoring beyond the two ratios) covers the Level 1 and Level 2 structure. Also keep the evidence for the "freely available" judgement: legal advice, host regulator correspondence and the date it was last reviewed (typical practice; confirm with your controller).

Case 3: The HQLA caps with all three classes, with and without the unwind

3.1 The idea from the basics

HQLA comes in classes. HQLA1 (cash, central bank balances, top government securities) can make up any share of the stock. HQLA2A (for example certain very highly rated corporate and covered bonds) takes a 15% haircut (Att A para 10). HQLA2B takes a 25% haircut for eligible residential mortgage-backed securities (RMBS) and 50% for other corporate debt and shares (Att A para 12). A haircut is a cut to the market value, because in a crisis you would not get full price.

Even after haircuts, the lower-quality classes are capped (Att A para 6):

  • HQLA2 (A and B together) may be at most 40% of the stock.
  • HQLA2B may be at most 15% of the stock, and sits inside the 40%.

Att A para 7 turns these percentages into amounts. Maximum adjusted HQLA2 is two-thirds of adjusted HQLA1. Maximum adjusted HQLA2B is the lower of 15/85 of (adjusted HQLA1 plus adjusted HQLA2A), or, where the 40% cap is binding, one quarter of adjusted HQLA1.

Why "adjusted"? Without a safeguard, a bank could swap its HQLA2 bonds for government bonds for 29 days and look as if it held only HQLA1. Att A paras 7 and 8 say the caps must be tested after unwinding short-term transactions, that is, treating secured funding (repos), secured lending (reverse repos) and collateral swaps that mature within 30 calendar days as if they had already finished and each side had its assets back. "Short-term" means a maturity up to and including 30 calendar days (Att A para 8).

Typical practice: Because no AUD asset is HQLA2 (see the ground rules), this case imagines a bank holding foreign-currency HQLA2, or is an illustration. It is not Example Bank.

Case 4: The 75% inflow cap binding

4.1 The idea from the basics

The LCR lets a bank count cash it expects to receive in the 30 days, but with a limit. A bank cannot rely entirely on money coming in during a crisis, because borrowers may not repay on time and because the bank must keep lending. So total inflows counted are limited to 75% of total outflows (Att A para 30):

Net outflows = Outflows - Min { Inflows ; 75% x Outflows }

That means net outflows can never be less than 25% of outflows. The cap "binds" when weighted inflows are bigger than 75% of outflows. Example Bank's cap is 0.75 x 13.45 = 10.0875 and its inflows are 1.90, so it is nowhere near binding. A bank with a large book of short-term lending to other banks can be.

4.5 Result and what it teaches

  • Count every inflow properly first, then apply the cap once, at the total level. Do not cap line by line.
  • When the cap binds, extra inflows do not help, and net outflows are simply 25% of outflows. Each extra 1.0 of outflow then adds only 0.25 to net outflows (it also raises the cap by 0.75). In the uncapped zone each extra 1.0 of outflow adds the full 1.0. So the cap also softens new outflows, which is a reason the reviewer must know which zone the bank is in.
  • A reviewer should always look at the "cap binding" flag on the daily LCR. A bank near the cap should be watching its outflows, not its inflows (document 08).

Watch out: APRA may set a lower limit on inflows if it sees an ADI relying on a few wholesale counterparties (Att A para 54). Also do not count an inflow from an asset already in the HQLA stock (Att A para 31).

Case 6: Derivatives in the NSFR

6.1 The idea from the basics

The NSFR asks whether long-term assets are funded by long-term funding over a one-year horizon. Derivatives are awkward here because a contract can be an asset (it is worth money to us today) or a liability (it costs us money today), and the position moves every day. The standard handles this with a few simple rules.

  1. Replacement cost is what it would cost to replace the contract today at market value. Where a contract has a negative value, it is a derivative liability; positive, a derivative asset. Where an eligible bilateral netting agreement exists, the set of contracts is treated as one net replacement cost (Att C para 9 for liabilities, and Att C para 27 for assets).
  2. Variation margin (VM) is the collateral that changes hands daily as values move. VM posted is deducted from derivative liabilities, whatever the asset type (Att C para 10; see also Att C footnote 11). The balance sheet asset for that posted VM is then left out of the RSF (Att C footnote 12). For derivative assets, collateral received does not offset the amount, unless it is cash VM, meets the conditions in APS 110, and is in the same currency as the settlement of the contract (Att C para 28). Initial margin received never offsets and gets a 0% ASF (Att C para 28).
  3. RSF on derivatives. (a) 100% of the net derivative asset: NSFR derivative assets less NSFR derivative liabilities, if positive (Att C para 37; see also Att C footnote 29). (b) A further 20% of the gross derivative liabilities, before deducting any VM posted (Att C para 37; see also Att C footnote 20). (c) 85% of initial margin posted, and of contributions to a central counterparty default fund (Att C para 36).
  4. ASF. If derivative liabilities exceed derivative assets, the excess gets 0% ASF (Att C para 15). So there is a charge on the asset side and never a credit on the liability side.

Case 7: Encumbrance in the NSFR

7.1 The idea from the basics

An asset is encumbered when it is pledged as security, so the bank cannot freely sell it. Encumbered assets include assets backing securities or covered bonds and assets pledged in repos or collateral swaps (Att C footnote 21). For the LCR, an encumbered asset is simply not in the HQLA stock (Att A paras 22 to 23). For the NSFR the question is for how long, because a long-pledged asset cannot be used to meet needs over the year.

Case 9: Intermediated and superannuation deposits through both ratios

9.1 The idea from the basics

Sometimes a customer does not deal with the bank directly. A person puts money into an investment platform or a superannuation (retirement savings) fund, and that intermediary places the money with the bank. Who is the depositor? If the bank treats it as a deposit from the person, it gets the gentler retail run-off rates. If it treats it as a deposit from a financial institution (the intermediary), it gets 100%. APS 210 sets four tests. A deposit placed through an intermediary is a deposit from a financial institution, unless during the next 30 days all of these are true (Att A para 35):

  • (a) the person keeps all legal rights over withdrawal or other movement of the funds;
  • (b) the person actually exercises those rights and cannot transfer them to the intermediary;
  • (c) the person is clearly and prominently told the funds will be placed with the ADI; and
  • (d) the intermediary or an associated entity cannot make investment decisions about the deposit for the person, or withdraw funds without the person's specific direction (apart from fees, expenses and taxes).

If all four are met, the ADI may treat the funds as if they are from the person, and must be able to demonstrate how (Att A para 35). Trustees of superannuation funds are named as financial institutions in the definition (APS 210 para 10).

For the NSFR, the person-level treatment also needs the intermediary to be unable to replace the ADI unless it gives enough notice:

9.4 Result and what it teaches

  • Each of the four conditions in Att A para 35 is a yes or no that the bank must be able to prove. If one fails, the whole deposit is a financial institution deposit.
  • The same deposit can be 5% in the LCR and 95% in the NSFR (T1), or 100% and 0% (T3).
  • Notice period matters only to the NSFR. Check the contract, not the product name.

I have not used it above. Confirm the treatment with your controller before booking such a product.

Watch out: Document 14's practice case treats at-call superannuation fund money as a financial institution deposit (100% and 0%). That is T3 above. Only evidence of all four tests moves a balance out of that category.

Case 10: A committed facility portfolio, including hedge fund and special purpose vehicle facilities

10.1 The idea from the basics

A committed facility is a promise to lend up to a limit. The undrawn part is the room the customer has not yet used. In a crisis customers draw on it, and the bank must pay out. The LCR therefore applies a rate to each undrawn amount (Att A Table 3), and the rate depends on who the customer is and on whether it is a credit facility (to fund the customer's business) or a liquidity facility.

A liquidity facility is a committed, undrawn back-up facility used to refinance a customer's debt when the customer cannot roll it over in the market. Only the amount of the customer's outstanding debt maturing within 30 days that the facility backs is a liquidity facility. Any extra capacity is a credit facility. General working capital facilities for corporates, such as a revolving credit facility, must not be classed as liquidity facilities (Att A para 51).

There is one trap. Any facility to a hedge fund, money market fund, special purpose funding vehicle or other vehicle used to finance the ADI's own assets must be captured in its entirety as a liquidity facility to a financial institution (Att A para 52), at 100%.

Rates (Att A Table 3), for the undrawn portion: retail and SME 5%; non-financial corporates, sovereigns, central banks and PSEs: credit 10%, liquidity 30%; banks and other prudentially supervised ADIs 40%; other financial institutions: credit 40%, liquidity 100%; other legal entity customers 100%. Revocable credit and liquidity facilities: 5%.

NSFR: irrevocable and conditionally revocable facilities 5% RSF; unconditionally revocable facilities 1% (Att C Table 1).

  • NSFR: base facility RSF is 16.0 x 5% = 0.80 (document 09). Replace with 0.96: RSF = 58.50 - 0.80 + 0.96 = 58.66. NSFR = 79.75 / 58.66 = 136.0%.

10.5 The cost of getting para 52 wrong

If the three para 52 facilities (rows 8, 9 and 10, total 2.0) were treated as ordinary corporate credit facilities at 10%, their outflow would be 0.20, not 2.00. Total outflows from facilities would fall to 2.53, outflows to 14.68 and net outflows to 12.78. The LCR would read 133.0% instead of 116.6%, an overstatement of 16.4 percentage points, with an understated outflow of 1.8.

10.6 Result and what it teaches

  • Classify by customer type first, then by credit or liquidity.
  • A single facility can be part liquidity and part credit (row 4).
  • Facilities to hedge funds, money market funds and funding vehicles carry 100% in full, whatever the contract calls them.
  • The NSFR treats all irrevocable facilities alike at 5%.

Watch out: Customers move categories. A corporate that becomes a financial institution (say after a restructure) needs a new classification, and your static data must carry a customer type that is kept current (document 07). Annual review of facility classification belongs in your control matrix (document 12).

Case 11: The historical Committed Liquidity Facility (illustration only)

11.1 The idea from the basics

Before 2023 there were not enough Australian government securities for banks to meet the LCR with HQLA alone. The Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) therefore offered a Committed Liquidity Facility (CLF): for a fee, it promised to lend (against collateral) up to an agreed amount in a crisis, and APRA let that promised amount count towards the LCR (Att A para 13). The aggregate CLF was phased out to zero as at 1 January 2023 [S3]. The paragraphs remain in the standard (Att A paras 13 to 25), and you will meet them in older files, in the NSFR (Att C para 32) and in the CLF-eligible rows of the secured-funding and secured-lending tables. Today a bank's CLF is zero, so nothing in this case adds to a current ratio. This is a worked illustration of the mechanics.

The rules you need (Att A):

  • Only for AUD shortfalls: the CLF "must only be used by an ADI to manage any shortfall in Australian dollars" (Att A para 14).
  • An ADI had to apply to APRA before establishing the CLF, show it in its funding strategy, try balance sheet management first, reapply each year and could ask for a change if circumstances changed unforeseeably (Att A paras 17 to 20). Senior management had to attest to the use of drawn proceeds, other than intraday or the funding of end-of-day balances at the RBA, and APRA had to be told of such a drawdown (Att A para 21).
  • The numerator of the LCR was HQLA after haircuts plus the lowest of three amounts (Att A para 15):
  • (a) the contractual amount of the CLF, approved by APRA for LCR purposes;
  • (b) that amount, less the market value (after RBA margins) of eligible CLF securities pledged in maturing secured funding with a zero per cent run-off, plus the market value (after RBA margins) of eligible CLF securities received in maturing secured lending with a zero per cent inflow, where the collateral is not used to cover short positions; or
  • (c) the market value, after RBA margins, of the assets the ADI holds as CLF collateral.

HQLA after haircuts: 12.0. Net cash outflows over 30 days: 20.0. So before the CLF the LCR is 12.0 / 20.0 = 60.0%, a shortfall of 8.0.

11.4 Result and what it teaches

  • Each of the three tests can be the binding one. Scenario 2 breaches 100% only because the bank held too little eligible collateral, even though APRA had approved 9.0.
  • The CLF counts only up to the collateral the bank really has available after pledges (test (b)) and after RBA margins (test (c)).
  • The CLF assets also had to meet the HQLA operational requirements and be well diversified (Att A para 16).
  • Today the same bank would show 12.0 / 20.0 = 60.0%. The CLF was a way of counting an RBA promise, and with the promise gone, the bank must hold real HQLA.
  • A related NSFR point: CLF-eligible third-party securities could attract RSF of 10% up to the lower of carrying value and the CLF amount in the numerator (Att C para 32). With a CLF of zero that limit is zero.

Watch out: Do not "revive" the CLF in a calculation by following an old template. Document 12 and the control matrix should show the CLF row set to zero with a note, and an unexpected non-zero value there is a data error.

Check yourself

  1. A bank has AUD HQLA of 10.0 and AUD net cash outflows of 12.0. Its all-currencies LCR is 120%. Does it meet APS 210 para 55? Answer: No. The AUD LCR is 10.0 / 12.0 = 83.3%, below 100%. Both the AUD and all-currencies ratios must be at least 100% (APS 210 para 55).

  2. HQLA1 is 30. HQLA2A has a market value of 50. Ignoring any secured transactions, what HQLA is counted? Answer: HQLA2A after the 15% haircut = 42.5. The cap on HQLA2 is two-thirds x 30 = 20. Counted stock = 30 + 20 = 50 (Att A paras 7 to 10). The excess 22.5 is dropped.

  3. Outflows 40, weighted inflows 36, HQLA 15. What is the LCR? Answer: Cap = 75% x 40 = 30. Counted inflows = 30. Net = 40 - 30 = 10. LCR = 15 / 10 = 150% (Att A para 30). Without the cap it would have been 15 / 4 = 375%.

  4. A bank swaps A$5.0bn of USD for AUD with the far leg in 20 days. Does the AUD LCR improve? Answer: No. The AUD HQLA rises by 5.0 but the far leg is an AUD outflow of 5.0 inside 30 days (Att A Table 3). In Case 1 the AUD ratio is 218.8% after a three-month swap but only 122.8% after a 20-day swap.

  5. Counterparty pays 70, receives 50 under a valid master netting agreement. Another counterparty has no netting agreement and the same flows (not FX). What are the LCR derivative flows? Answer: First: net outflow of 20. Second: an outflow of 70 and an inflow of 50 (Att A footnote 5).

  6. A bank's daily net collateral flows give a largest 30-day window of +120 posted and a smallest of -200 received over 24 months. What goes into the LCR? Answer: 200. The largest absolute value counts, at 100% (Att A Table 3).

  7. A bank has gross derivative liabilities (negative replacement costs) of 300 before variation margin, and posted VM of 100. What is the 20% charge in the NSFR? Answer: 20% x 300 = 60. The charge is on the amount before deducting VM (Att C para 37).

  8. A corporate bond with a one-year-plus maturity (unencumbered RSF 85%), A$2bn, is encumbered with eight months remaining. What is its RSF? Answer: It is in the six to under twelve month band. Its unencumbered factor (85%) is above 50%, so the higher factor applies: 2 x 85% = 1.70 (Att C para 23). A$2bn of HQLA2A in the same position would be 2 x 50% = 1.00.

  9. A hedge fund has an undrawn committed facility of A$0.4bn. What are the LCR outflow and NSFR RSF? Answer: LCR: the whole amount as a liquidity facility to a financial institution at 100% = 0.40 (Att A para 52). NSFR: 5% x 0.4 = 0.02 (Att C Table 1).

  10. (Historical.) A bank's approved CLF is 5.0; it pledged 1.0 and received 0.2 of CLF securities in maturing zero per cent deals; it holds CLF collateral of 6.0 after margins. How much does the CLF add to the numerator? Answer: (a) 5.0; (b) 5.0 - 1.0 + 0.2 = 4.2; (c) 6.0. The lowest is 4.2 (Att A para 15).

What to remember

  • The AUD LCR and the all-currencies LCR are two separate tests (APS 210 para 55). An FX swap moves liquidity between them but creates none. Always check the far leg date.
  • Level 2 includes the subsidiary's risks in full but its surplus liquidity only if it is freely available (Att A paras 24 to 29). Host supervisors set retail and SME run-off rates for offshore subsidiaries (Att A para 26).
  • The SPV is in the LCR and, if capital relief conditions are met, out of the NSFR (APS 210 para 5).
  • Caps are tested on adjusted amounts after unwinding secured transactions of 30 days or less (Att A paras 6 to 8). The standard does not give a formula: label your method, state your assumptions, and document it.
  • Net outflows can never be less than 25% of outflows (Att A para 30). When the cap binds, extra inflows are worth nothing.
  • For derivatives, net only under a valid master netting agreement (or a same-day exchange FX trade), and take the largest absolute 30-day collateral flow from 24 months of history (Att A footnote 6).
  • In the NSFR, the net derivative asset costs 100%, the gross liabilities 20% (before VM), and initial margin 85% (Att C paras 36 to 37).
  • An encumbered asset is excluded from the LCR at once, but in the NSFR it matters only by the remaining encumbrance period: under six months, no change; six to twelve months, at least 50% (higher where its normal factor is higher); a year or more, 100% (Att C para 23).
  • Collateral class decides the LCR rate for repos and reverse repos; term and counterparty decide the NSFR factor.
  • Intermediated deposits need four proofs (Att A para 35) and, for the better NSFR factors, a long notice period (Att C paras 12 to 14).
  • Facilities to hedge funds, money market funds and funding vehicles are 100% in the LCR in full (Att A para 52), but only 5% in the NSFR.
  • The CLF is zero today. Its mechanics (Att A para 15) are history.
