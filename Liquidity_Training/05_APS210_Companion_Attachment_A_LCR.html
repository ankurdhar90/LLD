05 APS 210 companion: Attachment A (LCR)

Purpose: This is your paragraph-by-paragraph companion to Attachment A of Prudential Standard APS 210 Liquidity (July 2025, F2025L00653), the part of the standard that defines the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR). Open the standard at any paragraph of Attachment A, find the matching entry here, and learn what it says, what it means in plain words, what the Liquidity Reporting team must do about it, what evidence we keep, and where to read more.

You will be able to:

  • Find the training entry for any Attachment A paragraph (1 to 66), table (1 to 4) or footnote (2 to 8) in under a minute.
  • Say in plain words what each paragraph requires and apply every rate, haircut and cap with a small calculation.
  • Tell which paragraphs create work for the Liquidity Reporting team and which belong to Treasury, Liquidity Risk or the Board.
  • Name the evidence we keep to show each rule is met.
  • Find the rate for any liability, facility, asset or inflow on the one-page summary at the end.

The Example Bank

All examples use Example Bank, the fictional bank you have joined. It is a locally incorporated Australian LCR ADI. "ADI" means authorised deposit-taking institution: a bank, building society or credit union that APRA (the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority) has authorised to take deposits. An "LCR ADI" is one that must meet the LCR rather than the simpler minimum liquidity holdings regime. Example Bank has one legal entity and one currency, so its Level 1 position (the bank on its own) equals its Level 2 position (the bank with its subsidiaries) and its AUD (Australian dollar) LCR equals its all-currencies LCR. Figures are in A$ billions (A$bn). Its LCR is:

LCR = Stock of HQLA / Total net cash outflows over the next 30 calendar days
    = 17.00 / 11.55
    = 147.2%

Where this companion says "Example Bank convention" or "typical practice; confirm with your controller", the point comes from the bank's own choices and not from APS 210.

Objective, components and formula

Att A para 1: The objective of the LCR

What it says: The objective of the LCR is to make sure an ADI keeps an adequate level of unencumbered high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) that can be converted into cash to meet its liquidity needs for 30 calendar days under a severe liquidity stress scenario (Att A para 1).

In everyday words: Imagine a bad month in which customers and lenders pull money out and almost nobody lends to you. The LCR asks: do you hold enough assets that you can sell or borrow against in a hurry to pay everything that falls due in those 30 days? "Unencumbered" means the assets are not already promised to someone else. A government bond you have lent out as security cannot also be sold to pay depositors.

Example: If you were self-employed and had A$10,000 of bills due next month, you would want at least A$10,000 in your bank account or in things you could sell at once at full price. A house is valuable but is not that kind of asset.

What you do about it: No separate calculation. This is the purpose against which every other paragraph is read. When a classification question is unclear, ask what the objective implies: would this asset really be available as cash, and would this outflow really happen in a stressed 30 days? Where two readings are possible, take the more conservative one and record why (Example Bank convention). Liquidity Reporting produces the ratio that shows whether the objective is met each day.

Evidence we keep: The daily LCR working paper, the signed-off ratio, and the methodology document that ties each of our classification rules to a paragraph of Attachment A.

Learn more: Document 02 (Liquidity from zero); document 08 (Producing the daily LCR, end to end); Guide Ch 5 s1.

Att A para 2: The two components

What it says: The LCR has two components: (a) the value of the stock of HQLA; and (b) total net cash outflows, calculated according to the scenario parameters set out in Attachment A (Att A para 2).

In everyday words: There is a top line (the liquid assets you hold) and a bottom line (the cash you expect to lose net over 30 days). Everything else in Attachment A is detail about how to measure one of those two numbers.

What you do about it: Organise the daily working paper in two blocks: a numerator block (Att A paras 6 to 25, the HQLA stock) and a denominator block (Att A paras 30 to 66, outflows and inflows). Treasury owns the liquid asset portfolio; Liquidity Reporting classifies and values both blocks. The "scenario parameters" are the fixed run-off and inflow rates in Tables 2, 3 and 4. You do not choose them, except where Table 3 says "to be set by the ADI".

Evidence we keep: The working paper layout showing the two blocks, with every line referenced to a paragraph or table.

Learn more: Document 08; Guide Ch 5 s1.4.

Att A para 3: The LCR formula

What it says: The LCR is calculated as the stock of HQLA divided by total net cash outflows over the next 30 calendar days (Att A para 3).

In everyday words: Liquid assets on top, expected net cash drain underneath. A ratio of 100% means the assets exactly cover the drain; above 100% means a surplus.

LCR = Stock of HQLA / Total net cash outflows over the next 30 calendar days

Example: Example Bank has A$17.00bn of HQLA and A$11.55bn of net outflows. 17.00 / 11.55 = 1.472, which is 147.2%. The surplus is 17.00 - 11.55 = A$5.45bn. If net outflows rose to A$17.00bn the ratio would be exactly 100%.

What you do about it: Compute the ratio every business day to one decimal place of a percentage, from the signed-off numerator and denominator. Show the surplus (HQLA minus net outflows) next to it because the surplus in A$ is easier to explain to Treasury than the percentage. Compute it in the all-currencies basis and, for a bank with foreign currency business, the AUD basis too (APS 210 para 55). For Example Bank the two are equal.

Evidence we keep: The ratio calculation on the daily working paper, the reviewer's initials and the controller's sign-off (Example Bank convention: one person prepares, a second reviews, the controller signs).

Learn more: Document 02; document 08; Guide Ch 5 s1.4.

Att A para 4: The buffer over the minimum

What it says: An ADI must include an appropriate buffer of HQLA over the LCR requirement, in line with its liquidity risk tolerance (Att A para 4).

In everyday words: The minimum is 100%, but a bank that runs at exactly 100% has no room for a bad day. The standard expects each bank to decide how much extra it needs, based on how much liquidity risk its Board is prepared to accept.

Example: At Example Bank the Board's risk appetite is an LCR of at least 120% (Example Bank convention). With net outflows of A$11.55bn, 120% means HQLA of at least 1.20 x 11.55 = A$13.86bn. The actual HQLA of A$17.00bn is A$3.14bn above that.

What you do about it: Show the daily LCR against three lines: the 100% regulatory minimum, the Board appetite (120%) and the management early-warning trigger (amber at 125%) (Example Bank convention). If the ratio falls below a trigger, start the escalation in document 12 (Controls, incidents and escalation). Liquidity Risk (the independent oversight function, APS 210 para 28) checks that the appetite is reasonable. The Board sets it.

Evidence we keep: The Board-approved risk appetite statement, the daily limit-monitoring record, and any escalation emails.

Learn more: Document 03 (Your role, the team and the working year); document 12; Guide Ch 5 s1.5.

Att A para 5: Using the buffer in stress, and telling APRA

What it says: In a period of financial stress an ADI may need to sell part of its HQLA and/or draw on its Committed Liquidity Facility (CLF) with the RBA, using the cash to cover outflows. As a result, the LCR may fall below the minimum in APS 210 para 55. An ADI must inform APRA immediately in the event of an actual breach of its LCR requirement, or if it becomes aware of circumstances that may result in a breach (Att A para 5; APS 210 para 55).

In everyday words: The buffer is there to be used. In a real crisis the ratio is expected to fall because the bank is spending its liquid assets, and that alone is not a failure. But the bank must call APRA straight away when the ratio drops below 100% or when it can see that it is about to. There are two triggers: an actual breach, and awareness of circumstances that may cause a breach. The second is forward looking.

Example: Example Bank's forecast shows that A$2.8bn of wholesale debt will mature inside 30 days and nobody had counted it. Outflows rise to A$18.24bn, net outflows to A$15.84bn and the LCR falls to 94.7%, a shortfall of A$0.84bn. That is an actual breach. If instead the debt were due in 45 days and the forecast showed the LCR crossing 100% in 20 days, that is a circumstance that may result in a breach, and APRA must be told immediately as well.

What you do about it: This is the paragraph that makes a calculation urgent. If the daily LCR is below 100%, or the forward-looking LCR shows it will fall below 100%, tell the controller at once. The controller informs Liquidity Risk, Treasury and the executive who will notify APRA (APS 210 para 14). You do not contact APRA yourself. Part of the job is to make sure the number is right before it goes up, but never to delay the message while checking: report and check at the same time.

Evidence we keep: The time-stamped breach notice, the record of who told APRA and when, the daily LCR that showed the breach, and the follow-up action plan.

Learn more: Document 12 (Controls, incidents and escalation), the worked LCR-below-100% case; document 11 (Returns to APRA and public disclosure), notifications table; Guide Ch 5 s1.6.

Eligible stock of high-quality liquid assets (HQLA)

Att A para 6: Two categories and the caps

What it says: There are two categories of assets that may be included in the stock of HQLA. Assets that may be included in each category are those the ADI holds on the first day of the stress period, irrespective of their residual maturity. The highest-quality liquid assets (HQLA1) may be included without limit. Other high-quality liquid assets (HQLA2), which includes HQLA2A and HQLA2B, may only make up to 40 per cent of the total stock of HQLA. HQLA2B must not exceed 15 per cent of the total stock of HQLA, and HQLA2B must be included within the overall 40 per cent cap on HQLA2 (Att A para 6).

In everyday words: The standard sorts liquid assets into grades. HQLA1 is the best (cash, central bank balances, government bonds). HQLA2A is good (some very high-rated bonds). HQLA2B is acceptable but riskier (certain mortgage-backed bonds, lower-rated corporate bonds, shares). The worse the grade, the lower the share of the buffer it may fill. You count what the bank holds on day one of the 30-day stress, and it does not matter how many years are left before the bond matures. A 10-year bond that you could sell tomorrow is as good as a 2-day one.

Example: Total HQLA after haircuts is A$100. HQLA1 is 60, HQLA2A is 30 and HQLA2B is 10. HQLA2 = 30 + 10 = 40, which is 40% of the stock, so it passes the 40% cap. HQLA2B = 10, which is 10% of the stock, so it passes the 15% cap. Everything counts. If HQLA1 were 50, HQLA2A 30 and HQLA2B 20, then HQLA2 = 50 out of 100 = 50%, which breaches the 40% cap, so only part counts (see Att A para 7 for the exact method).

What you do about it: Tag every security in the liquid asset portfolio with its class: HQLA1, HQLA2A, HQLA2B or not eligible (see document 07, Data: where the numbers come from and how each balance is classified). Apply the caps in Att A para 7 as a step in the daily calculation, after haircuts. For Example Bank, the portfolio is cash and RBA balances (A$5.0bn) plus government and semi-government securities (A$12.0bn), all HQLA1, so the caps do not bite. In Australian dollars, per APRA's liquidity FAQ, only notes and coin, balances with the RBA, and Australian Government and semi-government securities are HQLA1, and no Australian-dollar assets currently qualify as HQLA2. The caps matter when the bank holds foreign-currency assets or if APRA recognises more Australian-dollar assets.

Evidence we keep: The asset-by-asset eligibility schedule with class tags, the cap calculation (even when the caps do not bite, show that they were tested), and the market-value source for each security.

Learn more: Document 07; document 08; Guide Ch 5 s3 and s6.

Att A para 7: How the caps are calculated

What it says: The 40 per cent cap and the 15 per cent cap in Att A para 6 must take into account the effect on the stock of HQLA of the HQLA1 and HQLA2 amounts involved in secured funding, secured lending and collateral swap transactions maturing within 30 calendar days. The maximum amount of adjusted HQLA2 in the stock is two-thirds of the adjusted amount of HQLA1 after haircuts. The maximum amount of adjusted HQLA2B is either 15/85 of the sum of the adjusted amounts of HQLA1 and HQLA2A after haircuts, or, where the 40 per cent cap is binding, up to one quarter of the adjusted amount of HQLA1 after haircuts (Att A para 7).

In everyday words: "40% of the total" is circular, because the total includes the asset you are capping. So the standard gives you formulas based only on the other assets. If HQLA2 may be 40% of the total, HQLA1 must be at least 60%, so HQLA2 may be at most 40/60 = two-thirds of HQLA1. If HQLA2B may be 15% of the total, the rest is 85%, so HQLA2B may be at most 15/85 of the rest. "Adjusted" means: first undo any short-term (30 days or less) repo, reverse repo or collateral swap, so you test the caps on the portfolio the bank will really hold once those deals finish (Att A para 8).

Example 1 (HQLA2A too large): HQLA1 is 60. HQLA2A has a market value of 100, which is 85 after the 15% haircut. The cap on HQLA2 is two-thirds x 60 = 40. Counted HQLA = 60 + 40 = 100. The 45 above the cap (85 - 40) is excluded. Check: 40 / 100 = 40%.

Example 2 (the 15/85 rule): HQLA1 = 70, HQLA2A = 20 after haircut, HQLA2B = 30 after its haircut. HQLA2B cap = 15/85 x (70 + 20) = 15.88. Counted HQLA2B = 15.88. HQLA2 = 20 + 15.88 = 35.88, below the two-thirds cap of 46.67. Stock = 70 + 20 + 15.88 = 105.88. Check: 15.88 / 105.88 = 15.0%.

What you do about it: Build the cap step into the calculation in this order: (1) value each asset at market value; (2) apply the haircuts of Att A paras 10 and 12; (3) unwind the short-term secured transactions (Att A para 8); (4) apply the HQLA2B limit; (5) apply the HQLA2 limit; (6) add up. Then test the result against the percentages in Att A para 6 as a cross-check. The standard does not give a worked formula for combining the two HQLA2B alternatives, so the team's reading (apply the lower of the two limits, then the overall HQLA2 cap) is a methodology choice to confirm with your controller and document.

Evidence we keep: The cap calculation with each step shown, the approved methodology note describing how Att A paras 7 and 8 are read, and the transactions list used for the unwind.

Learn more: Document 08; document 14 (Exercises and assessments), the HQLA2 cap case; Guide Ch 5 s6.3.

Watch out: Do not apply the caps to market values before haircuts. The standard says "after haircuts have been applied".

Att A para 8: The adjusted amounts

What it says: The adjusted amount of HQLA1 is the amount of HQLA1 that would result after unwinding short-term secured funding, secured lending and collateral swap transactions that involve the exchange of any HQLA for any HQLA1, where the assets meet, or would meet if held unencumbered, the operational requirements in Att A paras 22 to 25. The adjusted amount of HQLA2A and of HQLA2B are defined in the same way for exchanges of any HQLA for HQLA2A and HQLA2B respectively. Short-term transactions are those with a maturity date up to and including 30 calendar days (Att A para 8).

In everyday words: To unwind a deal means to treat it as if it had already finished and each side had its assets back. Why do this? Without it a bank could swap HQLA2 bonds for government bonds for 29 days, look as if it held only HQLA1, and slip past the caps, even though the swap reverses on day 29. A deal that matures on day 30 counts as short-term; one that matures on day 31 does not.

Example: A bank holds 50 of HQLA1, of which 10 is cash it borrowed under a 20-day repo, giving HQLA2A bonds as collateral. Unwinding: the cash goes back (adjusted HQLA1 = 40) and the bonds return (adjusted HQLA2A = their value after haircut, say 10). The caps are tested on 40 of HQLA1 and 10 of HQLA2A. The bonds pledged in the repo are still not in the stock today, because they are encumbered (Att A para 23). The unwind only affects the cap test.

What you do about it: Extract every secured funding, secured lending and collateral swap with a maturity date within 30 days (inclusive) from the treasury system, with the class of each leg and the maturity. Run the unwind in the cap calculation. For Example Bank, which holds only HQLA1 and borrows under HQLA1-backed repo, the unwind changes nothing because both legs are HQLA1, but you still record that the test was done.

Evidence we keep: The list of short-term secured transactions used, the adjusted amounts by class, and the methodology note.

Learn more: Document 07; document 08; Guide Ch 5 s6.4.

HQLA1

Att A para 9: HQLA1, the highest-quality liquid assets

What it says: HQLA1 may make up an unlimited share of the stock of eligible HQLA. It is included at market value and is not subject to a haircut under the LCR. HQLA1 is limited to (Att A para 9):

  • (a) notes and coin;
  • (b) central bank balances, to the extent that these balances may be drawn down in times of stress;
  • (c) marketable securities representing claims on, or guaranteed by, sovereigns, central banks, public sector entities (PSEs), the Bank for International Settlements (BIS), the International Monetary Fund (IMF), the European Central Bank (ECB) and the European Union (EU) or multilateral development banks (MDBs), that satisfy all of these conditions: (i) assigned a zero per cent risk-weight under Attachment B of Prudential Standard APS 112 Capital Adequacy: Standardised Approach to Credit Risk (APS 112); (ii) traded in large, deep and active repo or cash markets characterised by a low level of concentration; (iii) a proven record as a reliable source of liquidity in the markets (repo or sale) even in stressed market conditions; and (iv) not an obligation of a financial institution or any of its associated entities;
  • (d) for sovereigns that carry a risk weight above zero: sovereign or central bank debt securities issued in domestic currencies by the sovereign or central bank in the country where the liquidity risk is being taken, or in the ADI's home country; and
  • (e) for sovereigns that carry a risk weight above zero: domestic sovereign or central bank debt securities issued in foreign currencies, eligible only up to the amount of the ADI's stressed net cash outflows in that specific foreign currency arising from its operations in the jurisdiction where the liquidity risk is being taken.

In everyday words: HQLA1 is cash and things that behave like cash even in a panic. The list is closed: if an asset is not on it, it is not HQLA1. "Market value" means what the asset would fetch if sold today, not what you paid or the face value. "No haircut" means you count 100 cents in the dollar. A "risk weight" is the capital rule's measure of how risky an asset is; zero means the regulator treats it as safe from default. Items (d) and (e) cover countries whose government debt is not rated zero risk, but only for debt in that country's own currency (or in foreign currency up to the cash you expect to need in that currency).

Example: Example Bank holds A$5.0bn of cash and RBA balances plus A$12.0bn of Australian Government and semi-government securities. All A$17.0bn is HQLA1 at market value, with no haircut. If the bonds' market value rose to A$12.3bn the stock would be A$17.3bn, because market value is what counts and not the A$12.0bn on the books.

What you do about it: Maintain the HQLA1 eligibility list and apply it to each line. Source the daily market values from the treasury system's price feed, and agree them to the securities ledger. For Australian-dollar assets, per APRA's liquidity FAQ, only notes and coin, balances with the RBA, and Australian Government and semi-government securities are HQLA1. Check that RBA balances "may be drawn down in times of stress": the Exchange Settlement Account balance counts, but any amount the RBA or the bank has set aside for a specific purpose that cannot be drawn does not. Do not count securities issued by a financial institution, even if guaranteed, unless they satisfy every condition in (c). Treasury owns the portfolio; you own the classification.

Evidence we keep: The eligibility schedule by security, price source and date, the RBA Exchange Settlement Account statement agreed to the ledger, and the cash count or custodian confirmation for notes and coin.

Learn more: Document 07 (Data and classification), securities decision tree; document 08; Guide Ch 5 s4.

Watch out: Never include an asset that has been pledged. Even HQLA1 loses its eligibility when it is encumbered (Att A para 23).

HQLA2A

Att A para 10: HQLA2A and its 15 per cent haircut

What it says: An ADI may include the following as HQLA2A where APRA, or the relevant prudential regulator in the jurisdiction where the liquidity risk is taken, has recognised these assets. A 15 per cent haircut is applied to the current market value of each HQLA2A asset in the stock. HQLA2A is limited to (Att A para 10):

  • (a) marketable securities representing claims on, or guaranteed by, sovereigns, central banks, PSEs or MDBs that satisfy all of the following: (i) assigned a 20 per cent risk-weight under Attachment B of APS 112; (ii) traded in large, deep and active repo or cash markets with a low level of concentration; (iii) a proven record as a reliable source of liquidity in the markets (repo or sale) even in stress, meaning that the maximum decline in price does not exceed 10 per cent, or the increase in haircut does not exceed 10 percentage points, over a 30-day period in a relevant period of significant liquidity stress; and (iv) not an obligation of a financial institution or any of its associated entities;
  • (b) corporate debt securities (including commercial paper) and covered bonds that satisfy all of the following: (i) for corporate debt securities, not issued by a financial institution or any of its associated entities, and plain vanilla assets whose valuation is readily available from standard methods and does not depend on private knowledge; (ii) for covered bonds, not issued by the ADI itself or any of its associated entities; (iii) a credit rating from a recognised external credit assessment institution (ECAI) of at least AA-, or, for an ADI approved to use the internal ratings-based (IRB) approach under Prudential Standard APS 113 Capital Adequacy: Internal Ratings-based Approach to Credit Risk (APS 113), with no ECAI assessment but an internal rating with a probability of default (PD) corresponding to at least AA-; (iv) traded in large, deep and active repo or cash markets with a low level of concentration; and (v) a proven record as a reliable source of liquidity in the markets (repo or sale) even in stress, meaning a maximum decline in price or increase in haircut over a 30-day period, in a relevant period of significant liquidity stress, not exceeding 10 per cent. Footnote 2 explains how to pick the rating when there are several (Att A footnote 2).

In everyday words: HQLA2A is a notch below cash-like. You may count it, but you "shave" 15% off its market value first, because in a crisis you would expect to get less than market price. The standard defines a "haircut" as the percentage cut applied to market value. "Covered bonds" are bonds issued by a bank and backed by a separate pool of loans; "plain vanilla" means simple, with no hidden features; "probability of default" is the estimated chance of non-payment. The key phrase is "recognised by APRA": an asset in this list is not automatically eligible; APRA or the relevant regulator must have recognised it.

Example: An eligible HQLA2A bond has a market value of A$200m. The haircut is 15% x 200 = A$30m. It counts as 200 - 30 = A$170m. In Australian dollars no assets currently qualify as HQLA2 (APRA's liquidity FAQ), so Example Bank holds none. The case matters for foreign-currency books and for Level 2 groups with offshore subsidiaries (Att A paras 26 to 28).

What you do about it: Keep an HQLA2A rule set in the classification tables, with a field for the regulator's recognition, the ratings, the price-decline test and the issuer type. If a security has several ratings, apply footnote 2. Apply the 15% haircut in the calculation and store both the market value and the post-haircut value. Check each month that no asset has lost eligibility (for example through a rating downgrade, see Att A para 25). For Example Bank the expected result is zero HQLA2A; a non-zero value is a classification error to investigate first.

Evidence we keep: The recognition source (APRA FAQ or regulator list), the ratings evidence and price-history test, and the haircut calculation.

Learn more: Document 07; document 08; Guide Ch 5 s5.

Att A footnote 2: Multiple ratings

What it says: In the event of an asset having multiple assessments, the applicable rating must be determined according to the method used in APS 112 (Att A footnote 2).

In everyday words: If two rating agencies rate the same bond differently, you do not pick the one you like. You follow the rule in the capital standard APS 112 for choosing between them. Footnote 2 is attached to the AA- requirement in Att A para 10(b)(iii) and is picked up again by footnotes 3 and 4.

What you do about it: Record every rating each security has, the date, and the rule used to select the applicable one. This is a data field on the security master. Ask the credit risk or capital team for the APS 112 selection method so you apply it the same way the capital calculations do. If a rating changes, re-run eligibility (Att A para 25).

Evidence we keep: A ratings history table by security and the written selection rule, with a reference to APS 112.

Learn more: Document 07; Guide Ch 5 s5.

Att A para 11: Diversification of HQLA2A

What it says: The portfolio of HQLA2A held by the ADI must be well diversified in terms of type of asset, type of issuer and specific counterparty or issuer (Att A para 11).

In everyday words: Do not hold all your second-grade liquid assets in one bond, one issuer or one kind of paper. If it all comes from one issuer and that issuer has trouble, the whole second-grade buffer goes at once.

What you do about it: Report concentration of the HQLA2A portfolio by asset type, issuer type and single issuer, as a monthly management information item to the Treasury and ALCO (the asset and liability committee). The standard sets no numerical limit, so any concentration threshold is the bank's own choice (typical practice; confirm with your controller). For Example Bank the portfolio is nil, so the report shows nil and says why.

Evidence we keep: The concentration report and the Treasury limit that applies, with ALCO minutes if a breach is discussed.

Learn more: Document 10 (Monitoring beyond the two ratios); Guide Ch 5 s5.

HQLA2B

Att A para 12: HQLA2B: RMBS, corporate debt and shares

What it says: An ADI may include the following as HQLA2B where APRA or the relevant prudential regulator in the jurisdiction where the liquidity risk is taken has recognised them (Att A para 12):

  • (a) subject to a 25 per cent haircut, residential mortgage-backed securities (RMBS) that satisfy all of these: (i) not issued by, and the underlying exposures not originated by, the ADI itself or an associated entity; (ii) a long-term credit rating from a recognised ECAI of AA or higher (see Att A footnote 3) or, if there is no long-term rating, a short-term rating equivalent in quality; (iii) traded in large, deep and active repo or cash markets with a low level of concentration; (iv) a proven record as a reliable source of liquidity in markets (repo or sale) even in stress, meaning a maximum price decline not exceeding 20 per cent, or increase in haircut over a 30-day period not exceeding 20 percentage points, during a relevant period of significant liquidity stress; (v) the underlying pool is restricted to residential mortgages and cannot contain structured products; (vi) the underlying mortgages are full-recourse loans and, at issuance, have a maximum average loan-to-valuation ratio (LVR) of 80 per cent; and (vii) the securitisation is subject to risk retention regulations that require an issuer to keep an interest in the assets it securitises;
  • (b) subject to a 50 per cent haircut, corporate debt securities (including commercial paper) that satisfy all of these: (i) not issued by a financial institution or an associated entity; (ii) either (1) a long-term rating from a recognised ECAI between A+ and BBB- (see Att A footnote 4) or, in the absence of a long-term rating, an equivalent short-term rating, or (2) for an ADI approved to use the IRB approach under APS 113, no ECAI assessment but an internal rating with a PD corresponding to between A+ and BBB-; (iii) traded in large, deep and active repo or cash markets with a low level of concentration; and (iv) a proven record as a reliable source of liquidity in markets (repo or sale) even in stress, meaning a maximum price decline not exceeding 20 per cent, or increase in haircut not exceeding 20 percentage points over a 30-day period, in a relevant period of significant liquidity stress; and
  • (c) subject to a 50 per cent haircut, common equity shares that satisfy all of these: (i) not issued by a financial institution or an associated entity; (ii) exchange traded and centrally cleared; (iii) a constituent of the major stock index where the liquidity risk is taken; (iv) denominated in the currency of the jurisdiction where the bank's liquidity risk is taken; (v) traded in large, deep and active repo or cash markets with a low level of concentration; and (vi) a proven record as a reliable source of liquidity in markets (repo or sale) even in stress, meaning a maximum fall in share price not exceeding 40 per cent, or increase in haircut not exceeding 40 percentage points over a 30-day period, in a relevant period of significant liquidity stress.

In everyday words: HQLA2B is the lowest grade of liquid asset and gets the heaviest shaving: 25% off for the best mortgage-backed bonds, and 50% off for lower-rated company bonds and for shares. A mortgage-backed bond is a bond paid from the repayments of a pool of home loans. "Full recourse" means the lender can chase the borrower's other assets, not only the house. "Loan-to-valuation ratio" is the loan divided by the property value.

Example: Eligible RMBS with a market value of A$100m: haircut 25% x 100 = A$25m; counts as A$75m. Eligible corporate bond rated BBB+ with a market value of A$100m: haircut 50% x 100 = 50; counts as A$50m. An RMBS the bank itself originated fails (a)(i) and counts as nothing, even if it is AAA.

What you do about it: Add the HQLA2B rule set (three sub-classes and their haircuts) to the classification tables, and apply the haircut before the caps in Att A para 7. The 15% HQLA2B cap (Att A para 6) is the binding limit in practice. Check "not originated by the ADI or an associated entity", a common error with the bank's own securitisations. Example Bank holds no HQLA2B; if one appears on the report, stop and escalate to the controller before it is counted.

Evidence we keep: The recognition evidence, the rating and its date, the stress price-history test, the underlying pool information for RMBS (average LVR at issuance, recourse, risk retention), and the haircut calculation.

Use of alternative liquid asset treatment for LCR

In the standard: This section is about the Committed Liquidity Facility (CLF). The aggregate CLF was phased out to zero as at 1 January 2023 (APRA). The paragraphs remain in the standard, so you must still be able to read them, but today no Australian ADI can count a CLF in its LCR. The entries below explain what the paragraphs say and then what that means for the team now.

Att A para 13: Establishing a CLF

What it says: A locally incorporated LCR ADI may establish a CLF with the RBA, sufficient in size to cover any shortfall in Australian dollars between the ADI's holdings of HQLA and its net cash outflows. Qualifying collateral for the CLF comprises all assets eligible for repurchase transactions with the RBA under normal market conditions, and any other assets the RBA nominates (Att A para 13).

In everyday words: Australia has a small supply of government bonds, so the RBA once offered banks a promise: "if you run short of liquid assets, we will lend against a wider pool of collateral." A bank that arranged this promise could count it as if it were liquid assets. The "collateral" is the pool of securities the bank would hand over to borrow.

Example: If a bank had A$10bn of HQLA and A$12bn of net outflows, it would be A$2bn short. A CLF of A$2bn would have closed the gap on paper: (10 + 2) / 12 = 100%.

What you do about it: Nothing in daily production now. The CLF line of the working paper is held at zero, and the control is to confirm it is zero. If anyone ever proposes to count a CLF, stop and take it to the controller, because it needs APRA approval (Att A para 17).

Evidence we keep: A signed confirmation that the bank has no CLF recognised by APRA for LCR purposes, and the CLF line at zero on the working paper.

Learn more: Document 04 (APS 210 companion: the main body); Guide Ch 5 s8.

Att A para 14: What the CLF may be used for

What it says: The CLF must only be used by an ADI to manage any shortfall in Australian dollars between its holding of HQLA and its net cash outflows (Att A para 14).

In everyday words: The facility is for the Australian-dollar gap and nothing else. It cannot be used to plug a shortage in US dollars or to fund growth.

What you do about it: No action while the CLF is nil. If a CLF ever existed, the Australian-dollar LCR (not the all-currencies one) would be where it appears. Liquidity Reporting would show it only in the AUD calculation.

Evidence we keep: As for Att A para 13.

Learn more: Guide Ch 5 s8.

Att A para 15: How much CLF counts in the numerator

What it says: The amount of HQLA that may be included in the numerator of the LCR is the market value of HQLA1 and HQLA2 held, after the haircuts in Att A paras 10 and 12, plus the lowest of (Att A para 15):

  • (a) the contractual amount of the CLF agreed with the RBA and approved by APRA for LCR purposes (Att A paras 17 to 20); or
  • (b) the contractual amount in (a), less the market value, after RBA margins, of eligible CLF securities that have been pledged as collateral under maturing secured funding transactions to a counterparty and to which a zero per cent run-off rate applies, plus the market value, after RBA margins, of eligible CLF securities received as collateral under maturing secured lending transactions, where the collateral received is not used to cover short positions (Att A para 56) and a zero per cent inflow rate applies; or
  • (c) the market value of the assets the ADI holds as collateral for the CLF, after applicable RBA margins.

In everyday words: The CLF counts only up to the least of three amounts: what was agreed, what the agreed amount is after allowing for collateral already tied up in short-term deals (and credit for collateral coming back), and what the bank's actual collateral is worth once the RBA has applied its own haircut (called a "margin"). A promise of A$2bn is useless if you only have A$1bn of eligible securities to pledge.

CLF counted = lowest of:
  (a) contractual CLF approved by APRA
  (b) (a) - pledged eligible CLF securities (maturing, 0% run-off)
          + received eligible CLF securities (maturing, 0% inflow, not covering shorts)
  (c) market value of CLF collateral after RBA margins

Example (hypothetical): (a) is A$2.0bn. Eligible securities of A$0.4bn are pledged in a maturing repo with 0% run-off, and A$0.1bn are received in a maturing reverse repo. (b) = 2.0 - 0.4 + 0.1 = A$1.7bn. (c) the unpledged collateral is worth A$1.5bn after margins. The CLF counted is the lowest: A$1.5bn.

What you do about it: The CLF line is zero, so the three-way test returns zero. Keep the formula in the working paper and set the input to zero so the structure is ready if the regime changes.

Evidence we keep: The CLF line at zero with the confirmation from Att A para 13.

Learn more: Guide Ch 5 s8.2.

Att A para 16: Assets held for the CLF

What it says: Assets held for the purposes of the CLF must (a) meet the operational requirements for HQLA in Att A paras 22 to 25; and (b) be well diversified in terms of type of asset, type of issuer and specific counterparty or issuer (Att A para 16).

In everyday words: The collateral behind the facility has to be as properly managed as HQLA itself: free of pledges, under Treasury's control and spread across issuers.

What you do about it: Not applicable while the CLF is nil. If it were revived, the same checks as for HQLA (Att A paras 22 to 25) and the diversification report (Att A para 11) would extend to the CLF collateral pool.

Evidence we keep: As for Att A para 13.

What it says: An ADI that requires a CLF from the RBA for LCR purposes must apply to APRA for approval before establishing the CLF (Att A para 17).

In everyday words: The bank asks first. It cannot just sign a facility with the RBA and start counting it.

What you do about it: No action for our team while the CLF is nil; owned by Treasury and the Board, with Liquidity Risk review. If an application were ever made, Liquidity Reporting would supply the LCR history and forecasts.

Att A para 18: Funding strategy and the "own balance sheet first" test

What it says: To help APRA's approval process, the ADI's funding strategy must clearly state the amount of CLF required in the forecast period. The ADI must make every reasonable effort to manage its liquidity risk through its own balance sheet management before applying for a CLF for LCR purposes (Att A para 18).

In everyday words: A bank may not lean on the RBA as a first resort. It must first try to fix its own balance sheet (hold more government bonds, lengthen its funding) and show APRA that it tried. The CLF amount must also appear in the funding strategy (see APS 210 para 44).

In everyday words: The approval is not permanent. The size is re-approved every year.

What you do about it: No action while the CLF is nil. If a CLF existed this would be a calendar item in the annual working year (document 03, Your role, the team and the working year).

Att A para 20: Change of circumstances

What it says: If circumstances change unexpectedly, an ADI may apply to APRA at any time to change the amount of CLF recognised for LCR purposes, by submitting an updated Board-approved funding and liquidity plan (Att A para 20).

In everyday words: Mid-year changes are allowed, but the Board must approve the new plan first.

What you do about it: No action while nil. The point to remember is that any such application has to carry Board approval.

Att A para 21: Drawing on the CLF

What it says: If an ADI draws funds under its CLF other than for intraday purposes or for funding end-of-day balances held at the RBA, senior management must attest that the proceeds will be used for Australian-dollar liquidity needs only. APRA must be advised of any proposed drawdown of the CLF that is not for intraday purposes or for funding end-of-day balances held at the RBA (Att A para 21).

In everyday words: Borrowing from the RBA for a few hours during the day to keep payments flowing is routine. Borrowing to meet a genuine funding need is a bigger event: management must give a written assurance on the use, and APRA must be told beforehand.

What you do about it: No action for our team while the CLF is nil. If it were not nil, the drawdown would be an escalation event under document 12 (Controls, incidents and escalation).

Operational requirements

Att A para 22: Control of the liquid asset stock

What it says: All assets in the stock of eligible HQLA must be managed as part of that stock and meet the operational requirements below (Att A para 22):

  • (a) the assets must be available for the ADI to convert into cash at any time;
  • (b) the assets must be unencumbered and under the control of the function or functions charged with managing the ADI's liquidity risk, typically the treasurer, who must have the continuous authority, and the legal and operational capability, to monetise any asset in the stock; and
  • (c) control must be evidenced either by keeping the assets in a separate pool managed by that function with the sole intent of using it as a source of contingent funds, or by demonstrating that the function can monetise the asset at any point in the 30-day stress period and that the proceeds are available to the function throughout the period without directly conflicting with a stated business or risk management strategy.

In everyday words: An asset only counts if the people who would have to sell it in a crisis actually can. To "monetise" an asset is to turn it into cash by selling it or borrowing against it. If a bond is sitting in a trading book that a trader will not sell because it supports a business strategy, or it is held in a way Treasury cannot touch, it is not part of the buffer. "Unencumbered" means not pledged to anybody.

Example: Example Bank has A$8.0bn of government bonds. A$6.0bn is in the Treasury liquidity portfolio. A$2.0bn is held by the markets desk as inventory for a trading strategy and Treasury has no authority to sell it. Only the A$6.0bn passes (b) and (c). The A$2.0bn is excluded until the position is brought under Treasury's control or a documented right to monetise it is shown.

What you do about it: Each month (typical practice; confirm with your controller), ask Treasury to confirm in writing that every security in the LCR stock is in the liquidity portfolio or has a documented monetisation right, and reconcile the securities in the LCR to the portfolio list. Daily, check that nothing in the LCR stock is flagged as encumbered, held in a trading strategy or held in a custody account that Treasury does not control. Treasury owns the portfolio and the demonstration of control; Liquidity Risk tests it.

Evidence we keep: The portfolio mandate stating the sole intent, Treasury's monthly confirmation, the securities reconciliation and the evidence of custody and settlement arrangements.

Learn more: Document 08 (Producing the daily LCR, end to end); document 07; Guide Ch 5 s9.1.

Att A para 23: No pledging; reverse repo collateral

What it says: The assets must not be pledged to secure, collateralise or credit enhance any transactions, or be designated to cover operational costs such as rent and salaries. Assets received in reverse repo and securities financing transactions that are held at the ADI, have not been rehypothecated and are legally and contractually available for the ADI's use may be considered part of the stock of HQLA (Att A para 23).

In everyday words: A pledged bond belongs to somebody else's claim, so it cannot also be your safety net. An asset set aside to pay the rent cannot be your safety net either. On the other hand, a bond you hold because you lent cash against it (a reverse repo) can be counted, but only if you still have it, have not on-lent it ("rehypothecated" means re-used as collateral for another deal), and the contract lets you use it. "Securities financing transactions" is the family of repo, reverse repo and securities lending deals.

Example: Example Bank has an A$2.0bn government bond holding pledged under a 14-day repo to borrow cash. Those A$2.0bn are encumbered and are not HQLA. They do not count, even though the repo matures in 14 days. The A$2.0bn of cash received in the repo is a liability: the repo is a secured funding outflow (Att A Table 3).

What you do about it: Take an encumbrance flag from the collateral system for every security. Exclude any security with a pledge, whatever its maturity. For reverse repo collateral, include it only when all three conditions are confirmed: held at the ADI, not rehypothecated, and legally and contractually available. Daily, reconcile the encumbrance flag to the repo and derivative lists. Note that the NSFR treats encumbrance differently (encumbered for under six months is treated as unencumbered for RSF purposes; document 06 for the NSFR rules). Do not mix the two treatments.

Evidence we keep: The encumbrance report from the collateral system, the repo and securities lending trade lists, and the reconciliation of the two.

Learn more: Document 07; document 08; document 06 (companion to Attachments B and C); Guide Ch 5 s9.2.

Watch out: An asset is not "partly" encumbered by maturity. Maturity of the pledge does not matter in the LCR: today's pledge excludes it today.

Att A para 24: Liquid assets held in legal entities (Level 2)

What it says: At Level 2, an ADI may also include in the stock of HQLA liquid assets that qualify for inclusion in the numerator under Att A paras 9 to 17, 22 to 23 and 28, where they are held to meet legal entity requirements and the related risks, as measured by the legal entity's net cash outflows, are also reflected in the LCR. Any surplus of liquid assets held at the legal entity may only be included in the Level 2 stock if those assets would be freely available to the consolidated (parent) entity in times of stress (Att A para 24).

In everyday words: A group (the parent bank and its subsidiaries) must be able to count liquid assets that sit in a subsidiary only if they genuinely help the group. If a subsidiary needs its assets to meet its own local rules, you may count them but must also count the outflows of that subsidiary. Extra assets, beyond what the subsidiary needs, count only if they could be moved up to the parent freely.

Example (hypothetical): A subsidiary holds A$300m of HQLA and has net outflows of A$200m. The subsidiary's own needs use A$200m. The A$100m surplus counts at group level only if it could be freely moved to the parent in a crisis. If a local law traps the cash, the surplus is excluded.

What you do about it: Example Bank has one legal entity, so Level 1 equals Level 2 and this paragraph does not change the numbers. If the group structure changes, a consolidation step would be needed and the trapped-liquidity test (Att A para 29) would be added. Tell your controller at once of any new subsidiary.

Evidence we keep: The group structure chart confirming a single entity, and the statement in the methodology that Level 1 = Level 2.

Learn more: Document 10 (Monitoring beyond the two ratios), Level 1 and Level 2; Guide Ch 5 s10.4.

Att A para 25: Grace period when an asset becomes ineligible

What it says: For calculating the LCR, if an eligible HQLA or eligible CLF liquid asset becomes ineligible (for example through a rating downgrade), an ADI may keep the asset in its stock of liquid assets for an additional 30 calendar days. This gives the ADI time to adjust its stock or replace the asset (Att A para 25).

In everyday words: If a bond is downgraded out of eligibility overnight, the LCR does not collapse the same day. You get another 30 calendar days to replace it. The clock starts on the day of ineligibility. The same relief covers any loss of eligibility; the standard's example is a rating downgrade.

Example: On 1 March a bond rated AA- (A$500m, HQLA2A) is downgraded to A+ and no longer qualifies as HQLA2A. It may remain in the stock, at its HQLA2A value, for a further 30 calendar days, which is until 31 March (confirm the counting convention with your controller). If it has not been replaced by then, it drops out of the LCR.

What you do about it: Run a daily eligibility check on rating, price behaviour, issuer type and any other criteria. If an asset fails, record the date it became ineligible, the 30-day end date and the plan to replace it. Warn Treasury and the controller when the asset joins the list, then again at 10 days to run (typical practice; confirm with your controller). The grace period applies only to loss of eligibility, not to encumbrance (an encumbered asset is excluded at once, Att A para 23).

Evidence we keep: The ineligibility log with the dates, the replacement plan, and the daily LCR showing the asset in and then out of the stock.

Learn more: Document 08; document 12 (Controls, incidents and escalation), the ineligible-asset procedure; Guide Ch 5 s9.3.

Level 2 consolidated banking group

Att A para 26: Level 1 and Level 2, offshore branches and subsidiaries

What it says: The LCR is to be met by an ADI on both a Level 1 and a Level 2 basis. Where an ADI has a branch or subsidiary in an offshore jurisdiction, in calculating a subsidiary's LCR the ADI must apply the requirements in the standard. The only exceptions are (Att A para 26):

  • (a) for retail and small and medium enterprise (SME) deposits, the host supervisor's outflow assumptions must be applied; and
  • (b) alternative liquid assets, as provided for in the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision's global framework for liquidity risk and allowed by the host supervisor, may be included.

In everyday words: Level 1 is the bank on its own. Level 2 is the bank plus its subsidiaries. For an overseas arm, you use the Australian rules except for retail and small-business deposit run-off (use the local regulator's rates) and for extra liquid assets that the local regulator allows under the Basel framework. The Basel Committee is the international body that sets global banking standards.

Example (hypothetical): A New Zealand subsidiary's retail deposits would be run off at the rates in the host supervisor's rules, but its wholesale funding would use the Table 3 rates.

What you do about it: Not applicable to Example Bank, which has no offshore branch or subsidiary and meets the LCR at both levels with the same numbers. Note in the methodology that both levels are calculated and equal. For a group with offshore arms, Liquidity Reporting would need a data feed from each arm and a mapping of local deposit rules.

Evidence we keep: The methodology statement on the scope of consolidation and the check that Level 1 equals Level 2.

Att A para 27: Offshore regimes that do not follow Basel

What it says: Where an ADI has an offshore branch or subsidiary in a jurisdiction that does not apply the Basel Committee's global framework for liquidity risk, the cash flow assumptions in the standard must be applied in calculating the ADI's Level 2 LCR (Att A para 27).

In everyday words: If the host country has no Basel-style liquidity rules, there is no local rate to use, so the Australian rates apply in full, including to retail deposits.

What you do about it: As for Att A para 26: not applicable to Example Bank. For a group with such an arm, record the decision that no host rule exists and apply Attachment A in full.

Att A para 28: Material offshore banking subsidiaries

What it says: An ADI with a material banking subsidiary in another offshore jurisdiction must ensure that the subsidiary keeps an LCR of at least 100 per cent. APRA may allow the ADI to include assets formally recognised as eligible liquid assets by the relevant offshore prudential regulator (Att A para 28).

In everyday words: A big foreign subsidiary has to stand on its own feet: its own LCR must be at least 100%. APRA can let it count local assets that its own regulator recognises.

What you do about it: Not applicable to Example Bank. For a group with a material subsidiary, the team would calculate the subsidiary's LCR daily and report it separately. Att A para 29: Trapped liquidity

What it says: For the Level 2 LCR, an ADI must take into account restrictions on the transferability of liquid assets across borders. Excess liquidity is not to be recognised in the Level 2 LCR unless there is reasonable certainty about its availability (Att A para 29).

In everyday words: Cash in a subsidiary abroad is only a buffer for the group if it can be sent home. Foreign exchange controls, local ring-fencing rules, tax and operational limits can all trap it. If you cannot be reasonably sure it can move, do not count it.

What you do about it: Not applicable to Example Bank at present. If a subsidiary is added, obtain a legal opinion or Treasury confirmation on transferability, and exclude any amount not freely transferable.

Net cash outflows

Att A para 30: The denominator and the 75 per cent inflow cap

What it says: Total net cash outflows are the total expected cash outflows minus the total expected cash inflows in the stress scenario for the next 30 calendar days. Expected outflows are calculated by multiplying the outstanding balances of the various categories of liabilities and off-balance sheet commitments by the rates at which they are expected to run off or be drawn down, as set out in Table 3 in Att A para 53. Expected inflows are calculated by multiplying the outstanding balances of the various categories of contractual receivables by the inflow rates in Att A paras 54 to 66, up to an aggregate cap of 75 per cent of total expected cash outflows. That is (Att A para 30):

Total net cash outflows over the next 30 calendar days
   = Total expected cash outflows
     - Min { total expected cash inflows ; 75% of total expected cash outflows }

In everyday words: Work out how much cash would leave (balance x run-off rate for each type of liability and commitment). Work out how much would arrive (balance x inflow rate for each type of contractual receivable). Then subtract, but you may never take credit for more than 75% of the outflows. So net outflows can never be below 25% of gross outflows. The reason: a bank cannot rely wholly on its borrowers repaying on time in a crisis. A "run-off rate" is the percentage of a balance expected to leave in the stress period. A "balance" means the outstanding amount, not a limit. An "off-balance sheet commitment" is a promise to lend or pay that is not yet on the balance sheet, for example an undrawn credit line.

Example 1 (Example Bank, cap not binding): Outflows are 13.45. Inflows are 1.90. The cap is 75% x 13.45 = 10.09 (10.0875). Min{1.90; 10.09} = 1.90. Net outflows = 13.45 - 1.90 = 11.55. LCR = 17.00 / 11.55 = 147.2%.

Example 2 (cap binding): Outflows are 100 and calculated inflows are 90. The cap is 75% x 100 = 75. Min{90; 75} = 75. Net outflows = 100 - 75 = 25, not 100 - 90 = 10. Using 10 would overstate the LCR by two and a half times.

What you do about it: In the daily calculation, compute gross outflows and gross inflows separately, then apply the cap as its own explicit line, and show both the uncapped and capped inflows. Check each day whether the cap is binding. If it is, say so in the commentary, because it means the ratio is being held back by the cap. The cap must be on the total, not applied category by category. Reviewers check that the cap line equals 0.75 x outflows to two decimal places.

Evidence we keep: The calculation sheet with outflows, inflows, cap and net outflows, with the formula visible, plus the reviewer's tick against each.

Learn more: Document 08 (Producing the daily LCR, end to end); document 14 (Exercises and assessments), inflow cap case; Guide Ch 5 s11.1.

Att A para 31: No double counting

What it says: An ADI must not double count items. If assets are included in the stock of HQLA (the numerator), the associated cash inflows cannot also be counted as cash inflows (Att A para 31).

In everyday words: Each dollar can only be counted once. If a government bond is already in the HQLA stock at its market value and it matures in 10 days, you cannot also count the redemption cash as an inflow. The same applies to derivative collateral already counted as HQLA (see Att A footnote 5).

Example: Example Bank holds an A$1.0bn government bond maturing in 10 days. It is in the stock at A$1.0bn. Counting the A$1.0bn redemption as an inflow as well would add A$1.0bn to the inflows (within the 75% cap), cut net outflows from 11.55 to 10.55 and falsely lift the ratio from 147.2% to 161.1%.

What you do about it: When classifying inflows, check each item against the HQLA list. Securities that mature within 30 days and are in the stock are excluded from inflows. Securities that mature within 30 days but are not in the stock (Att A para 62: "maturing securities not included in the stock of HQLA" get 100%) are included. Add a control: the sum of the inflow lines should not include any security identifier that is in the HQLA stock. Do the same for cash collateral.

Evidence we keep: The matching test between the inflow list and the HQLA list, and a zero-exceptions result.

Learn more: Document 08; Guide Ch 5 s11.2.

Att A para 32: Assumptions APRA assesses

What it says: APRA will assess the suitability of an ADI's assumptions for cash flow items that are derived (not given by the standard). An ADI must be able to provide analysis and evidence to justify the assumptions (Att A para 32).

In everyday words: Wherever the standard says "to be set by the ADI" (for example Table 3, "other non-contractual contingent funding obligations") or you have to estimate (for example the average monthly net outflow on trade finance), you decide, but you must be able to defend it to the regulator with data.

Example: For trade finance the rate is the average of actual monthly net outflows in a recent 12-month period (Att A Table 3). Example Bank's twelve monthly net outflows sum to A$0.24bn. The average is 0.24 / 12 = A$0.02bn. The evidence is the 12 monthly figures and the source of each.

What you do about it: Keep an "assumptions register" listing every derived input: what it is, the method, the data period, the owner, the date last reviewed and the evidence. Review it at least annually and whenever the business changes (typical practice; confirm with your controller). When APRA asks, you must be able to produce the file in a day.

Evidence we keep: The assumptions register, the supporting data, the approval minute and the review dates.

Learn more: Document 08; document 11 (Returns to APRA and public disclosure), responding to APRA; Guide Ch 5 s11.3.

Att A para 33: Collateral swaps APRA may require

What it says: Where APRA determines that an ADI is actively using collateral swaps for liquidity management purposes, APRA may require the ADI to include in its LCR calculation the net cash flow impact of those collateral swaps, using a method APRA determines at the time (Att A para 33).

In everyday words: A collateral swap exchanges one set of securities for another without any cash. A bank might use it to turn weak collateral into strong collateral at the right moment. If APRA thinks the bank is doing so to flatter its liquidity, it can tell the bank to model the cash effect in its own way.

What you do about it: Include collateral swaps in the transactions list you use for the adjusted amounts (Att A para 8). If APRA gives such a direction, build the method into the calculation as instructed and document the date and the instruction. Until then, no separate cash-flow line is needed. Example Bank's convention is that Treasury tells the controller before entering any collateral swap (typical practice; confirm with your controller).

Evidence we keep: The list of collateral swaps and any APRA direction.

Learn more: Guide Ch 5 s6.4 and s11.4.

Cash outflows

Retail deposit outflows

Att A para 34: What is a retail deposit?

What it says: For the LCR, retail deposits are deposits placed with an ADI by a natural person. Deposits from legal entities, sole proprietorships or partnerships fall into the wholesale deposit categories. Retail deposits include demand deposits and term deposits, unless excluded under the criteria in Att A paras 41 and 42 (Att A para 34).

In everyday words: A "natural person" is a human being, not a company. A deposit is retail only if a person holds it. A plumber's account in his trading name (a sole proprietorship) is not retail for the LCR, it is wholesale, however small. A "demand deposit" can be withdrawn at any time; a "term deposit" is locked for a fixed period. Term deposits can be excluded from the outflows if they meet the rules in Att A paras 41 and 42.

Example: Example Bank's A$25.0bn of stable retail deposits and A$15.0bn of less stable retail deposits are held by individuals. The same bank's A$5.0bn of SME deposits come from businesses and are handled under Att A para 46 (treated like retail if they qualify), not Att A para 34.

What you do about it: The customer type must be a data field on every deposit record: natural person or not. Check that sole traders and partnerships are mapped to wholesale (or the SME rule in Att A para 46). Reconcile total retail deposits to the ledger each day. Customer master data owners are accountable for the type flag; Liquidity Reporting tests it.

Evidence we keep: The mapping table from customer type to LCR category, a reconciliation to the ledger and the data quality test results.

Learn more: Document 07 (Data and classification), deposit decision tree; Guide Ch 5 s12.1.

Att A para 35: Deposits placed through an intermediary

What it says: Where a person places funds with an intermediary, which then places those funds with an ADI, the deposit is considered a deposit from a financial institution unless, during the next 30 days (Att A para 35):

  • (a) the person retains all legal rights regarding withdrawal or other movement of the funds;
  • (b) the person exercises these rights in practice and cannot transfer them to the intermediary;
  • (c) there is clear and prominent disclosure to the person that the funds will be placed with the ADI; and
  • (d) the intermediary or an associated entity can neither make investment decisions for the person about the deposit, nor withdraw funds from the ADI without the person's specific direction (other than miscellaneous items such as fees, expense reimbursements and taxes).

If all conditions are satisfied, the ADI may treat the funds as being from the person. The ADI must be able to demonstrate how the treatment satisfies the conditions.

In everyday words: If a platform, adviser or broker puts customers' money into your bank, the safe assumption is that the platform could move it all in a panic, so it is treated as a financial institution deposit at 100% run-off in most cases. You may treat it as retail only if each customer really keeps control, knows where the money is, and the intermediary cannot move it on its own. All four conditions must hold for the whole 30 days.

Example: An investment platform places A$200m of client cash into Example Bank. If conditions (a) to (d) all hold, the A$200m can be treated as retail (stable or less stable as the case may be, for example at 10% = A$20m). If any one fails, it is a financial institution deposit, non-operational, at 100% = A$200m. The difference in outflow is A$180m.

What you do about it: Identify every intermediated deposit (a flag on the account). For each intermediary relationship, hold a signed assessment against (a) to (d) approved by the controller and Liquidity Risk. Review at least annually and when the contract or product changes (typical practice; confirm with your controller). Default to the financial institution treatment where evidence is missing and record it.

Evidence we keep: The assessment of each intermediary against (a) to (d), the contract extracts, the customer disclosure, and the approval record, so that the ADI can "demonstrate" compliance as the paragraph requires.

Learn more: Document 07; Guide Ch 5 s12.2; Guide Ch 4 s4.1.

Att A para 36: Stable and less stable portions

What it says: Retail deposits are divided into stable and less stable portions of funds, as described in Att A paras 37 and 38 (Att A para 36).

In everyday words: Not all retail money behaves the same. Some of it is very unlikely to leave in a panic (stable). The rest is not (less stable). Note "portions": one customer's balance can be split, with the covered part stable and the excess less stable.

Example: A customer has A$300,000 in a transaction account where the salary is paid. The first A$250,000 is covered by the Financial Claims Scheme (FCS) and is stable. The remaining A$50,000 is not covered and is less stable.

What you do about it: Split each retail balance by customer (not by account) into the covered and uncovered parts, then classify each part. Sum the stable and less stable portions separately for the working paper.

Evidence we keep: The split calculation by customer and the reconciliation of the total to the ledger.

Learn more: Document 07; Guide Ch 5 s12.3.

Att A para 37: Stable deposits

What it says: Stable deposits are the portion of deposits that is fully covered by the Financial Claims Scheme (or an effective government deposit insurance scheme) where (Att A para 37):

  • (a) the depositor has other established relationships with the ADI that make withdrawal highly unlikely; or
  • (b) the deposits are in transactional accounts (for example accounts into which salaries are automatically credited).

In everyday words: To be stable, a deposit must pass two tests: insured, and sticky. The Financial Claims Scheme is the Australian Government guarantee of deposits up to A$250,000 per account holder per ADI. Sticky means the customer would find it hard or unlikely to leave: either they have other products with the bank (a home loan, say) or the account is where their pay arrives and bills are paid. Stable deposits run off at 5% (Att A Table 3).

Example: Example Bank's A$25.0bn of stable retail deposits at 5% gives outflow of 25.0 x 5% = A$1.25bn. A customer with a home loan and A$60,000 in savings is stable (insured, with an established relationship). A customer with A$60,000 in a new savings account with no other product and no salary is not stable, because neither (a) nor (b) holds.

What you do about it: For each customer, work out the insured portion (up to A$250,000 across the ADI, not per account), then test (a) and (b). The definition of an "established relationship" is not in the standard, so the bank's own definition applies and must be written down and approved (typical practice; confirm with your controller). Typical tests are other products held, and length of time as customer. Review it annually. Liquidity Risk challenges it.

Evidence we keep: The written definition of an established relationship, the approval, the FCS cover calculation and the customer-level classification file.

Learn more: Document 07; document 14; Guide Ch 5 s12.3; Guide Ch 2 (Financial Claims Scheme).

Att A para 38: Less stable deposits

What it says: Less stable deposits are the portion of deposits that do not meet the requirements of stable deposits (Att A para 38).

In everyday words: Everything that is not stable is less stable. It is a "default" bucket: if you cannot prove stability, the money is less stable. The less stable bucket is then run through the scorecard (Att A para 40) to decide between 10% and 25%.

What you do about it: Define less stable by exclusion in the code: total retail minus stable. Never classify as stable by default. Where an attribute is missing, put the balance in less stable and record the gap.

Evidence we keep: The classification logic and the missing-attribute log.

Learn more: Document 07; Guide Ch 5 s12.3.

Att A para 39: Deposits more likely to leave

What it says: Certain types of deposits are considered more likely to be withdrawn in stress. These include high-value deposits (those above any government deposit guarantee limit), deposits from customers without established relationships with the ADI that would make withdrawal unlikely, deposits where the internet is integral to the design, marketing and usage of the account (on-line accounts), and deposits with promotional interest rates (heavily rate-driven) (Att A para 39).

In everyday words: Four warning signs: lots of money (more than the A$250,000 guarantee, so the customer has a real reason to worry); no connection to the bank; an internet-only account (a click moves the money); and a bonus rate that attracts rate chasers who leave when the rate drops. These four signs become the four rows of the scorecard in Att A para 40.

Example: A customer opens an internet-only savings account at a promotional rate with A$20,000 and has no other product. Three of the four signs apply: on-line, no relationship, rate-driven.

What you do about it: Make sure the data has the four flags at account or product level: balance over the guarantee limit, on-line, no established relationship, rate-driven. The product team owns the on-line and promotional flags (they set up the products); the customer team owns relationship. Liquidity Reporting checks that every product has all four flags populated.

Evidence we keep: The product register with the four flags and its approval, and the exception log for missing flags.

Learn more: Document 07; Guide Ch 5 s12.3.

Att A para 40: The scorecard

What it says: An ADI must assess each less stable account or product balance against the scorecard in Table 1. The total score is then used to allocate the outflow rate under Table 2 (Att A para 40).

In everyday words: Give each less stable balance points for each warning sign, add them up and read off the run-off rate. Low score (0 to 2): 10% leaves. High score (3 or more): 25% leaves. The assessment is "each less stable account or product balance", so it is done at product or account level. Where all customers in a product share the same features, you may score the product once.

What you do about it: Run the scorecard on every balance that fails the stable test, at account or product level, and store the score with the balance. Re-score when a product's features change.

Evidence we keep: The scored deposit file for each reporting date and the approved scoring rules.

Learn more: The two table entries that follow; document 07 (Data and classification); document 13 (Job aids and checklists), scorecard card.

What it says: Four criteria earn points: a balance above the guarantee limit (or the equivalent of A$250,000 where there is no guarantee limit) scores 2; an on-line account scores 2; no established relationship scores 1; a heavily rate-driven deposit scores 1. Fixed-term deposits

Att A para 41: Term deposits locked in beyond 30 days

What it says: Cash outflows on fixed or term deposits with a residual maturity or withdrawal notice period of more than 30 days are excluded from the LCR calculations if the depositor has no legal right to withdraw within the 30-day horizon, or if early withdrawal results in a significant penalty that is materially greater than the loss of interest (Att A para 41).

In everyday words: If the customer cannot take the money out inside 30 days (or would lose much more than just the interest if they did) then in the 30-day window nothing can leave, so the run-off is 0%. "Residual maturity" is the time left until the deposit is due; "notice period" is the warning the customer must give before withdrawing.

Example: Example Bank's A$10.0bn of retail term deposits has 31 to 360 days left, with no early withdrawal and a penalty greater than the interest lost. Outflow is 10.0 x 0% = nil. By contrast, a term deposit with 20 days left falls within the 30-day window and is run off as a normal retail deposit: A$2.0bn at 10% = A$0.20bn (the practice case, item d).

What you do about it: Use the contractual residual maturity at the reporting date and the product terms: if over 30 days, check that (i) there is no legal right to withdraw early, or (ii) the penalty is significant and materially greater than the lost interest. The bank must define "significant" and "materially greater" (typical practice; confirm with your controller), and keep the test with the product approval. Deposits that fail the test are treated as demand deposits and run off at the retail rates in Table 3. Treasury or product owners confirm no early-withdrawal practice (see Att A para 42).

Evidence we keep: The product terms, the penalty test and approval, the maturity profile and the Att A para 42 confirmation.

Learn more: Document 07; Guide Ch 5 s12.5.

Att A para 42: If the bank lets customers out anyway

What it says: If an ADI allows a depositor to withdraw such deposits despite a clause saying the depositor has no legal right to withdraw, the entire category of these funds must be treated as demand deposits (Att A para 42).

In everyday words: A rule on paper does not count if the bank does not follow it. If the bank lets one customer out early, the whole category (every deposit with the same clause) loses the 0% treatment. This is called "the whole pool is tainted". The reason is that depositors will expect the bank to let them out too.

Example: Example Bank's A$10.0bn of non-breakable retail term deposits (base position) normally run off at 0%. If the bank starts allowing early withdrawal on request for convenience, all A$10.0bn are treated as demand deposits and, as less stable balances scoring 0 to 2, run off at 10% (Att A Table 2). Outflow becomes A$1.00bn instead of nil, net outflows rise from 11.55 to 12.55 and the LCR falls from 147.2% to 17.00 / 12.55 = 135.5%.

What you do about it: Agree with the branch and product teams a rule that no early withdrawal is permitted outside the hardship policy (Att A para 43). Ask for a monthly count of early withdrawals and the reason for each. Any non-hardship early withdrawal is escalated the same day. The controller decides, with Liquidity Risk, whether the pool must be reclassified.

Evidence we keep: The monthly early-withdrawal log with reasons, and the decision record for any reclassification.

Learn more: Document 07; document 12 (Controls, incidents and escalation); Guide Ch 5 s12.5.

Watch out: One favour to a well-connected customer can undo 0% treatment for billions. Ask for the log; do not assume.

Att A para 43: Hardship withdrawals

What it says: An ADI may allow depositors experiencing hardship to withdraw their term deposits without changing the treatment of the entire pool of deposits. The ADI must have a policy defining hardship (Att A para 43).

In everyday words: A customer who has lost their job or is ill can be let out early without spoiling the pool, as long as the bank has a written policy that defines hardship and the bank follows it.

What you do about it: Obtain the hardship policy from the product owner, confirm it is approved and current, and check that each early withdrawal on the monthly log is tagged "hardship" with a reference to the policy. Untagged ones are Att A para 42 events.

Evidence we keep: The approved hardship policy, the case-by-case approval, and the tagged log.

Learn more: Document 07; Guide Ch 5 s12.5.

Unsecured wholesale funding outflows

Att A para 44: What unsecured wholesale funding is

What it says: For the LCR, unsecured wholesale funding means those liabilities and general obligations raised from non-natural persons (that is, legal entities, including sole proprietorships and partnerships) that are not collateralised by legal rights to specifically designated assets owned by the borrowing institution in the case of bankruptcy, insolvency, liquidation or resolution. Obligations related to derivative contracts are explicitly excluded from this definition (Att A para 44).

In everyday words: Wholesale means the money comes from organisations, not individual people. Unsecured means the lender has no specific asset to claim if the bank fails, so the lender can only rely on the bank's general promise. Derivative obligations are not in this bucket: they have their own rows in Table 3.

Example: Example Bank's corporate deposits (A$10.0bn), financial institution deposits (A$4.0bn) and wholesale debt (A$21.0bn) are unsecured wholesale funding. A repo, where the bank gives a bond as security for cash, is secured funding and not in this bucket. A swap payable is a derivative and also not in this bucket.

What you do about it: Each liability needs three flags: customer is a natural person or not; secured by designated assets or not; derivative or not. Then route it: retail (Att A para 34), unsecured wholesale (this section), secured (Table 3), or derivative (Table 3). Check that the wholesale total plus retail, secured and derivative totals equal total liabilities on the ledger.

Evidence we keep: The routing table, the reconciliation to total liabilities and the data quality tests.

Learn more: Document 07; Guide Ch 5 s13.1; Guide Ch 4 s4.10.

Att A para 45: Which wholesale funding is inside the 30 days

What it says: Wholesale funding included in the LCR means all funding that is callable within the 30-day horizon, or whose earliest possible contractual maturity date is within it (such as maturing term deposits and unsecured debt securities), as well as funding with an undetermined maturity. It must include all funding with options exercisable at the investor's discretion within the 30-day horizon. Wholesale funding that the provider of the funds can call, subject to a contractually defined and binding notice period longer than the 30-day horizon, may be excluded (Att A para 45).

In everyday words: Ask one question of every wholesale liability: could the lender get the money back inside the next 30 days? If yes, it counts. This includes at-call deposits, deposits with no maturity date, debt maturing within 30 days, and debt where the lender holds an option to ask for repayment early (a "put"). Use the earliest date the lender could possibly demand repayment, not the headline maturity. A bond with a 5-year maturity but a put in 20 days counts. A deposit the lender can call only on 60 days' written notice, if the notice is binding, may be left out.

Example: Example Bank's wholesale debt is A$1.0bn due within 30 days (included, 100%), A$3.0bn due 30 days to under 6 months (excluded, because it falls outside the window), A$2.0bn due in 6 to 12 months (excluded) and A$15.0bn due in a year or more (excluded). But if A$2.8bn of the A$3.0bn contains an investor put that can be exercised in 25 days, it moves inside the window and is included, with a 100% outflow. This is the incident scenario in document 12.

What you do about it: For every wholesale instrument keep the earliest contractual maturity, any put or call options, the date each could be exercised, and any binding notice period. Calculate days to the earliest possible date, not the stated maturity date. Include anything of 30 days or fewer. Run a forward-looking view of maturities to catch debt "rolling into" the window 30 days before it arrives (document 08, forward-looking LCR). Treasury must inform you of any new option clause on new issues.

Evidence we keep: The instrument terms database extract, the options register, the earliest-date calculation and the forward-looking maturity ladder.

Learn more: Document 07; document 08; document 12; Guide Ch 5 s13.2.

Watch out: "30 days" is measured from the reporting date. A maturity on day 31 is outside; a maturity on day 30 is inside.

Att A para 46: SME funding treated as retail

What it says: Unsecured wholesale funding provided by SME customers may be treated as retail deposits where (Att A para 46):

  • (a) the deposits and other extensions of funds made by non-financial SME customers are managed as retail exposures and are generally considered as having similar liquidity risk characteristics to retail accounts; and
  • (b) the total aggregated funding raised from the SME customer is less than AUD 2 million (on a consolidated basis where applicable).

In everyday words: A small business that banks like a household can be treated like a household for run-off purposes, if two tests are met: the bank manages the relationship the way it manages retail customers, and the business's total funding with the bank is under A$2 million (counting related companies together). Above A$2 million, it is wholesale: in Table 3 these are "SMEs of greater than AUD 2m" in the non-operational line. Footnote 7 explains what to do if you cannot apply this definition (Att A footnote 7).

Example: An SME has A$1.5m with the bank and a related company has A$0.4m. Consolidated funding is A$1.9m, under A$2m, so the deposits may be treated as retail and (if less stable, score 0 to 2) run off at 10%: 1.9 x 10% = A$0.19m. If the related company had A$0.7m, the total would be A$2.2m and the whole amount would be non-operational wholesale: at 40% if not fully covered by deposit insurance, 2.2 x 40% = A$0.88m.

What you do about it: Aggregate each SME customer group's total funding across all products and related entities, daily or at least on each reporting date. Test (a) against product management information (is the customer on a retail or business banking platform?) and (b) against the A$2 million figure. Apply the retail rules from Att A para 36 (stable or less stable, scorecard). Example Bank's A$5.0bn of qualifying SME deposits are less stable at 10% (A$0.50bn). Review SME classification once a year with Business Banking.

Evidence we keep: The SME aggregation logic and group-linkage data, the test results for each customer above A$1.5m (typical practice; confirm with your controller), and the confirmation from the business that the segment is managed as retail.

Learn more: Document 07; Guide Ch 5 s13.3.

Operational deposits

Att A para 47: What an operational deposit is

What it says: For the LCR, operational deposits are those where customers place, or leave, deposits with an ADI in order to facilitate their access and ability to use payment and settlement systems and otherwise make payments. Deposits may be included as operational only if the customer has a substantive dependency on the ADI and the deposit is required for such activities (Att A para 47).

In everyday words: Some companies keep money with a bank because they have to, so they can pay staff and suppliers through it, or because the bank holds their securities. They cannot move it away quickly without disrupting their business. These deposits are stickier, so they run off less.

Example: A company runs its payroll and supplier payments through Example Bank and keeps A$20m there to fund them. That is operational. The same company also has A$80m on deposit chasing a high rate, which is not operational.

What you do about it: Maintain a list of operational deposit relationships, approved by the controller, with the evidence required by Att A paras 48 and 49. Review every year. Liquidity Risk challenges the list. The default for a deposit with missing evidence is non-operational.

Evidence we keep: The operational relationship file for each customer: the services agreement, the dependency assessment, and the approval.

Learn more: Document 07; Guide Ch 5 s13.4; Guide Ch 4 s4.1.

Att A para 48: Qualifying activities

What it says: Qualifying activities are clearing, custody or cash management activities where the customer relies on the ADI to perform these services as an independent third-party intermediary in order to carry out its normal banking activities over the next 30 days. The services must be provided to institutional customers under a legally binding agreement, and termination of the agreement must be subject either to a notice period of at least 30 days, or to significant switching costs borne by the customer if the operational deposits are moved before 30 days (Att A para 48).

In everyday words: There are four tests: (1) the service is clearing, custody or cash management; (2) the customer relies on the bank as an independent third party; (3) the customer is an institution, not a household; (4) there is a binding contract that either needs 30 days' notice to end or would cost the customer a lot to leave quickly. "Clearing" is settling payments or trades; "custody" is safekeeping of securities; "cash management" is running a customer's payments and collections.

Example: A pension fund uses Example Bank as custodian under a contract that needs 90 days' notice to terminate. Test 4 is met by notice. A company that can walk away from its cash management service at a day's notice and without cost fails test 4, so its balances are non-operational.

What you do about it: For each customer on the operational list, record the service type, the agreement reference, the termination terms (notice days or switching cost) and the customer type. Check that all four tests are met each time the list is reviewed.

Evidence we keep: The agreements, the extracted termination clauses, and the test record.

Learn more: Document 07; Guide Ch 5 s13.4.

Att A para 49: Qualifying operational deposits

What it says: Qualifying operational deposits generated by such an activity are those where the deposits are (Att A para 49):

  • (a) by-products of the underlying services provided by the ADI;
  • (b) not offered by the ADI in the wholesale market for the sole purpose of offering interest income; and
  • (c) held in specifically designated accounts and priced without giving an economic incentive to the customer to leave excess funds in these accounts.

In everyday words: The deposit must exist because of the service, not because the bank is chasing cash. It should sit in a designated operating account and not earn a rate designed to attract surplus cash.

Example: A custody customer's cash account pays a very low rate and exists only to settle trades. This qualifies. A cash account paying a market-leading rate that attracts the customer's spare millions does not.

What you do about it: Add a data flag "designated operational account" and check the pricing: if the account pays an above-normal rate, escalate it. Confirm with Treasury and the product owner that no operational account is marketed for interest.

Evidence we keep: The account designation record and the pricing review.

Learn more: Document 07; Guide Ch 5 s13.4.

Att A para 50: Excess balances are not operational

What it says: Any excess balances that could be withdrawn without jeopardising the clearing, custody or cash management activities do not qualify as operational deposits (Att A para 50).

In everyday words: Only the part of the balance the customer truly needs for the service counts. If a customer needs A$20m to run payroll and has A$50m in the account, A$20m is operational and A$30m is not.

Example: A customer's balance averages A$50m. The bank estimates the amount needed to operate at A$20m. Operational: A$20m; excess: A$30m, which is non-operational. If the customer is a corporate and not fully insured, operational runs off at 25% (20 x 25% = A$5.0m) and the excess at 40% (30 x 40% = A$12.0m), a total of A$17.0m. Treating all A$50m as operational would have given only A$12.5m.

What you do about it: Maintain a documented method for estimating the operational portion per customer (for example a minimum balance based on average daily payment volumes), approved by the controller and Liquidity Risk, and reviewed at least annually. Apply it to the customer's balance each day. Method choice is a bank decision (typical practice; confirm with your controller), but the result must be defensible to APRA (Att A para 32).

Evidence we keep: The method paper, the calculation per customer and the approvals.

Learn more: Document 07; Guide Ch 5 s13.4.

Liquidity facilities

Att A para 51: Credit facility versus liquidity facility

What it says: A liquidity facility is any committed, undrawn back-up facility that would be used to refinance the debt obligations of a customer in situations where the customer is unable to roll over that debt in financial markets. The amount of any commitment treated as a liquidity facility is the amount of the outstanding debt issued by the customer (or the proportionate share of a syndicated facility) maturing within a 30-day period that is backstopped by the facility. Any additional capacity of the facility is treated as a committed credit facility. General working capital facilities for corporate entities (for example revolving credit facilities for general corporate or working capital purposes) must not be classified as liquidity facilities but as credit facilities (Att A para 51).

In everyday words: A bank may promise a company: "if you cannot refinance your commercial paper (short-term IOUs) when it falls due, we will lend you the money." That promise is a liquidity facility. It is riskier for the bank, because the need for it arises exactly when markets freeze. A normal working capital line is a credit facility. Only the slice that backstops debt maturing inside 30 days is "liquidity"; the rest of the same facility is "credit". "Undrawn" means not yet used. "Committed" means the bank has a binding obligation to lend.

Example: A corporate has a committed A$100m facility, undrawn. A$30m of the corporate's commercial paper matures in 30 days and is backstopped by the facility. Liquidity facility: A$30m at 30% = A$9.0m. Remaining credit facility: A$70m at 10% = A$7.0m. Total outflow: A$16.0m. Treating it all as credit would give only A$10.0m. Example Bank's A$1.0bn of corporate liquidity facilities at 30% = A$0.30bn and A$5.0bn of corporate credit at 10% = A$0.50bn.

What you do about it: For each committed facility keep: undrawn amount, customer type, purpose (backstop for market debt, or general working capital), the maturing backstopped debt within 30 days and the syndicate share. Split each facility into liquidity and credit parts. The default for an unclear purpose is credit (never "liquidity" for general working capital), and the controller approves any liquidity classification. Credit teams own the data; Liquidity Reporting tests it each month against the facility register.

Evidence we keep: The facility register, the purpose classification, the maturing-debt data and the monthly reconciliation to the loan systems.

Learn more: Document 07; document 13 (Job aids and checklists), facility classification card; Guide Ch 5 s16.1.

Att A para 52: Facilities to hedge funds, money market funds and vehicles

What it says: Despite Att A para 51, any facilities provided to hedge funds, money market funds and special purpose funding vehicles, or other vehicles used to finance an ADI's own assets, must be captured in their entirety as a liquidity facility to a financial institution (Att A para 52).

In everyday words: For these customers, no splitting. The whole undrawn amount is a liquidity facility to a financial institution, at 100% in Table 3. A "special purpose funding vehicle" is a separate company set up to hold assets and fund them. These customers can draw all of it at once in a crisis.

Example: Example Bank's practice case includes a A$0.2bn undrawn credit facility to a hedge fund. Under this paragraph it is a liquidity facility to a financial institution at 100%: 0.2 x 100% = A$0.20bn, not 0.2 x 40% = 0.08 as a credit facility to a financial institution.

What you do about it: Add a customer type flag for hedge funds, money market funds, special purpose vehicles and bank-sponsored funding vehicles. Route all their undrawn commitments to the 100% row. Review the customer list each quarter against new facilities and with the legal team, since these structures are not always obvious from the customer name.

Evidence we keep: The vehicle list with dates added, the facility register extract and the reviewer's note.

Att A footnote 5: Netting of derivatives

What it says: Expected derivative amounts payable and receivable are taken into account on a net basis by counterparty, only where a valid master netting agreement exists. Amounts must also be net of HQLA1 and HQLA2 collateral, to the extent that this collateral is not already counted in the stock of HQLA, in line with the principle in Att A para 31 that items cannot be double counted. An ADI may net payments that are subject to industry-wide netting agreements, subject to approval from APRA. Cash flows from foreign exchange derivative transactions that involve a full exchange of principal amounts on a simultaneous basis (or within the same day) may be reflected as a net payable or receivable figure, even where those transactions are not covered by a master netting agreement (Att A footnote 5).

In everyday words: If the bank owes a counterparty A$10m on one derivative and is owed A$7m on another with the same counterparty, you may offset them (net A$3m owed) only if a legal master netting agreement says offsetting works if things go wrong. Collateral already held that is liquid (HQLA1 or HQLA2) reduces the net figure, but only if that collateral is not already counted in the HQLA stock (no double counting). Foreign exchange trades where both currencies are swapped on the same day can be shown net even without an agreement. The same netting is used for derivative inflows (Att A footnote 8).

Example: Counterparty X: payables A$10m, receivables A$7m, valid master netting agreement. Net payable A$3m. Collateral of A$1m HQLA1 cash held from X, not already in the stock: net A$2m. Outflow at 100% = A$2m. Without the agreement the outflow would be the gross A$10m, with the A$7m receivable not offset (although receivables can still be counted as inflows, subject to the cap).

What you do about it: Hold a list of counterparties with valid master netting agreements, approved by Legal and reviewed annually. Net per counterparty. Deduct HQLA1 and HQLA2 collateral only if it is excluded from the stock (cross-check with Treasury). Keep a list of any APRA approval for industry-wide netting. Apply the FX same-day netting only to qualifying trades. Example Bank's net derivative outflows are A$0.3bn in the base position.

Evidence we keep: The netting agreement register, the netting calculation by counterparty, the collateral reconciliation, and any APRA approval.

Learn more: Document 07; Guide Ch 5 s15.2; Guide Ch 4 s4.8.

What it says: An ADI may, in certain circumstances, apply to APRA for approval to use an alternative method to calculate collateral outflows due to market valuation changes on derivative transactions (Att A footnote 6).

In everyday words: The standard's default for the market valuation row is the largest 30-day net collateral flow in 24 months. A bank with a different, better method may ask APRA for permission to use it. Until APRA agrees, use the standard method.

What you do about it: Calculate the 24-month look-back method every month. The bank may only use an alternative method after written approval from APRA, which is a Treasury and Liquidity Risk matter. Liquidity Reporting would implement the method and document the date from which it applies.

Cash inflows

Att A para 54: Only performing contractual inflows

What it says: When considering available cash inflows, an ADI must include only contractual inflows from outstanding exposures that are fully performing and for which it has no reason to expect a default within the 30-day horizon. Where APRA believes an ADI relies too heavily on cash inflows from one or a limited number of wholesale counterparties, APRA may set an alternative limit, in consultation with the ADI, on the inflows that may be included in the LCR (Att A para 54).

In everyday words: Count only money that someone is legally obliged to pay you in the next 30 days and that you fully expect to arrive. A loan that is overdue, in default or likely to default contributes nothing. A cash hope (a customer who "usually pays") contributes nothing. And if you depend on a few big borrowers, APRA can cut the amount you may count.

Example: Example Bank has a loan repayment of A$0.3bn due in 10 days from a borrower that is 60 days past due on another loan. The borrower is not fully performing, so the A$0.3bn has no inflow. The practice case includes A$0.5bn of non-performing loans and the treatment is "no inflow".

What you do about it: Filter the contractual cash flow schedule to exposures flagged as performing, with no arrears and no watch-list marker indicating expected default. Take the flag from credit risk and reconcile the flagged non-performing balance to the ledger. Check the concentration of inflows by counterparty each month (top 10 counterparties as a share of total inflows; typical practice; confirm with your controller). Credit risk owns the performing flag.

Evidence we keep: The performing flag source and its date, the exclusions list, the concentration report and any APRA correspondence on inflow limits.

Secured lending, including reverse repos and securities borrowing

Att A para 55: Maturing reverse repos and securities borrowing

What it says: An ADI must assume that maturing reverse repurchase or securities borrowing agreements secured by HQLA1 will be rolled over and will not give rise to any cash inflows (zero per cent). Maturing reverse repurchase or securities borrowing agreements secured by other HQLA are modelled as cash inflows as in Table 4, because less money is lent against the collateral. Collateralised loans extended to customers for the purpose of taking leveraged trading positions are modelled with a 50 per cent cash inflow from contractual inflows made against non-HQLA1 or non-HQLA2 collateral (Att A para 55).

In everyday words: A reverse repo is where the bank lends cash and takes securities as security. If the security is government bonds (HQLA1), the bank is expected to keep rolling the loan, so no cash comes back (0%). If the security is weaker, the bank would lend less next time, so some cash comes back, with the rate rising as the collateral gets weaker (Table 4). Margin loans to customers for leveraged trading are given 50%. "Securities borrowing" is the same idea: borrowing securities against cash collateral. "Rolled over" means renewed.

Example: Example Bank has A$1.0bn of reverse repo against government bonds maturing in 10 days: inflow = 1.0 x 0% = nil. A A$1.0bn reverse repo against an equity index share (HQLA2B, other) gives 1.0 x 50% = A$0.5bn. A reverse repo against collateral that is not HQLA at all gives 1.0 x 100% = A$1.0bn.

What you do about it: For every maturing reverse repo, securities borrowing and margin loan, take the collateral class on the day (from the collateral system), the maturity and the flag for whether the collateral is re-used (Att A para 56). Apply Table 4. The wording "non-HQLA1 or non-HQLA2" in this paragraph is not precise, and Table 4 lists the row for margin lending as "backed by securities that are not HQLA1, HQLA2 or CLF-eligible securities" at 50%, so use Table 4 as the definition and note this in the methodology file. Treasury confirms collateral details.

Evidence we keep: The list of maturing reverse repos with collateral classes, the Table 4 mapping and the margin loan list.

Learn more: Document 07; document 08; Guide Ch 5 s18.2.

Att A para 56: Re-used collateral covering short positions

What it says: As an exception to Att A para 55, if collateral obtained through reverse repo, securities borrowing or collateral swaps, which matures within the 30-day horizon, is re-used (rehypothecated) and is tied up for 30 days or longer to cover short positions, an ADI must assume the reverse repo or securities borrowing arrangements will be rolled over and will not give rise to any cash inflows (zero per cent), reflecting its need to continue to cover the short position or to re-purchase the relevant securities (Att A para 56).

In everyday words: If the bank has sold securities it does not own ("a short position") and used the securities it borrowed in a reverse repo to cover the sale, it cannot let that reverse repo run off, because it would then be unable to deliver. So no inflow, whatever the collateral. The right-hand column of Table 4 shows 0% for every collateral type for this reason.

Example: A A$1.0bn reverse repo against HQLA2A bonds matures in 10 days. Normally the inflow is 15% = A$0.15bn. If the bonds are re-used to cover a short position for 30 days or more, the inflow is nil.

What you do about it: Add a rehypothecation flag and a "covers a short position for 30 days or more" flag to the collateral records. Treasury (or the trading desk) owns the flags. The Liquidity Reporting check each day is a count of maturing reverse repos with flag = yes, compared with the previous day. Example Bank has no short positions or rehypothecation (confirm with Treasury).

Evidence we keep: The flag source, the confirmation from Treasury and the Table 4 right-hand column calculation.

Att A para 58: Head office facility for a foreign ADI

What it says: APRA may allow recognition of access to head office funds via a committed funding facility if the ADI is a foreign ADI. The head office committed funding facility must meet both of these criteria: (a) it is an irrevocable commitment from the branch's head office and is appropriately documented; and (b) it is quantified. This paragraph does not apply to a foreign ADI that is part of a group that also has a locally incorporated banking subsidiary in Australia (Att A para 58).

In everyday words: A foreign bank's Australian branch may be allowed to count a binding promise from its overseas head office to supply funds, if the promise cannot be withdrawn, is documented and has a fixed amount. The branch gets no such relief if the same foreign group also owns a locally incorporated Australian bank.

What you do about it: Not applicable to Example Bank, which is locally incorporated. For awareness: a foreign ADI would need a signed, irrevocable, quantified facility and APRA's recognition before counting it.

Evidence we keep: For a foreign ADI, the facility and APRA's recognition. For Example Bank, none.

Att A para 59: The 50 per cent limit on head office funding

What it says: A foreign ADI may meet up to 50 per cent of its LCR requirement with a head office committed funding facility. The remaining liquidity requirement must be met with eligible liquid assets as detailed in Attachment A (Att A para 59).

In everyday words: Even where allowed, the head office promise can cover at most half of what the branch must hold. The other half must be real liquid assets. Foreign ADIs must hold an LCR of at least 40% (APS 210 para 55), so this paragraph limits how that 40% may be met.

Example (hypothetical foreign ADI): Net outflows are A$100m. The minimum LCR is 40%, so the requirement is A$40m of HQLA-equivalent. Up to 50% of that, A$20m, may be met by a head office facility, and at least A$20m must be HQLA. My reading is that "50 per cent of its LCR requirement" is measured against the 40% requirement; confirm with your controller for any real case.

What you do about it: Not applicable to Example Bank. For awareness only.

Evidence we keep: Not applicable to Example Bank.

Learn more: Guide Ch 5 s18.4 and s21.

Inflows by counterparty

Att A para 60: Timing of inflows

What it says: All inflows are taken only at the latest possible date, based on the contractual rights available to counterparties. Inflows from loans that have no specific maturity are not included, except for minimum payments of principal, fee or interest associated with an open maturity loan (Att A para 60).

In everyday words: When a borrower has the right to pay any time between two dates, assume they pay on the later date. If the later date is outside 30 days, you get nothing in the window. An overdraft with no maturity brings in nothing, except the minimum payment the borrower must make (the minimum repayment on a credit card, for example).

Example: A corporate loan of A$10m may be repaid by the borrower at any time up to day 45. The latest possible date is day 45, outside the window, so no inflow. A credit card book of A$2.0bn with contractual minimum monthly payments of 3% brings in 2.0 x 3% = A$0.06bn, and that is the figure to start from before applying the retail rate (Att A para 61).

What you do about it: Build the inflow schedule on the latest contractual date. For open-maturity products, use only the contractual minimum payments. The flows data comes from the loan systems; Liquidity Reporting checks that no inflow is dated by behaviour or expectation. Reconcile the cash flow schedule totals to the loan balances.

Evidence we keep: The contractual cash flow schedule, the rule for open-maturity products and the reconciliation to the loan ledger.

Learn more: Document 07; document 08; Guide Ch 5 s18.

Retail and SME inflows

Att A para 61: Retail and SME inflows at 50 per cent

What it says: An ADI is assumed to receive all fully performing contractual inflows from retail and SME customers (see Att A footnote 7). At the same time, the ADI is assumed to continue extending loans to retail and SME customers at a rate of 50 per cent of contractual inflows. This results in a net inflow rate of 50 per cent of the contractual amount (Att A para 61).

In everyday words: Borrowers pay you back; but a bank cannot stop lending, so assume it re-lends half of what it receives. Net: 50 cents in the dollar counts. Receiving 100 and lending 50 leaves 50.

Example: Example Bank's contractual loan repayments within 30 days from retail and SME customers are A$1.0bn. Inflow = 1.0 x 50% = A$0.50bn. In the practice case, retail and SME repayments of A$0.9bn give 0.9 x 50% = A$0.45bn.

What you do about it: Apply 50% to the contractual principal, interest and fee payments due within 30 days from fully performing retail and SME customers. Exclude arrears and non-performing balances (Att A para 54). Take the SME flag as in Att A para 46 and footnote 7. Check that the contractual payments reconcile to the loan system's cash flow report.

Evidence we keep: The cash flow extract by customer segment, the performing filter and the 50% calculation.

Learn more: Document 07; document 08; Guide Ch 5 s18.5.

Att A footnote 7: SME definition when liability data is missing

What it says: Where the SME definition in Att A para 46 cannot be applied because the ADI has no liability products from which to work, the SME definition in Prudential Standard APS 113 Capital Adequacy: Internal Ratings-based Approach to Credit Risk (its 40th numbered paragraph) can be used instead (Att A footnote 7).

In everyday words: The footnote gives a fall-back definition of a small business, borrowed from the capital rules, for cases where the A$2 million funding test cannot be run (for example, you are applying it to an inflow from a loan customer who has no deposit with you). It is attached to the words "retail and SME customers" in Att A para 61.

What you do about it: Where you need an SME flag for a borrower with no deposit relationship (Att A para 61 inflows), use the APS 113 definition from the credit risk or capital team, not your own. Record that the fall-back was used. This matters because retail and SME loan inflows run at 50%, while other corporate loan inflows also run at 50%, so the effect on the number is often nil, but the classification must still be defensible.

Evidence we keep: The APS 113 definition as applied by credit risk and the list of customers classified under it.

Learn more: Att A para 61; document 07; Guide Ch 5 s18.5.

Other wholesale inflows

Att A para 62: Wholesale inflows

What it says: An ADI is assumed to receive all fully performing contractual wholesale cash inflows. In addition, an ADI is assumed to continue extending loans to wholesale clients at a rate of zero per cent of inflows for financial institutions and central banks, and 50 per cent for all others, including non-financial corporates, sovereigns, PSEs and MDBs. This results in an inflow rate of (Att A para 62):

  • (a) 100 per cent from financial institution and central bank counterparties; and
  • (b) 50 per cent for other entities.

Inflows from maturing securities not included in the stock of HQLA receive an inflow rate of 100 per cent.

In everyday words: The bank assumes it keeps lending to ordinary companies and governments (re-lending half), so only 50% of what they repay counts. But it assumes it will not re-lend to other banks and central banks, so 100% of what those repay counts. A bond the bank owns that is not in the HQLA stock and matures within 30 days brings in 100% of its redemption value.

Example: Example Bank: corporate repayments within 30 days of A$0.8bn x 50% = 0.40; loans to banks maturing within 30 days A$1.0bn x 100% = 1.00. In the practice case, unsecured loans to other banks maturing in 20 days (A$1.5bn) give 1.5 x 100% = A$1.50bn. A non-HQLA bond (A$2.5bn of senior bank bonds) maturing in 3 years gives no inflow, because it matures outside the window.

What you do about it: Classify each counterparty as financial institution or central bank (100%) versus other (50%), using the financial institution definition in APS 210 para 10(b) (which includes money market corporations, finance companies, friendly societies, and trustees of superannuation funds, public unit trusts, mutual funds and cash management trusts). Include maturing securities that are not in the HQLA stock at 100%, and check against the HQLA list (Att A para 31). Reconcile to the treasury and loan cash flow reports.

Evidence we keep: The counterparty classification table, the inflow schedule by type and the HQLA cross-check.

Learn more: Document 07; document 08; Guide Ch 5 s18.6.

Operational deposits

Att A para 63: Operational deposits at other institutions

What it says: A zero per cent inflow rate applies to deposits held at other financial institutions for operational purposes (Att A para 63).

In everyday words: Cash the bank keeps at another institution for payments and settlement (a nostro account, say) is needed to keep operating and cannot be assumed to come back, so there is no inflow.

Example: Example Bank has A$0.3bn in operating accounts at a correspondent bank. Inflow nil, even if the funds are "at call".

What you do about it: Identify operational balances held at other institutions (Treasury and Payments own the list) and map them to 0%. Do not mix them with non-operational placements with banks, which receive 100% (Att A para 62).

Evidence we keep: The list of operational accounts held with other institutions and their purpose.

Learn more: Document 07; Guide Ch 5 s18.7.

Att A para 64: Cooperative banking networks

What it says: Likewise, the depositing ADI must not count any inflow for deposits held at the centralised institution in a cooperative banking network, which are assumed to stay at the centralised institution. These funds receive a zero per cent inflow rate (Att A para 64).

In everyday words: In a cooperative network, small member institutions place their liquidity with a central body. That money is assumed to stay there, so no inflow for the depositing member.

What you do about it: No action for Example Bank, which is not part of a cooperative network. A member of such a network would map its central-institution deposits to 0%.

Evidence we keep: A statement in the methodology that the bank has no such deposits.

Learn more: Guide Ch 5 s18.7.

Other cash inflows

Derivatives cash inflows

Att A para 65: Derivative inflows

What it says: Derivative cash inflows receive an inflow rate of 100 per cent (Att A para 65). The treatment in footnote 5 applies here too (Att A footnote 8).

In everyday words: Net amounts the bank expects to receive on derivatives within 30 days count in full, after offsetting against what it owes under the netting rules.

Example: In the practice case, net derivative cash inflows within 30 days of A$0.1bn count at 100% = A$0.10bn. Outflows on the same contracts (A$0.4bn) are on the outflow side. If both sides are on the same counterparty and a valid master netting agreement exists, they would be netted first and only the net shows on one side.

What you do about it: Calculate derivative inflows by counterparty with the same netting as outflows (footnote 5), and make sure the same contract is not counted both in the outflow and inflow lines. These inflows count towards the inflow cap (Att A para 30). The derivatives data come from the treasury system; the netting list comes from Legal.

Evidence we keep: The netting calculation, the counterparty list and the reconciliation to the treasury system.

Learn more: Document 07; document 08; Guide Ch 5 s18.8.

Att A footnote 8: Netting also applies to inflows

What it says: The treatment in footnote 5 also applies here (Att A footnote 8).

In everyday words: The netting rules for derivative outflows (valid master netting agreement, net of liquid collateral not already counted, same-day foreign exchange netting) apply equally to inflows.

What you do about it: Use the same netting engine and counterparty list for inflows and outflows so that the two sides are consistent.

Evidence we keep: As for footnote 5.

Learn more: Att A footnote 5; Guide Ch 5 s15.2 and s18.8.

Other contractual cash inflows

Att A para 66: Other contractual inflows

What it says: Other contractual cash inflows receive an inflow rate of 100 per cent. Cash inflows related to non-financial revenues are not taken into account in the calculation of net cash outflows for the LCR (Att A para 66).

In everyday words: Any other legally due receipt (a settlement amount owed to the bank, say) counts in full. But the bank's income from selling goods or services outside banking, such as rent or non-financial fees that are not contractual receivables from an exposure, does not count.

Example: A counterparty owes the bank A$0.05bn under a contract, due in 12 days: inflow 0.05 x 100% = A$0.05bn. Expected fee income from the business's ordinary trading is not counted.

What you do about it: Include other contractual receipts only where a legal contract fixes the amount and date. Exclude forecast revenue. Do not double count (Att A para 31).

Evidence we keep: The contract list and the exclusion note.

Learn more: Document 07; Guide Ch 5 s18.8.
