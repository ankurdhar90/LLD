17 APG 210 companion: APRA's practice guide

Purpose: To let you open APRA's Prudential Practice Guide APG 210 Liquidity at any numbered paragraph and find, straight away, what it says, what it means in plain words, what the Liquidity Reporting team does about it and what evidence we keep.

You will be able to:

  • Explain what a practice guide is and why it carries less legal weight than a standard.
  • Find the training entry for any of the 167 numbered paragraphs of APG 210 and the APS 210 paragraph it supports.
  • Say which guidance changes how the team classifies deposits, values liquid assets, builds stress tests and prepares for an emergency.
  • Use the table at the end to check the 25 most practical points against your own working papers.

Before you start: Read document 02 (Liquidity from zero), document 04 (APS 210 companion: the main body) and document 05 (APS 210 companion: Attachment A). This document is a look-up, not a story.

Time: About 3 hours to read once; after that, use it as a reference beside the guide.

What a practice guide is, and its legal status

APRA is the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority, the regulator of banks. It writes two kinds of document about liquidity.

  • A prudential standard, here APS 210 Liquidity. It sets rules. APS 210 says it is made under section 11AF of the Banking Act 1959 (APS 210 para 1) and that it applies to all authorised deposit-taking institutions, or ADIs (APS 210 para 2). Banks must comply with it.
  • A prudential practice guide (PPG), here APG 210. It gives APRA's view of sound practice. The guide itself says PPGs often discuss legal requirements but "do not themselves create enforceable requirements" [S1]. It says it aims to help ADIs comply with APS 210 and to outline prudent practice, and that not every practice will suit every ADI, depending on size, complexity and risk profile [S1].

The guide's disclaimer adds that it is not legal advice and that APRA disclaims liability for loss from using it [S1].

In everyday words: the standard says what you must do. The guide says how APRA expects a sensible bank to go about it. The guide uses words such as "would", "generally" and "good practice"; it also repeats some real requirements ("APS 210 requires..."). When it quotes a requirement, the requirement comes from the standard, not the guide.

From the controller: Treat the guide as the way APRA examiners will read your work. Nobody is penalised for a guide paragraph as such, but if we depart from it we need a written reason that a supervisor would accept. Record that reason.

Unverified: What APRA may do if a bank departs from guidance (for example, supervisory action or a higher requirement) is general understanding only. I did not find a statement of it in the sources read. Confirm with your controller or on apra.gov.au.

How to read an entry

Every entry has five parts: What it says (a plain restatement, naming the APS 210 paragraph it supports), In everyday words, What you do about it, Evidence we keep, and Learn more (documents in this pack and chapters of the knowledge guide, file LIQUIDITY_GUIDE.md). Headings copy the guide's own section headings, with the chapter in front. The guide's footnotes are covered inside the paragraphs they belong to.

Chapter 1: Liquidity risk appetite and tolerance

APG 210 para 1: Liquidity risk tolerance

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 21 and para 16(a). The APG repeats the standard: a bank's liquidity risk tolerance is the level of liquidity risk it is willing to take, it is set by the Board, documented, and suited to the bank's operations, strategy and role in the financial system (APG 210 para 1).

In everyday words: "Tolerance" is the most risk the Board will accept. Think of a speed limit the Board chooses for the bank.

What you do about it: You do not set the tolerance. You produce the LCR and NSFR that are measured against it. Know where the Board statement is kept and what the limits are, so you can tell your controller the day a number gets close.

Evidence we keep: The current Board-approved liquidity risk tolerance statement; the version in force on each reporting date.

Learn more: Documents 03, 04, 10; Guide Ch 8.

APG 210 para 2: Risk appetite statement

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 16(a) and para 21. The Board-approved risk appetite statement normally contains the liquidity risk appetite and names the liquidity risk tolerance (APG 210 para 2).

In everyday words: Risk appetite is the bank's overall attitude to risk, written down by the Board. The liquidity part sits inside it.

What you do about it: Check that the limits you report against (for Example Bank, LCR at least 120% and NSFR at least 110%, an Example Bank convention) match the current statement. Raise any mismatch.

Evidence we keep: Copy of the risk appetite statement used to set report thresholds; change log when it is updated.

Learn more: Documents 03, 10, 12; Guide Ch 8.

APG 210 para 3: Appetite versus tolerance

What it says: Supports APS 210 paras 21 to 24. Risk appetite is the nature and level of risk that is acceptable in pursuing strategy, and not all of it can be put in numbers. Risk tolerance is the numeric maximum acceptable risk after allowing for mitigants and controls (APG 210 para 3).

In everyday words: Appetite is the attitude ("we are careful with funding"). Tolerance is the number ("LCR never below 120%").

What you do about it: When you build a report, the tolerance column is the one with numbers. Make sure each tolerance you show has a number and a source.

Evidence we keep: Limit table in the risk appetite or limits framework, with approval date.

Learn more: Documents 10, 12; Guide Ch 8.

APG 210 para 4: Tolerance and business plans

What it says: Supports APS 210 paras 21 to 24 and para 31. The bank should consider its business objectives, strategic direction and budget outcomes. The Board's tolerance decides which risks, including liquidity risk, are acceptable in planned activities. It usually reflects financial condition, funding capacity and role in the financial system (APG 210 para 4).

In everyday words: If the business plans to grow loans fast, the Board's tolerance decides whether the funding to support it is acceptable.

What you do about it: When you forecast the LCR and NSFR for planning, use the plan's balance sheet. Flag where the plan would push you toward a limit.

Evidence we keep: Forecast working papers tied to the approved plan; the minutes where the tolerance was considered.

Learn more: Documents 08, 09, 10; Guide Ch 8.

APG 210 para 5: Explicit tolerance with sensitivity analysis

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 21 and para 16(a), and the stress testing paras 62 to 65. The tolerance must be set and documented. APRA expects it to be explicit, comprehensive and designed around the bank's own vulnerabilities, and says it is required to be subject to sensitivity analysis (APG 210 para 5).

In everyday words: "Sensitivity analysis" asks: if one assumption changes, how much does the answer move?

What you do about it: Support Liquidity Risk by running the LCR with changed assumptions (for example a higher run-off rate) when asked. Keep the inputs and outputs.

Evidence we keep: Sensitivity runs with date, assumptions changed, results, requester.

Learn more: Documents 10, 22; Guide Ch 8.

Interpretation: I could not find the words "sensitivity analysis" in APS 210 paras 21 to 24. The APG states it as a requirement; the nearest rule in the standard is that stress results must be considered in setting limits (APS 210 para 65). Confirm with your controller how the bank evidences it.

APG 210 para 6: Limits make tolerance measurable

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 37. Tolerance would generally be expressed as measurable limits so that monitoring is clear and transparent and the bank can show it stays within tolerance (APG 210 para 6).

In everyday words: A limit turns "do not take too much risk" into a number someone can check each day.

What you do about it: Compare each day's and month's results with every limit on the limits list, record the headroom, and report breaches the same day. Never adjust an input to stay under a limit.

Evidence we keep: Daily limit monitoring log; breach reports and actions taken.

Learn more: Documents 08, 10, 12; Guide Ch 8.

APG 210 para 7: What tolerances should cover

What it says: Supports APS 210 paras 21, 29, 37 and 43. Good practice is tolerances for: quality and diversification of liquid asset portfolios (by instrument and counterparty); liability diversification (by market, product, counterparty, maturity); reliance on offshore short and long-term funding; overall maturity mismatch; liquidity risk across borders and legal entities; currency mismatch, including derivative cash flow mismatches tied to offshore funding; and contingent liquidity exposures whether or not they fall in the 30-day LCR window (APG 210 para 7).

In everyday words: The LCR alone is not enough. APRA suggests limits for the make-up of your liquid assets, your funding sources, maturity gaps, foreign currency, and promises that might be drawn after 30 days.

What you do about it: Supply the data behind each limit in the list: asset concentration, funding concentration, maturity ladder, currency position, off-balance sheet commitments beyond 30 days. Check each exists in your monitoring pack.

Evidence we keep: Limit-by-limit data extract and the monitoring pack that shows it.

Learn more: Document 10; Guide Ch 8.

Typical practice: Banks usually write one limit per bullet above. Example Bank's actual limit list is a convention; confirm with your controller.

Chapter 1: Future cash flows of assets and liabilities

APG 210 para 8: Identify, measure, monitor and control

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36 and para 43. A bank must identify, measure, monitor and control liquidity risk positions for future cash flows of assets and liabilities, contingent demands from off-balance sheet positions, in all currencies in which it is active, and any correspondent, custody and settlement activities (APG 210 para 8).

In everyday words: Know every way cash can leave or arrive, in every currency, including the payment services you provide to others.

What you do about it: Make sure the cash flow data you use covers all products, off-balance sheet items and currencies. Report any product or currency missing from the feed.

Evidence we keep: Data coverage reconciliation to the general ledger; list of products and currencies in scope.

Learn more: Documents 07, 10; Guide Ch 3, Ch 8.

APG 210 para 9: Prospective cash flows over time horizons

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36 and paras 62 to 63. A bank is generally expected to estimate future cash flows for assets, liabilities and off-balance sheet commitments (including derivatives) over several time horizons, in normal conditions and a range of stress scenarios, including severe systemic and bank-specific stress. This helps it meet needs without hurting daily operations or financial condition (APG 210 para 9).

In everyday words: Forecast the money coming in and going out, for days, weeks and months ahead, both on a normal day and on a very bad one.

What you do about it: Produce or feed the maturity ladder and the forward-looking LCR. Make sure derivatives and commitments are in them.

Evidence we keep: Maturity ladder and forecast outputs with dates; scenario definitions.

Learn more: Documents 08, 10, 22; Guide Ch 8.

APG 210 para 10: Wholesale funding dependence

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36 and para 29(b). APRA envisages that a bank assesses its total wholesale funding, the capacity of those markets, and its own contribution to the overall funding position (APG 210 para 10).

In everyday words: Work out how much you borrow from big institutions and whether those markets can really supply it if needed.

What you do about it: Report total wholesale funding by type and maturity each month, with the change since last month.

Evidence we keep: Wholesale funding report with source extracts.

Learn more: Documents 07, 10; Guide Ch 4.

APG 210 para 11: Granularity of forecasts

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36. Cash flow forecasts should be granular enough to capture all material sources of liquidity risk, and assumptions about short and long-term needs should reflect the complexity of the bank's businesses and products (APG 210 para 11).

In everyday words: A forecast that lumps everything together hides risk. Break it down until the big risks show.

What you do about it: When a product is lumped into "other", ask if it is material. Propose a separate line when it is.

Evidence we keep: Forecast methodology note; list of cash flow categories and their materiality test.

Learn more: Documents 07, 10, 22; Guide Ch 8.

APG 210 para 12: Collateral quality analysis

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 38. A bank would generally analyse the quality of assets that could be used as collateral, to judge whether they could be used in secured funding in stress (APG 210 para 12).

In everyday words: Which of your assets could you pledge to borrow against in a crisis, and would lenders accept them?

What you do about it: Keep an up-to-date list of unencumbered assets by type, with the RBA or market eligibility flag, as feed for Treasury.

Evidence we keep: Unencumbered asset report with eligibility flags and valuation date.

Learn more: Documents 07, 10; Guide Ch 4, Ch 9.

APG 210 para 13: Concentration of expected inflows

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36 and Att A paras 54 to 62 (inflows). Monitoring should include how concentrated expected inflows are across counterparties, so the bank is not overly dependent on one or a few paying on time (APG 210 para 13).

In everyday words: Do not rely on one customer's repayment to make your own numbers work.

What you do about it: Add a report of the largest contractual inflows within 30 days by counterparty. Highlight any large one.

Evidence we keep: Inflow concentration report with date and review sign-off.

Learn more: Documents 08, 10; Guide Ch 5.

APG 210 para 14: Propensity to withdraw

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36 and Att A paras 34 to 53. In estimating liability cash flows, the bank should assess how likely liabilities are to be withdrawn in stress. A bank relying heavily on wholesale funding should assess rollover likelihood and how fund providers' behaviour could change (APG 210 para 14).

In everyday words: Ask who could take their money back and how quickly, then guess how they would behave when scared.

What you do about it: Know which of our funding is wholesale and when each piece matures. Tell your controller when a large roll-over date enters the 30-day window.

Evidence we keep: Wholesale maturity profile; roll-over watch list.

Learn more: Documents 08, 10, 22; Guide Ch 5.

APG 210 para 15: Facilities that support other entities' funding

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36 and Att A paras 51 to 52. A bank would generally assess potential draws on facilities it gives to support term funding programs of other entities and when they can be used (APG 210 para 15).

In everyday words: If you promised a backup loan to a borrower's bond program, you must plan for being asked to lend.

What you do about it: Check facilities are correctly tagged (liquidity versus credit) and counterparty type. Facilities to hedge funds, money market funds and securitisation vehicles count in full (Att A para 52).

Evidence we keep: Facility register with classification, counterparty type and undrawn amount, reconciled to the ledger.

Learn more: Documents 05, 07; Guide Ch 5.

APG 210 para 16: Stickiness of retail deposits

What it says: Supports Att A paras 36 to 40 and APS 210 para 36. In forecasting retail deposits, the bank might consider factors affecting stickiness, such as size, interest-rate sensitivity and the deposit channel (direct, internet or broker) (APG 210 para 16).

In everyday words: "Sticky" deposits stay put. Big, rate-chasing, online or broker-sourced deposits are less sticky.

What you do about it: Make sure the deposit data carries balance, rate and channel fields so the scorecard and behaviour models can use them.

Evidence we keep: Data dictionary showing fields; sample of customer-level data checks.

Learn more: Documents 07, 22; Guide Ch 5.

APG 210 para 17: Deposit insurance across jurisdictions

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36 and para 43. Internationally active banks would consider differences in deposit insurance across jurisdictions, and how the speed and manner of payout and resolution of problem banks affect retail depositor behaviour in stress (APG 210 para 17).

In everyday words: If a country pays depositors out slowly, people there may run sooner.

What you do about it: Example Bank has one entity and one currency, so no action today. If the bank goes offshore, ask which guarantee limit applies in each place; the scorecard needs it.

Evidence we keep: Not applicable now; a jurisdiction table would be kept if the bank operated offshore.

Learn more: Documents 05, 21; Guide Ch 2.

APG 210 para 18: Limits of historical deposit data

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36 and paras 62 to 65. Historical analysis of deposit loss is informative but may not be a solid base for withdrawal assumptions, because Australian data is unlikely to show a stress as severe as prudent management requires (APG 210 para 18).

In everyday words: The past in Australia has been calm. Do not assume a crisis would be as gentle.

What you do about it: When someone proposes a lower run-off from history, remember it cannot replace the prescribed LCR rates. Use it only for internal stress work.

Evidence we keep: Behavioural model documentation stating its limits.

Learn more: Documents 10, 22; Guide Ch 5.

Chapter 1: Contingent liquidity demand from off-balance sheet positions

APG 210 para 19: Projecting draws on commitments

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36 and Att A Table 3. A bank is expected to project the effects of undrawn and other off-balance sheet commitments being drawn, considering the nature of the commitment, the counterparty's creditworthiness and sector and geographic exposures, since same-sector counterparties may suffer stress together (APG 210 para 19).

In everyday words: Promises to lend can all be called at once, especially within one industry in trouble.

What you do about it: Report undrawn commitments by type, counterparty and sector. The LCR rates are fixed in Att A Table 3; this is extra monitoring.

Evidence we keep: Commitments report by sector and type, reconciled to the ledger.

Learn more: Documents 05, 07, 10; Guide Ch 5.

APG 210 para 20: Non-contractual support

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36 and para 5. Processes should consider potential non-contractual obligations, because a bank can end up supporting off-balance sheet vehicles in stress. Reputation can add liquidity risk, for example supporting funds it manages or accepting early redemption of products it sponsored (APG 210 para 20).

In everyday words: You may feel bound to help even when the contract does not say so.

What you do about it: Tell Liquidity Risk of any affiliated fund or vehicle you see in the data. Include them on the list of items needing stress treatment.

Evidence we keep: Register of affiliated vehicles and sponsored products.

Learn more: Documents 10, 22; Guide Ch 5.

APG 210 para 21: Identifying trigger events

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36. Contracts behind undrawn commitments are often customised, so trigger events can be hard to identify. The business that created the risk and the liquidity risk function must build systems to analyse triggers and how risk factors would cause outflows, even without a history of such flows, including the behaviour of the bank and its counterparties (APG 210 para 21).

In everyday words: Read the contracts for the "if this happens, you must pay" clauses, and test them even if they never fired before.

What you do about it: Maintain the trigger list (rating triggers, material adverse change, margin calls) with the data needed to value each. Keep it current when new contracts are signed.

Evidence we keep: Trigger register with contract references, owner and last review date.

Learn more: Documents 07, 10; Guide Ch 5.

APG 210 para 22: Items of particular importance

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36. Special purpose vehicles (SPVs), financial derivatives, and guarantees and commitments are singled out because they are common and hard to assess (APG 210 para 22).

In everyday words: Three places where hidden cash demands usually sit.

What you do about it: Check these three get extra review each month in the monitoring pack.

Evidence we keep: Monitoring pack sections for each item.

Learn more: Documents 10, 21; Guide Ch 5.

Chapter 1: Special purpose vehicles

APG 210 para 23: Liquidity facilities to SPVs

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36, para 5 and Att A para 52. A bank should understand its contingent liquidity exposure and triggers from contractual and non-contractual SPV relationships. Where it provides liquidity facilities to an SPV or would support it under stress, it may need to monitor the SPV's inflows and outflows, including in stress tests, and assess its position with the SPV's liquidity draws but not its surplus (APG 210 para 23).

In everyday words: Count the SPV's shortfalls against you, but do not count its spare cash in your favour.

What you do about it: If the bank has SPVs, include their maturing assets and liabilities in the LCR as APS 210 para 5 requires. Never net an SPV surplus against a deficit elsewhere.

Evidence we keep: SPV cash flow table with inclusion basis noted.

Learn more: Documents 05, 21; Guide Ch 5.

APG 210 para 24: Securitisation as a funding source

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 26 and paras 62 to 63. Where securitisation SPVs fund the bank, it should consider whether they would keep working in adverse scenarios, noting experience suggests they might not. A bank in stress often loses access to the securitisation market, and this should show in the risk framework (APG 210 para 24).

In everyday words: Do not assume you can sell more loan packages in a crisis.

What you do about it: Make sure forecasts and stress tests do not assume new securitisation issuance in stress.

Evidence we keep: Stress test assumptions showing no securitisation issuance, or a documented reason.

Learn more: Documents 10, 22; Guide Ch 4.

Chapter 1: Financial derivatives

APG 210 para 25: Derivative cash flows and collateral

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36 and Att A Table 3. A bank would generally include cash flows from repricing, exercise or maturity of derivatives, including counterparties demanding more collateral if the bank's rating or creditworthiness falls or the derivative's value changes materially (APG 210 para 25).

In everyday words: A derivative can cost cash later, either on its due date or when the other side asks for more security.

What you do about it: Supply net derivative flows by netting set and the downgrade collateral amount for the LCR (Att A Table 3 and footnote 5).

Evidence we keep: Derivative cash flow extract by netting set; downgrade trigger calculation.

Learn more: Documents 05, 07, 08, 21; Guide Ch 4, Ch 5.

Chapter 1: Guarantees and commitments

APG 210 para 26: Undrawn commitments, guarantees and letters of credit

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36 and Att A Table 3. Undrawn loan commitments, letters of credit and financial guarantees can be a significant drain. A bank should ascertain expected outflows in normal conditions and estimate how much they could increase in stress (APG 210 para 26).

In everyday words: Work out how much these promises cost on a normal day and on a bad day.

What you do about it: Keep the history of drawdowns for each product so the normal-day level can be shown.

Evidence we keep: Drawdown history by product; basis for the stress uplift.

Learn more: Documents 05, 07, 10; Guide Ch 5.

APG 210 para 27: Reliance on guarantees from others

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36. Liquidity problems can arise when the bank relies on credit lines or guarantees from others, for example holding assets backed by a third party guarantee, or raising funds against them, if the guarantor's credit is closely tied to the assets (APG 210 para 27).

In everyday words: If your safety net is held by someone who falls when your assets fall, it is not safe.

What you do about it: Identify assets supported by third-party guarantees and flag where guarantor and underlying credit move together.

Evidence we keep: Register of third-party guaranteed assets and lines relied on.

Learn more: Documents 10; Guide Ch 4.

Chapter 1: Currencies in which an ADI is active

APG 210 para 28: Liquid assets matched to currency needs

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 43(a). Effective management for a bank in several currencies keeps liquid assets consistent with how its liquidity needs are spread across currencies (APG 210 para 28).

In everyday words: Hold your safety cushion in the currencies you might need to pay.

What you do about it: Produce LCR results by currency where the bank has material foreign currency. Example Bank has one currency, so the AUD LCR equals the all-currencies LCR.

Evidence we keep: Currency breakdown of HQLA and net outflows.

Learn more: Documents 05, 10, 21; Guide Ch 5.

APG 210 para 29: Operational limits offshore

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 43(c). A bank active in several offshore places would generally consider operational limits on liquidating assets in stress. For example, if offshore liquid assets are mostly US government debt used for outflows in many currencies and places, time zones and currency conversion risks must be considered in the liquidation strategy (APG 210 para 29).

In everyday words: Selling a bond is useless if the market is shut when you need the cash, or if you must convert currency first.

What you do about it: No action for Example Bank today. If offshore assets exist, record where each is held, its market and its settlement time.

Evidence we keep: Asset location and settlement cut-off table, if relevant.

Learn more: Documents 10, 21; Guide Ch 9.

APG 210 para 30: Assessing needs in all currencies

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 43(b) and (c). A bank would generally assess liquidity needs in all currencies of its business and set acceptable currency mismatches. It could analyse each significantly active currency separately, considering constraints in stress (APG 210 para 30).

In everyday words: Check each currency on its own, not only the total.

What you do about it: Report net funding gap by currency monthly if more than one currency is material.

Evidence we keep: Per-currency gap report with approval of mismatch limits.

Learn more: Documents 10, 21; Guide Ch 5.

APG 210 para 31: Sizing foreign currency mismatches

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 43(b). The size of a mismatch should consider the ability to raise foreign currency funds, the ability to transfer surplus between currencies, jurisdictions and legal entities, and the likely convertibility of currencies, including closure of markets for some currency pairs (APG 210 para 31).

In everyday words: A gap is only safe if you can really fill it: raise the money, move it, or swap it.

What you do about it: Keep the FX swap and market access evidence that Treasury relies on, with dates of last use.

Evidence we keep: Treasury's evidence of access to FX and funding markets.

Learn more: Documents 10, 21; Guide Ch 9.

APG 210 para 32: Foreign currency funding of domestic assets

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 43 and para 36. A bank needs to be aware of and manage risks from funding domestic currency assets with foreign currency and the reverse, and consider sudden exchange rate or market liquidity changes that could break hedges (APG 210 para 32).

In everyday words: If you fund Australian dollar loans with US dollars, you depend on swaps working.

What you do about it: Know which swaps hedge which funding. Show hedges separately in the maturity ladder.

Evidence we keep: Hedge register linking swaps to funding.

Learn more: Documents 10, 21; Guide Ch 4.

Chapter 1: Correspondent, custody and settlement activities

APG 210 para 33: Correspondent, custody and settlement services

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36 and para 41. Management should understand and manage how correspondent, custodian and settlement bank services affect cash flows. Gross payment flows can be huge, so unexpected changes may cause large net flows, intraday and overnight. They should also understand liquidity needs from failure-to-settle procedures of systems in which the bank is a direct participant (APG 210 para 33).

In everyday words: If you move other people's money, small surprises can become big cash swings.

What you do about it: If the bank offers these services, supply daily balance and flow data by customer for monitoring. Example Bank's position is a convention to confirm.

Evidence we keep: Settlement balance and flow reports; participant rule summaries.

Learn more: Documents 10, 20; Guide Ch 9.

Chapter 1: Measurement tools

APG 210 para 34: Sound measurement processes

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36. APS 210 requires sound processes for measuring liquidity risk, and the bank would be expected to use a range of customised measurement tools (APG 210 para 34).

In everyday words: One number is never enough. Use several tools that fit your own bank.

What you do about it: Know the tool list (LCR, NSFR, maturity ladder, concentration reports, early warning indicators) and which you produce.

Evidence we keep: Inventory of measurement tools with owner and frequency.

Learn more: Documents 08, 09, 10; Guide Ch 8.

APG 210 para 35: Normal and stressed measures

What it says: Supports APS 210 paras 36, 37 and 39. Tools should cover vulnerabilities in normal and stressed conditions over various time horizons. In normal conditions, prospective measures identify needs from projected outflows against routine funding. In stress, they identify funding gaps at various horizons and form the basis for limits and early warning indicators (APG 210 para 35).

In everyday words: Measure on a quiet day and on a stormy day, over short and long periods, and use the results to set limits and alarms.

What you do about it: Keep the forward-looking views (the LCR projected 30 to 90 days and the ladder) current and feed them to limit reviews.

Evidence we keep: Forward-looking LCR and ladder outputs with dates.

Learn more: Documents 08, 10; Guide Ch 8.

APG 210 para 36: Pro-forma cash flow projections

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36. Pro-forma cash flow projections are a critical tool. They produce a cash flow mismatch (liquidity gap) analysis based on assumptions about how assets, liabilities and off-balance sheet items behave, and can calculate cumulative net cash flows over a time frame, in incremental periods, including projected and contingent flows (APG 210 para 36).

In everyday words: A day-by-day table of expected money in and out, adding up as it goes, shows where the gaps open.

What you do about it: Build or check the contractual ladder from the ledger each month, then the behavioural version with the assumptions documented.

Evidence we keep: Ladder with assumptions sheet and reviewer sign-off.

Learn more: Documents 10, 22; Guide Ch 8.

APG 210 para 37: Uncertain cash flows

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 36. For items with uncertain cash flows, assumptions about stress behaviour matter most. Assumptions about the market liquidity of such positions should be adjusted for market conditions or bank-specific circumstances (APG 210 para 37).

In everyday words: Where nobody knows the timing, the guess you choose matters most, and it must change when markets change.

What you do about it: Keep a list of items where you assume timing (no maturity date, options, early repayments) and the assumption used. Update after market events.

Evidence we keep: Assumption register with approval dates.

Learn more: Documents 07, 10, 22; Guide Ch 5.

APG 210 para 38: Early warning indicators

What it says: Supports APS 210 para 39 and para 33. Early warning indicators can be qualitative or quantitative and could include: rapid asset growth funded by less stable liabilities; growing concentrations; slowing deposit growth, unusual deposit loss or more notices on minimum notice deposits; rising currency mismatches; shorter average maturity of liabilities; inability to issue debt or demand only for shorter terms; repeated limit approaches or breaches; negative product trends such as rising delinquencies; weaker earnings, asset quality or financial condition; negative publicity; credit rating downgrade or negative outlook; material share price falls; rising wholesale or retail funding costs or credit-default-swap spreads; requests for more collateral or reluctance to trade; cut or removal of correspondent credit lines; and rising early redemptions of certificates of deposit or other debt (APG 210 para 38).

In everyday words: These are warning lights on the dashboard: any one may mean trouble is coming.

The bank determines its method for finding excess balances, considering that wholesale customers often hold above-average balances before specific payments, and using indicators such as ratios of balances to payment or settlement volumes or to assets under custody (APG 210 para 136).

In everyday words: Compare balance to activity: a customer with big balances and small payments has excess cash.

What you do about it: Document the ratio method and get it approved. Run it monthly.

Evidence we keep: Methodology document and monthly results.

Learn more: Documents 05, 07, 22; Guide Ch 5.

APG 210 para 137: Historical balances

What it says: Supports Att A para 50. The assessment could include historical average and minimum balances to estimate a threshold below which balances are operational (APG 210 para 137).

In everyday words: The lowest the balance usually goes is a fair measure of what is really needed.

What you do about it: Hold the history by account so the minimum can be found.

Evidence we keep: Balance history extracts.

Learn more: Documents 07, 22; Guide Ch 5.

APG 210 para 138: Accounts paying higher interest elsewhere

What it says: Supports Att A paras 49(c) and 50. Where the customer can readily move funds to accounts paying materially higher interest, the operational accounts could be considered to have no excess balance. For operational accounts paying interest, or where no ready alternative exists, APRA expects robust analysis of excess and operational balances (APG 210 para 138).

In everyday words: If it is easy to move spare money to a better account, any spare money would already have moved, so what remains is operational.

What you do about it: Ask Treasury whether accounts have easy alternatives. Keep the analysis for interest-bearing accounts.

Evidence we keep: Analysis papers.

Learn more: Documents 05, 07; Guide Ch 5.

APG 210 para 139: Clearing, custody and cash management

What it says: Supports Att A para 48. APS 210 allows only clearing, custody or cash management relationships. The APG describes each. Clearing: a service enabling customers to move funds or securities indirectly through direct participants in domestic settlement systems, limited to transmitting, reconciling and confirming payment orders; daylight overdraft, overnight financing and post-settlement balances; and intraday and final settlement positions. Custody: safekeeping, reporting or processing of assets and related operational and administrative elements, limited to settling securities transactions, contractual payments, collateral processing and custody cash management, plus dividends and income and client subscriptions and redemptions, and extending to trust, treasury, escrow, funds transfer, stock transfer and agency services, including payment and settlement (excluding correspondent banking) and depository receipts. Cash management: services for managing cash flows, assets and liabilities and the transactions needed for operations, limited to payment remittance, collection and aggregation of funds, payroll administration and control over disbursement (APG 210 para 139).

In everyday words: Only three kinds of service count, and only the listed activities within them.

What you do about it: Map each operational customer's service to one of the three. If it fits none, it is not operational.

Evidence we keep: Service mapping per customer.

Learn more: Documents 05, 07; Guide Ch 5.

APG 210 para 140: Excluded relationships

What it says: Supports Att A paras 47 and 48. Correspondent banking and prime brokerage do not meet the criteria. Correspondent banking is one bank holding deposits owned by other banks to settle foreign currency transactions (for example nostro and vostro accounts). Prime brokerage is a package for large active investors, particularly hedge funds: clearing, settlement and custody; consolidated reporting; financing; securities lending; capital introduction; and risk analytics (APG 210 para 140).

In everyday words: Accounts of other banks and hedge fund packages are never operational.

What you do about it: Exclude correspondent and prime broker accounts from operational treatment.

Evidence we keep: Exclusion rule.

Chapter 2: Derivative transactions - collateral

APG 210 para 141: Downgrade triggers

What it says: Supports Att A para 53 and Table 3. Contracts often require extra collateral, drawing contingent facilities or early repayment if the bank is downgraded. For each contract with a downgrade trigger, the LCR assumes 100 per cent of the additional collateral or cash outflow up to and including a three-notch downgrade of the long-term rating. Short-term rating triggers are taken to trigger at the corresponding long-term rating under published criteria. The bank considers all types of margin collateral and triggers that change rehypothecation rights for non-segregated collateral (APG 210 para 141).

In everyday words: Add up everything you would have to pay or post if the bank dropped three rating steps.

What you do about it: Hold a register of contracts with rating triggers, map short-term triggers to long-term ratings, and update the total monthly. For Example Bank the 3-notch collateral is A$0.2bn in the base case (Example Bank figure).

Evidence we keep: Trigger register and calculation.

Learn more: Documents 05, 07, 08, 21; Guide Ch 5.

APG 210 para 142: Largest net 30-day collateral flow

What it says: Supports Att A paras 53 (Table 3). The Att A para 53 table requires collateral outflows from market valuation changes to be 100 per cent of the largest absolute net 30-day collateral flow in the past 24 months. APRA expects all derivatives (including futures) and other margined transactions (repos, reverse repos, collateral swaps and any margined products) in the calculation. Only flows driven by market valuation changes count. Collateral received or returned at maturity or reset, where offset by a settlement amount, is not included (APG 210 para 142).

In everyday words: Look at every 30-day stretch in the last two years and take the biggest net collateral move caused by prices changing.

What you do about it: Keep a daily collateral flow series for 24 months for all margined products. Separate valuation moves from settlement. Recompute monthly.

Evidence we keep: 24-month collateral series with classification of flows and the maximum.

APG 210 para 143: Head office overnight placements

As it is outside the LCR window, the branch assumes it stays. The treatment may also apply to retained earnings held by the branch (APG 210 para 144).

Chapter 2: Contingent funding obligations - buyback of debt securities

APG 210 para 145: Debt buybacks

What it says: Supports Att A para 53 and Table 3 (buybacks of domestic Australian debt securities). In stress, some buyback requests could still be honoured for reputation reasons or because restrictions take time to apply. A bank can apply to APRA for permission to use a lower buyback run-off rate than APS 210 requires if it can show tangible measures in policy and practice to reduce buybacks (for example a prudent hard daily limit) and that they work on an ongoing basis (APG 210 para 145).

APG 210 para 146: What counts as trade finance

Trade finance instruments are trade-related obligations underpinned by movement of goods, such as documentary trade letters of credit, documentary and clean collection, import bills and export bills; and guarantees directly related to trade finance, such as shipping guarantees (APG 210 para 146).

Check yourself

  1. Does APG 210 create enforceable requirements? Where does it say so? Answer: No. The "About this guide" section says practice guides do not themselves create enforceable requirements [S1]. The binding rules are in APS 210.
  2. A bank has a customer with A$400,000 of less stable retail savings. Describe the split in APG 210 para 114. Answer: The first A$250,000 (the Financial Claims Scheme limit) scores zero for the limit criterion. The remaining A$150,000 scores two points (APG 210 para 114; Att A Table 1).
  3. Example Bank's net cash outflows are A$11.55bn. What self-securitised holding does APG 210 para 67 call prudent? Answer: 30 per cent of 11.55 = A$3.465bn, unencumbered.
  4. A corporate customer holds A$10m in an account called "cash management" paying a low rate, and you cannot work out how much is needed for payments. What treatment? Answer: Product name and rate are not enough (APG 210 para 133). If the excess cannot be determined, the entire deposit is non-operational (APG 210 para 135; Att A para 50).
  5. A 31-day notice deposit of A$1m has not been called and its next withdrawal date is 40 days away. Then notice is given with 25 days left. What outflow in each case? Answer: First case zero. Once called, with 30 days or less to repayment, 100 per cent, so A$1m (APG 210 para 121).
  6. A repo of 18 months is secured by a bond with 4 months to maturity. What RSF applies to the bond? Answer: The bond is treated as encumbered for the repo's term, 18 months, so 100 per cent RSF (APG 210 para 165; Att C para 23).
  7. What did the July 2025 version of APG 210 add compared with July 2024? Answer: ELA operational capability (paras 75 and 76), market value of liquid assets for the LCR (para 77) and for MLH ADIs, with a quarterly revaluation expectation (paras 149 to 151) [S1] [S3].
  8. A retail run is feared. Which two things must you not assume in the plan, and who is told? Answer: Do not assume cutting withdrawal limits or shutting digital access (APG 210 para 71). APRA and the RBA should be told as soon as practicable (APG 210 para 74).

What to remember

  • A practice guide describes sound practice; the standard sets the rules. Keep a written reason when we depart from the guide.
  • The current APG 210 is the July 2025 version with 167 paragraphs; older paragraph numbers are 3 to 6 lower.
  • Deposit classification is where the guide bites hardest: customer-level data, FCS split, operational excess, notice deposits, intermediaries, SMEs.
  • Value liquid assets at market value, diversify them and never count an asset twice.
  • Stress tests, the funding plan and the contingency funding plan must connect: results feed limits and the plan.
  • Be able to supply accurate information quickly in an emergency (ELA) and test it each year.
  • Chapter 3 is for MLH ADIs; Example Bank only needs it for awareness.
