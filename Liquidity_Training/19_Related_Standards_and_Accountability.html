1. The big picture: how the rules fit together

1.1 What a prudential standard is, from the start

APRA (the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority) is the government body that supervises banks. A bank that takes deposits is called an ADI (authorised deposit-taking institution). The Banking Act 1959 lets APRA write rules for ADIs. Those rules are called prudential standards. The power is in section 11AF of the Banking Act, and section 11AG says an ADI "must comply with the standard" [S24]. That is why APS 210 opens with "This Prudential Standard is made under section 11AF of the Banking Act 1959" (APS 210 para 1) [S1].

The standards come in families, shown by the first letters of their names:

Prefix Meaning Example
APS ADI Prudential Standard: applies to banks, building societies and credit unions only APS 210 Liquidity
CPS Cross-industry Prudential Standard: applies to banks and also to insurers (and some to superannuation funds) CPS 220 Risk Management
ARS / ARF ADI Reporting Standard / Reporting Form: the returns, covered in documents 11 and 18 ARS 210.0 Liquidity
APG / CPG Prudential Practice Guide: APRA's views on good practice, not binding; document 17 APG 210

Prudential standards are legally binding on the ADI. Practice guides are not. The Banking Act is the law above both. Breaking a standard is a breach of section 11AG [S24].

1.2 Words every standard uses

These words are defined once, in CPS 001 Defined terms, and every other standard says "terms defined in CPS 001 appear in bold the first time they are used" (APS 210 para 7) [S14]. You will meet:

  • Level 1: "either the ADI itself or the ELE" (the extended licensed entity, which is the bank plus certain closely tied entities) [S14].
  • Level 2: the bank and its subsidiaries added together (consolidated) [S14].
  • SFI (significant financial institution): an ADI that "is not a foreign ADI and has total assets in excess of AUD $30 billion" for 12 months, or that APRA determines to be one; non-SFI is any other [S14].
  • MSFI (most significant financial institution): an SFI with total assets above AUD $300 billion, or so determined [S14].

Interpretation: Example Bank's balance sheet is A$100 billion (document 02). On the CPS 001 definition it is therefore an SFI, and it is not an MSFI (below A$300 billion). That matters below, because CPS 190 and APS 330 treat SFIs differently from smaller banks. The definition counts "total assets" as last reported to APRA, so confirm the reported figure with your controller; the training balance sheet is not a regulatory report.

2. CPS 001 Defined terms

What it is for. A rulebook with 20 standards needs one dictionary. Before CPS 001, each family (banks, general insurers, life insurers, health insurers) had its own "001" definitions standard. CPS 001 centralises the definitions "used in the banking and insurance prudential frameworks within one prudential standard" [S14].

The clauses that touch us.

  • Clause 4 says references in other standards to "APS 001 Definitions" (and the insurer equivalents) are to be read as references to CPS 001 [S14]. This matters because some older standards, for example CPS 220 (made in 2019), still print the words "APS 001" (CPS 220 clause 6) [S3].
  • The key terms for liquidity work: Level 1, Level 2, SFI, non-SFI, MSFI, RBA, "net credit balance" and "extended licensed entity" [S14].

How it connects to APS 210. APS 210 para 7 says terms defined in CPS 001 appear in bold the first time they are used. APS 210 paras 3 and 4 use Level 1 and Level 2. APS 210 para 52 lets APRA classify a locally incorporated ADI as an LCR ADI or MLH ADI; that classification is APRA's, not a CPS 001 definition (APS 210 paras 52 and 53).

What our team does. Nothing to submit. When you are unsure whether a word in APS 210 has a special meaning, look in CPS 001. Do not define terms yourself in a working paper; quote CPS 001 and note the version.

Version. Banking, Insurance, Life Insurance and Health Insurance (prudential standard) determination No. 1 of 2026, dated 19 May 2026, commences 1 July 2026 (registered as F2026L00607) [S14]. APRA's page shows it "In force" [S14]. A 27 May 2026 APRA announcement says CPS 001 was updated to formalise a third tier in the banking prudential framework, which is where the new MSFI term comes from [S36].

Unverified: I read the new CPS 001 text, but I did not compare it clause by clause with the 2025 version. What the "third tier" changes for standards that we use, such as APS 330 or CPS 190, depends on those standards' own wording. Check with your controller before relying on any tier-specific rule.

3. CPS 220 Risk Management

What it is for. CPS 220 requires a bank to have a risk management framework: "systems for identifying, measuring, evaluating, monitoring, reporting, and controlling or mitigating material risks" [S3]. Think of it as the umbrella. Liquidity risk is one of the risks under the umbrella; APS 210 is the detailed standard for that one risk.

The requirements that touch liquidity work.

CPS 220 clause What it requires Why it matters to us
9 The Board is "ultimately responsible" for the risk management framework and sets the risk appetite The Board's liquidity appetite (for example Example Bank's "LCR at least 120%", an Example Bank convention) lives here
23 The framework must include a risk appetite statement, a risk management strategy (RMS), a business plan, a risk management function, an ICAAP, a management information system (MIS) and a review process Our daily and monthly liquidity reports are part of the MIS

For a group, clause 17 requires the Head of a group to "maintain a Board-approved liquidity management policy for the group", to include a strategy for sufficient liquidity "including in stressed conditions", to outline processes to find constraints on moving funds within the group, and to send APRA a copy within 10 business days of Board approval (CPS 220 clause 17) [S3].

Interpretation: Example Bank is a single legal entity (document 02), so Level 1 equals Level 2 and it probably has no group to head. Clause 17 is then unlikely to apply as a separate obligation. A bank with subsidiaries would send its group liquidity policy to APRA under clause 17. Check with your controller whether Example Bank is regarded as a Head of a group.

How it connects to APS 210. APS 210 para 35 says the liquidity risk management framework must be reviewed "as part of the review of the risk management framework under Prudential Standard CPS 220". APS 210 para 27 says the liquidity framework "must be well integrated into the ADI's overall risk management process". APS 210 para 17 requires an annual review and documentation, which is a stricter rhythm than the CPS 220 three-yearly comprehensive review.

What our team must do or supply.

  1. Keep reporting that is accurate and timely, so the MIS duty (CPS 220 clauses 23(g) and 25) is met. Document 12 (Controls, incidents and escalation) lists the controls.
  2. Supply the monthly and quarterly liquidity numbers used in the Board risk appetite reports.
  3. When asked by internal audit (clause 44), give them the control evidence from document 12.
  4. Tell your controller immediately if you find a control failure that might be a "significant breach of, or material deviation from" the framework (clause 53), so the risk function can judge whether a 10-business-day notice is due.

Typical practice: In many banks the Chief Risk Officer's team, not Finance, prepares the notices to APRA under CPS 220, using facts supplied by the business. Confirm Example Bank's arrangement with your controller.

4. CPS 230 Operational Risk Management

What it is for. Operational risk is the risk of loss or disruption from failed processes, systems, people or outside events. CPS 230 says a bank must manage operational risk, keep its "critical operations" running within set "tolerance levels" through severe disruptions, and manage risks from outside service providers [S4]. It matters to liquidity because the daily LCR needs data, systems and people, and because a bank that cannot make payments can fail even when it has plenty of liquid assets.

The requirements that touch liquidity work.

All clause numbers above are from CPS 230 [S4].

How it connects to APS 210. APS 210 para 36 requires "a robust framework for comprehensively projecting cash flows". APS 210 para 40 requires "a reliable management information system". APS 210 para 41 requires active management of intraday liquidity so the bank can meet "payment and settlement obligations on a timely basis". APS 210 para 70 says an ADI "must have the operational capability to provide information along with its application for ELA". Each of these needs working systems, which CPS 230 protects.

What our team must do or supply.

  1. List the processes that produce our numbers (data load, LCR engine, NSFR workbook, return submission, public disclosure inputs) and the systems and spreadsheets behind them, for the critical operations map (clause 26(b)).
  2. Give your controller a view on how long each could be down before a regulatory deadline is missed, so tolerance levels can be set (clause 37).
  3. Take part in the annual business continuity exercise (clause 42), including a "no daily LCR" scenario.
  4. Report data and system incidents at once, so the 72-hour and 24-hour clocks (clauses 32 and 41) can be met by the people who decide whether APRA must be told.
  5. Report any third party that touches our data or calculations, so it can be assessed as a service provider (clauses 46 to 49).

Typical practice: Banks usually set the tolerance for a daily regulatory calculation in hours, tied to the time the result is needed for management decisions. The figures are bank choices and not in CPS 230 (the standard only requires that tolerances are set). Ask your controller for Example Bank's figure.

5. CPS 190 Recovery and Exit Planning

What it is for. A recovery plan is the bank's own plan for what it would do if it ran into severe stress: sell assets, raise capital, cut costs, raise funding. An exit plan is how it could leave the banking business in an orderly and solvent way if recovery fails. CPS 190 requires a plan that is "appropriate to the size, business mix and complexity" and "approved by the Board" [S5].

The requirements that touch liquidity work.

CPS 190 clause What it requires Link to liquidity
13 Develop and maintain a recovery and exit plan showing how the bank could recover financial resilience and, if that fails, exit in an orderly and solvent way
15 The plan must be integrated into the risk management framework (CPS 220) and the "capital management and liquidity management frameworks" Links the plan to APS 210
16 "Must not assume extraordinary public sector support" A recovery plan cannot rely on Exceptional Liquidity Assistance or a government guarantee as a planned source
17 and 18 The Board approves the plan; an SFI's Board must form a view on whether recovery capacity is sufficient
19(b) A trigger framework "for the early identification and monitoring of stress", including "a range of early warning indicators" Our early-warning indicators under APS 210 para 39 are natural triggers
20 An SFI must include scenario analysis with at least two severe scenarios (one systemic, one idiosyncratic) and an assessment of recovery capacity "measured in quantitative terms by calculating the amount of capital and liquidity that can be rebuilt" Needs liquidity numbers from us
21(d) For each action an SFI must estimate "where relevant" the effect on capital and liquidity We calculate the pro-forma LCR and NSFR for each action

All clause numbers are from CPS 190 [S5].

How it connects to APS 210. The contingency funding plan under APS 210 paras 47 to 51 is the liquidity-only plan for stress. The recovery plan is wider: it covers capital, profit and the whole bank. A good contingency funding plan feeds the liquidity actions in the recovery plan. APS 210 para 63 says stress test outcomes must "develop effective contingency plans"; CPS 190 clause 20 asks for recovery scenarios "severe enough to threaten the SFI's viability".

What our team must do or supply.

  1. Provide the starting LCR, NSFR and liquid asset position for each recovery scenario.
  2. Recalculate the LCR and NSFR after each recovery action (for example selling a portfolio, term funding, a repo) using the same methods as document 08 and document 09.
  3. Supply the early-warning indicator levels that are used as triggers (document 10).
  4. Keep a record of which numbers went into the plan, so the plan can be traced to a dated report.

Interpretation: Applying the CPS 001 definition to Example Bank's A$100 billion balance sheet makes it an SFI, so the SFI additions (clauses 18, 20, 21, 28(a), 30 and 31) apply, including the annual review. If the bank were a non-SFI the plan would need review only every three years (clause 28(b)).

6. CPS 900 Resolution Planning

What it is for. "Resolution" is what happens when a bank can no longer meet its obligations and APRA takes charge of the outcome: for example a wind-down, a transfer of the business to another bank, or recapitalisation (CPS 900 clauses 11(c) and 13) [S6]. CPS 900 is about preparing in advance so that APRA can do this in an orderly way.

The requirements that touch liquidity work.

  • Clause 7: the standard "commences on 1 January 2024" but a bank is subject to it "following notification from APRA" [S6]. Clause 4 applies it to SFIs, and to non-SFIs that APRA determines provide critical functions.
  • Clauses 14 and 28: the bank must support APRA and maintain capabilities for resolution, including "financial management" and "data and systems" (clause 28(c) and (d)).
  • Clause 26: maintain "the financial resources required to support the resolution plan"; clause 27 lets APRA require loss-absorbing capacity and adjust an ADI's Total Capital requirement under APS 110.
  • Clauses 20 to 25: a resolvability assessment and, if APRA requires, a pre-positioning plan to remove barriers, including renegotiating contracts and ensuring "operational continuity of key functions".
  • Clause 32: review the resolvability assessment at least every three years.

How it connects to APS 210. CPS 900 does not set liquidity ratios. It affects liquidity work in two ways. First, in a resolution APRA needs fast, reliable data on deposits, funding and collateral; the datasets behind our LCR (balances by counterparty and maturity, encumbrance, intragroup positions) are the same data. Second, APS 210 para 70 (the ELA information duty) and the Banking Act powers in section 14 of this document are part of the same crisis toolkit.

What our team must do or supply. Nothing is required of us regularly. If APRA has notified Example Bank, the resolution team may ask us for data extracts at short notice; the rule in document 12 is to keep the daily LCR source data retrievable and reconciled.

Unverified: Whether APRA has notified Example Bank under clause 7 is not something the standard shows. A real bank would know. Ask your controller.

7. The capital standards: APS 110, APS 111 and APS 112

7.1 Capital in five lines

Capital is the bank's own money: shareholders' equity and some long-term debt that can absorb losses. Deposits are owed to customers. Capital is owed to nobody in the same way, so it is a cushion. Liquidity (cash and liquid assets) answers "can I pay today?"; capital answers "can I absorb losses?". Document 02 explains the difference in a four-row table.

Standard What it does Touchpoint for us
APS 110 Capital Adequacy Sets minimum capital ratios, the capital buffers, the internal capital adequacy assessment (ICAAP) and the leverage ratio Leverage ratio; ICAAP stress testing alongside liquidity; some definitions used in the NSFR
APS 111 Capital Adequacy: Measurement of Capital Says what counts as regulatory capital (Common Equity Tier 1, Additional Tier 1, Tier 2) The 100% ASF factor for capital in the NSFR
APS 112 Capital Adequacy: Standardised Approach to Credit Risk Gives the risk weights for each type of exposure Risk weights used to decide HQLA class and some NSFR factors

7.2 Regulatory capital in the NSFR (APS 111)

The NSFR counts "available stable funding" (ASF): funding that is expected to stay for a year. Capital is the most stable funding there is. The standard says the 100 per cent ASF factor applies to:

In the standard: "the total amount of regulatory capital, before the application of regulatory adjustments, as defined in Prudential Standard APS 111 Capital Adequacy: Measurement of Capital (APS 111), excluding the proportion of Tier 2 instruments with a residual maturity of less than one year" (Att C para 11).

So the team takes the capital figure from the capital team's APS 111 calculation, before deductions, and removes any Tier 2 debt due within a year. Att C para 11(b) also gives 100 per cent to other capital instruments of one year or more. Assets that are "deducted from regulatory capital (as defined in APS 111)" get a 100 per cent required stable funding factor (Att C para 37(c)). In Example Bank's base case, equity of A$8.0bn is treated as regulatory capital with a 100% ASF factor (document 02).

7.3 Risk weights (APS 112)

A risk weight is a percentage that scales an exposure to show its credit risk: a government bond might be 0%, a loan to a bank 20% or more, a house loan about 35%. APS 210 borrows these:

APS 210 use Rule Reference

HQLA2A securities Claims "assigned a 20 per cent risk-weight under Attachment B of APS 112" Att A para 10(a)(i)
Secured funding with domestic sovereigns, PSEs, MDBs Counterparty risk weight of 20 per cent or lower under APS 112 gives the 25% run-off category Att A Table 3

So if APS 112 changes a risk weight (see section 15), a bond could change HQLA class or a loan could change NSFR bucket. That is why we watch APS 112 consultations.

7.4 The leverage ratio (APS 110)

The leverage ratio is capital divided by total exposures with no risk weights. It is a simple backstop. APS 110 says an IRB ADI (one approved to use its own models for credit risk) "must, at all times, maintain a minimum leverage ratio of 3.5 per cent" and APRA may apply a requirement to a standardised ADI (APS 110 clauses 37 to 39) [S7]. The ratio is Tier 1 Capital divided by the exposure measure in Attachment D (APS 110 Attachment D clause 1) [S7]. The exposure measure includes all balance sheet assets, including cash and RBA balances, and no netting of assets and liabilities (Attachment D clause 2). APRA "may temporarily exclude central bank reserves" in exceptional macroeconomic circumstances (Attachment D clause 3).

Why this touches liquidity. Holding a large pool of RBA balances to pass the LCR increases total assets, which increases the leverage exposure. Treasury therefore balances the liquidity buffer against the leverage ratio. Securities financing transactions (repos) also count in the exposure measure. APS 210 Att C para 26 allows netting of securities financing transactions for the NSFR only if the netting conditions in paragraph 38 of Attachment D of APS 110 are met. Att C para 28 uses the cash variation margin conditions in "paragraph 23 of Attachment D to APS 110".

Example: Example Bank has equity of A$8.0bn and total assets of A$100.0bn (document 02). On a crude basis, 8.0 divided by 100.0 is 8.0%. The real ratio uses Tier 1 Capital after deductions and an exposure measure that also includes off-balance sheet items and derivatives, so it would be lower. It is shown only to give you a feel for the scale against the 3.5% minimum.

Other APS 110 touchpoints. The ICAAP (clauses 14 to 22) requires stress testing and scenario analysis. Capital planning and liquidity planning use the same scenarios in many banks; align assumptions so the Board sees one story. Minimum prudential capital ratios are 4.5% CET1, 6.0% Tier 1, 8.0% Total Capital (APS 110 clause 24).

What our team must do or supply.

  1. Take regulatory capital, before regulatory adjustments, from the capital reporting team for the NSFR each month, with the Tier 2 maturity split (Att C para 11).
  2. Take risk weights from the credit risk system using APS 112, not our own tables.
  3. Warn Treasury and Capital Management when a planned increase in RBA balances or reverse repos would move the leverage ratio.
  4. When APS 110, 111 or 112 change, tell your controller which APS 210 paragraphs cite them (the table above).

8. APS 117 Interest Rate Risk in the Banking Book, and how it differs from liquidity risk

What it is for. The "banking book" is the part of the balance sheet that the bank holds to earn interest over time: loans, deposits, held-to-maturity securities. It is separate from the "trading book" of positions held to trade. Interest rate risk in the banking book (IRRBB) is the risk that a change in interest rates reduces the bank's earnings or the economic value of its balance sheet [S10].

The difference, in one table.

Liquidity risk (APS 210) IRRBB (APS 117)
The question Can the bank pay what it owes, when it falls due? What happens to earnings and value if rates move?
The loss Forced sale of assets, expensive funding, failure to pay Lower net interest income, lower economic value
Main measure LCR, NSFR Change in economic value; earnings at risk; an IRRBB capital charge
Cause of a bad outcome Money leaves faster than it comes in Rates move against the bank's mismatch of repricing dates
The remedy Hold liquid assets, stable funding Hedge, change repricing, hold capital
Capital or liquidity? Liquidity requirement Capital requirement (and a risk framework)

The two risks meet because both depend on how long customers leave deposits and how fast loans are repaid. A bank that is short of liquidity may borrow at higher rates, and a bank that hedges rate risk with derivatives may post collateral that uses liquid assets.

What APS 117 requires that touches us.

  • It applies to all ADIs other than foreign ADIs and purchased payment facility providers (APS 117 clause 2) [S10]. Clause 34 requires a risk appetite statement for IRRBB with limits on both economic value and earnings, and clause 36 requires the Board, or a committee, to review IRRBB reports at least semi-annually [S10].
  • Attachment A defines a core deposit as the part of a portfolio of non-maturity deposits that "has a stable balance" and pays an interest rate that is "managed by the ADI" and "does not usually change in response to movements in wholesale market rates" (APS 117 Attachment A clause 9) [S10].
  • An ADI approved to use an internal IRRBB model must use that definition; the repricing balance of core deposits is limited by a rule that expects the balance usually to be no more than 90 per cent of the product balance (Attachment A, immediately after clause 9) [S10].

The trap. "Stable" has two meanings. In APS 210, stable deposits are the portion "fully covered by the Financial Claims Scheme" where the depositor has other established relationships, or the account is transactional (Att A para 37). In APS 117, a core deposit is a modelling concept about a stable balance and a managed rate. A deposit can be core for IRRBB and less stable for the LCR, and the reverse. Never use one population for the other without checking the definition. Document 07 (Data and classification) lists the fields.

10. APS 221 Large Exposures and APS 222 Associations with Related Entities

10.1 APS 221 Large Exposures

What it is for. A large exposure is one to a single counterparty, or a group of connected counterparties, equal to or more than 10 per cent of the bank's Tier 1 Capital (APS 221 clause 16) [S12]. APS 221 stops the bank depending too heavily on any one name. Aggregate large exposures to one counterparty or connected group "must not exceed 25 per cent of the ADI's Tier 1 Capital" (clause 30). Foreign government exposures with a zero risk weight may go to 50 per cent, and exposures between domestic systemically important banks to 20 per cent (clause 30(a) and (b)). The limits "do not apply to the exposures of an ADI to its related entities" (APS 222 covers those).

Links to liquidity.

  • Clause 18(d) excludes from large exposures "exposures to governments or central banks that are held as high-quality liquid assets under ... APS 210" and clause 18(c) excludes Australian Government exposures and Australian dollar exposures to the RBA [S12]. So the HQLA portfolio does not breach large exposure limits, but the HQLA definition in APS 210 Att A para 9 decides what is excluded; a bond that falls out of HQLA class may fall back into the limit.
  • Clause 18(f) excludes intraday interbank exposures. Clause 18(j) excludes exposures to an ADI under an industry support contract on liquidity certified by APRA under section 11CB of the Banking Act.
  • Funding concentration is not a large exposure: clause 17 says exposures to "funding sources" are not subject to the large exposure limits. Funding concentration is a liquidity matter under APS 210 para 29(b) ("the diversity and stability of funding sources") and para 33.
  • Counterparties on both sides: the bank's loans to and deposits from the same large counterparty need consistent connected-group mapping in our data (document 07).

We receive from Credit Risk the list of HQLA exclusions used in large exposure reporting, to keep the two consistent.

10.2 APS 222 Associations with Related Entities

What it is for. A related entity is a company in the same group as the bank, or tied to it, such as a subsidiary, a parent or a funds management arm. APS 222 aims for the bank to "identify, monitor and control contagion risks arising from their associations and dealings with related entities" [S13]. If a related company fails, the bank must not be dragged down.

What it requires that touches liquidity.

  • Dealings must be on an arm's-length basis and market terms (APS 222 clause 14(a)).
  • Limits on exposures to related entities must consider "the impact on the ADI's stand-alone capital and liquidity positions in the event of a failure of a related entity" (clause 14(b)(ii)) [S13].
  • Limits on exposures at Level 1, measured against Tier 1 Capital: 25% for an individual related ADI (75% in aggregate for all related ADIs); 25% for an individual regulated related entity; 15% for an individual unregulated related entity; 35% in aggregate for other related entities (clause 29) [S13]. "Exposure" covers funds lent, guarantees, facilities and similar.
  • When the bank takes part in group operations, they must not harm its safety and soundness as a stand-alone entity, and shared services need written agreements (clause 28).
  • A liquidity facility the bank gives to a funds management vehicle must be deducted from Common Equity Tier 1 Capital if, for example, it can be drawn to fund additional assets of the vehicle (APS 222 Attachment B clause 7) [S13].

Links to APS 210. The standard does not refer to APS 222 by name. The connections are practical:

  • Intragroup funding. Funds placed with a related ADI or a subsidiary count against the APS 222 limits and, in the LCR, are inflows from or outflows to a financial institution. APS 210 Att A para 24 allows Level 2 liquid assets held at a legal entity only if "freely available to the consolidated (parent) entity in times of stress", and para 29 requires the Level 2 LCR to ignore excess liquidity that cannot cross borders unless "there is reasonable certainty about the availability of such liquidity". Intragroup lending is not automatically stable funding.
  • Facilities to group vehicles. A facility to "special purpose funding vehicles, or other vehicles used to finance an ADI's own assets" is a liquidity facility captured in full (Att A para 52). The same vehicle is likely a related entity under APS 222.
  • Group operations. Shared treasury or settlement operations with group members engage both APS 222 clause 28 and CPS 230 service provider rules. APS 222 footnote 5 refers to the outsourcing standard CPS 231; I understand CPS 230 has replaced CPS 231, but I did not check that on apra.gov.au, so look at the current text.

What our team must do or supply. (1) Identify related-entity counterparties in the data (document 07 attribute: "related entity flag"). (2) Report balances with related entities separately in the daily LCR working paper so exposure limits and the liquidity position can be read together. (3) Supply Treasury with the effect on the LCR of a related-entity failure when they test a limit (clause 14(b)(ii)).

Typical practice: Banks run the related-entity limit monitoring in the credit or finance function, and the liquidity team receives the list of related parties each month. 11.1 What it is for, from the start

A bank sends private returns to APRA (documents 11 and 18). Public disclosure is what the bank publishes for everyone: investors, analysts, customers and other banks. The aim, in APS 330's words, is "to contribute to the transparency of financial markets and to enhance market discipline" [S15]. 12. The Financial Accountability Regime (FAR)

12.1 What it is, from the start

Before 2018 it was hard to say which senior person was answerable when a bank failed its customers or its regulator. The Banking Executive Accountability Regime (BEAR) started in 2018 for banks. In 2023 Parliament passed the Financial Accountability Regime Act 2023 (the FAR Act), which replaced BEAR and extended the idea to insurers and superannuation trustees. APRA and ASIC administer it jointly [S19]. FAR started for ADIs and their authorised non-operating holding companies on 15 March 2024, and for insurers and superannuation trustees from 15 March 2025 [S19].

The idea is simple. A bank is an accountable entity. Its most senior people with real responsibility are accountable persons. They must meet duties set by law, and the bank must keep the right people in place and tell the regulators about changes. Failing the duties can lead to civil penalties, reduced variable pay or disqualification [S18].

12.2 Who is an accountable person

Section 10(1) of the FAR Act makes an individual an accountable person of an accountable entity if they hold a position in the entity (or in a company of which it is a subsidiary) and "because of that position, the person has actual or effective senior executive responsibility" for "management or control of the accountable entity", or "of a significant or substantial part or aspect of the operations" of the entity or its group [S18]. Section 10(2) and (3) add people whose responsibilities or positions are prescribed by the Minister's rules. Section 11 lets the regulator exclude some responsibilities [S18].

So accountability follows real senior responsibility, not the job title. The Act's own example is an accountable person of an ADI with "senior executive responsibility for management of the ADI's financial resources" (section 21, example) [S18].

Typical practice: In a bank like Example Bank the accountable persons would include the Board directors, the CEO, the CFO, the Chief Risk Officer, the Treasurer and others with prescribed responsibilities. For liquidity, the executive who owns liquidity and funding management carries the main responsibility; the CRO answers for the independent oversight function (APS 210 para 28); the CFO answers for the reporting. The list is the bank's, held in its accountability statements. Ask your controller whom the register names for liquidity.

Are you an accountable person? Almost certainly not. A graduate analyst who prepares numbers does not have "senior executive responsibility" for a significant part of the bank. But your work is the evidence on which accountable persons rely, so their duties shape your working discipline.

12.3 What accountable persons must do

Section 21(1) of the FAR Act says an accountable person must conduct the responsibilities of their position [S18]:

In the standard: "(a) by acting with honesty and integrity, and with due skill, care and diligence; and (b) by dealing with the Regulator in an open, constructive and cooperative way; and (c) by taking reasonable steps ... to prevent matters from arising that would ... adversely affect the prudential standing or prudential reputation of the accountable entity; and (d) by taking reasonable steps ... to prevent matters from arising that would ... result in a material contravention by the accountable entity of" the FAR Act, the Banking Act, credit and financial services law and instruments made under them (FAR Act section 21(1)).

A contravention of APS 210 is a breach of section 11AG of the Banking Act, and section 21(1)(d) covers "regulations, instruments, directions or orders made under" the Banking Act. So a material liquidity breach is exactly the kind of matter an accountable person must take reasonable steps to prevent.

Section 22 says "taking reasonable steps" includes [S18]:

  • "having appropriate governance, control and risk management in relation to that matter";
  • "having safeguards against inappropriate delegations of responsibility";
  • "having appropriate procedures for identifying and remediating problems that arise or may arise"; and
  • "taking appropriate action in response to non-compliance, or suspected non-compliance".

Each of these is something your daily work supplies: controls (document 12), a review chain, error logging, escalation. When your controller signs the LCR, an accountable person relies on that sign-off.

The accountable entity's own duties are in section 20 (honesty and integrity, dealing openly with the regulator, preventing matters that harm prudential standing, ensuring each accountable person meets their duties) and its key personnel obligations in section 23: it must ensure the responsibilities of its accountable persons cover all parts of its operations, and that no prohibited person holds a role (section 24) [S18].

12.6 What FAR means for someone who prepares liquidity numbers

  1. You are the first line of evidence. Accountable persons must be able to show "reasonable steps". Your review notes, reconciliation sign-offs, error logs and escalation emails are those steps.

13. The Financial Claims Scheme (FCS)

13.1 What it is

If a bank fails, depositors with small balances need their money fast. The Financial Claims Scheme is a government scheme, run by APRA, that pays them. APRA's page says it protects deposits "up to $250,000 per account holder per ADI" and "aims to return deposits to account holders within seven days of activation" [S25].

The scheme is in Division 2AA of the Banking Act. Its purpose is to entitle holders of "protected accounts" with a "declared ADI" to be paid amounts "to maintain the account-holders' liquidity before they would receive payment in a winding up" (section 16AB) [S24]. The Minister may declare an ADI when APRA has applied to wind it up or a statutory manager has taken control (section 16AD). Payment is limited to "the limit prescribed by, or worked out under, the regulations" (section 16AG) [S24]. The A$250,000 figure is APRA's published limit [S25].

Deposits are added together across all accounts of one account holder at one ADI, and joint accounts are split equally between holders [S25, from a search summary of APRA's coverage FAQ; I did not read that page in full].

All from the Banking Act 1959 [S24].

Section 13(3) and APS 210 para 14 are not the same test. APS 210 para 14 says an ADI "must inform APRA as soon as possible of any concerns it has about its current or future liquidity position", and "if an ADI experiences a severe liquidity stress, it must notify APRA immediately". The offence in section 13(3) applies at a higher threshold: the bank itself considers it likely to become unable to meet its obligations, or about to suspend payment. In practice a bank tells APRA long before that. The duty to tell APRA early is the prudential standard's; the duty of last resort is the Act's.

Example: In the incident scenario of document 12 (the LCR falls to 94.7% after A$2.8bn of wholesale debt moves inside 30 days), Example Bank breaches the 100% minimum (APS 210 para 55) and must inform APRA immediately (Att A para 5 and APS 210 para 14). A ratio breach alone does not engage the section 13(3) offence, because the bank can still meet its obligations; it would if the bank came to believe it was likely to become unable to pay.

What our team must do. Know that the facts in our reports may be used for these notifications. Escalate at once (document 12) so the people whose duty it is can decide what to tell APRA. Do not decide that a breach is "not serious enough" to report.

Check yourself

  1. Question: Which section of the Banking Act gives APRA the power to make prudential standards, and which makes compliance a legal duty? Answer: Section 11AF gives the power (APS 210 para 1 cites it); section 11AG says an ADI to which a standard applies "must comply with the standard".

  2. Question: Example Bank has Tier 1 Capital of A$8.0bn (a simplification). Using APS 221 and APS 222, what are (a) the large exposure threshold, (b) the single-counterparty limit, (c) the limit for one unregulated related entity, and (d) the aggregate limit for related entities that are not ADIs? Answer: (a) 10% of 8.0 = A$0.8bn (APS 221 clause 16). (b) 25% of 8.0 = A$2.0bn (APS 221 clause 30). (c) 15% of 8.0 = A$1.2bn (APS 222 clause 29(b)(ii)). (d) 35% of 8.0 = A$2.8bn (APS 222 clause 29(b)(iii)).

  3. Question: Example Bank has Tier 1 Capital of A$8.0bn and an exposure measure of A$100.0bn. Treasury raises A$3.0bn of funding and holds it all as RBA balances. What is the leverage ratio before and after, against a 3.5% minimum? Ignore other effects. Answer: Before: 8.0 / 100.0 = 8.0%. After: the exposure rises to 103.0, so 8.0 / 103.0 = 7.77%. Both are above 3.5%, but the ratio fell, because RBA balances count in the exposure measure (APS 110 clause 37 and Attachment D clause 1).

  4. Question: A cyber incident stops the daily LCR for a day, and payments are disrupted for two hours beyond tolerance. Which APRA notifications under CPS 230 might be needed, and by when? Answer: If the incident is likely to have a material financial impact or a material impact on critical operations, notify as soon as possible and not later than 72 hours after becoming aware (CPS 230 clause 32). A disruption to a critical operation outside tolerance (payments are a minimum critical operation for an ADI, clause 35(a)) must be notified within 24 hours (clause 41). The risk function decides; we report the facts at once.

  5. Question: A self-securitisation is used to obtain a repo with the RBA. Can Example Bank exclude the SPV from its NSFR under APS 210 para 5? Answer: No, on the reading in section 9. APS 210 para 5 allows exclusion only where the operational requirements for regulatory capital relief are met, and APS 120 clause 38 treats a self-securitisation as funding-only, which gets no relief. The loans stay in the NSFR. This is an Interpretation; confirm with your controller.

  6. Question: What is the difference between a "stable deposit" in APS 210 and a "core deposit" in APS 117? Answer: A stable deposit (Att A para 37) is the part fully covered by the Financial Claims Scheme where the depositor has other established relationships or the account is transactional; it is a run-off concept for the LCR. A core deposit (APS 117 Attachment A clause 9) is the part of a non-maturity deposit portfolio with a stable balance and a managed rate that does not usually follow wholesale rates; it is a repricing concept for interest rate risk.

  7. Question: Who must attest to the public disclosures under APS 330, and to what? Answer: An Accountable Person of the ADI must attest in writing that the disclosures were prepared in accordance with the ADI's disclosure policy, and the attestation is included in the disclosure (APS 330 clause 30). The 2027 version defines Accountable Person by the Financial Accountability Regime Act 2023; the 2025 version cites subsection 37BA of the Banking Act.

  8. Question: From which date does the new APS 330 apply, and what number does the clause for SFIs using MLH (no LCR or NSFR disclosure) carry in each version? Answer: From 1 January 2027. It is Attachment A clause 44 in the version in force and Attachment A clause 47 in the 2027 version.

  9. Question: A customer holds A$400,000 in a single on-line savings account at Example Bank, with no other products and a promotional rate. Score the account and give the run-off rate for the balance, ignoring any stable portion. Answer: Balance above A$250,000: 2. On-line: 2. No established relationship: 1. Heavily rate-driven: 1. Total 6; 3 or more gives 25% (Att A para 40, Tables 1 and 2).

  10. Question: Are you, as a graduate analyst, an accountable person under the Financial Accountability Regime? What does the regime still mean for you? Answer: Almost certainly not, because an accountable person has "actual or effective senior executive responsibility" for management or control of the bank or a significant part of its operations (FAR Act section 10(1)). But accountable persons must take "reasonable steps", including controls and "taking appropriate action in response to non-compliance, or suspected non-compliance" (section 22). Your accurate work, evidence and prompt escalation are the steps they rely on.

What to remember

  • Prudential standards are legally binding under sections 11AF and 11AG of the Banking Act; practice guides are not.
  • APS 210 does not stand alone: CPS 220 frames it, CPS 230 keeps the systems running, CPS 190 links the contingency funding plan to recovery, and APS 330 turns our numbers into public data.
  • Capital standards touch us in three ways: regulatory capital is 100% ASF (Att C para 11); risk weights decide HQLA class and some NSFR buckets; the leverage ratio counts RBA balances.
  • APS 210 para 5: the SPV exclusion applies to MLH and NSFR, never to the LCR, and only where capital relief is met.
  • "Stable" means different things in APS 210 (FCS cover, Att A para 37) and APS 117 (core deposits).
  • Public disclosure of the LCR (LIQ1) uses daily averages, so keep the daily components. An Accountable Person attests (APS 330 clause 30).
  • The APS 330 version from 1 January 2027 keeps the liquidity rules but renumbers them in Attachment A (41 to 44 become 44 to 47).
  • FAR does not make you an accountable person, but your controls, evidence and escalation are the proof of "reasonable steps".
  • The legal duty to tell APRA (Banking Act section 13(3)) is a last resort; the prudential duty to tell early is APS 210 para 14.
  • As at 14 November 2026 the liquidity review is announced but no consultation draft is published. Read apra.gov.au every month and keep a change log.
