20 The Reserve Bank, settlement and where cash actually moves

Purpose: To explain, from the very basics, what the Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) does for a bank like ours, how money really moves between banks each day, which RBA facilities exist, how they appear in the liquidity coverage ratio (LCR) and net stable funding ratio (NSFR) numbers you produce, and what we must be able to do if we ever ask for emergency help.

1 What a central bank does

Start with an everyday picture. You bank with a commercial bank. That bank in turn needs a bank of its own, for two jobs: a place to keep its own spare cash safely, and a way to pay other banks. In Australia the bank of the banks is the Reserve Bank of Australia, the central bank.

The RBA does four things that matter to us:

  1. It sets the price of overnight money. The Monetary Policy Board sets a target for the cash rate, which is the interest rate on unsecured overnight loans between banks. A change takes effect the day after the decision is announced, and the decision is released at 2.30 pm on the day of each Board meeting [S2]. On the RBA's cash rate page the target was 4.85 per cent from 4 November 2026 [S30]. (That number moves; never hard-code it. See section 16.)
  2. It runs the settlement system. The RBA owns and operates the Reserve Bank Information and Transfer System (RITS), Australia's high-value payment settlement system [S12].
  3. It lends to banks against collateral. Through its market operations and standing facilities it lends cash to eligible banks against securities (section 7 and section 8) [S2].
  4. It is the ultimate provider of liquidity. The Council of Financial Regulators describes the RBA as "the ultimate provider of liquidity to the financial system" [S26].

The RBA is not our prudential supervisor. The Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA) supervises banks and wrote APS 210. The RBA's own page says that eligible counterparties "are not prudentially supervised by the Reserve Bank" and that eligible status gives no entitlement to obtain liquidity [S10].

From the controller: When someone says "the RBA", ask which hat: monetary policy (the cash rate), payments (RITS and the ESAs), or market operations (repos and facilities). Our numbers touch the second and third most.

2 Exchange Settlement Accounts: the purest liquid asset

2.1 What an ESA is

An Exchange Settlement Account is a bank account that a bank holds at the RBA. Banks use it to settle their payment obligations to each other. The RBA describes final settlement of interbank obligations in Australian dollars as occurring "across ESAs held at the Reserve Bank" [S12]. The money in these accounts is also called exchange settlement (ES) balances or reserves [S2].

Three rules about ESAs [S12] [S13]:

  • an ESA must be kept in credit at all times; it cannot go overdrawn;
  • ESAs are mandatory for any ADI whose wholesale RTGS transactions are 0.25 per cent or more of the total value of wholesale RITS RTGS transactions; smaller ADIs may settle through an agent bank, or apply for their own ESA;
  • the RBA "does not guarantee that an ESA holder will be able to meet its settlement obligations" [S13].

An ADI that wants to operate its own ESA for the first time must obtain APRA's non-objection [S13].

2.2 Why an ESA balance is the best liquid asset

An asset is liquid when you can turn it into the thing you need, cash that settles payments, quickly and without losing value. A balance in an ESA is settlement money already. There is nothing to sell, no price to fall, and no counterparty other than the RBA. A government bond must first be sold or lent against, and its price can move.

APS 210 reflects this in three ways:

  • HQLA1 includes "central bank balances, to the extent that these balances may be drawn down in times of stress" (Att A para 9). HQLA1 is counted at market value with no haircut (Att A para 9).
  • In the NSFR, central bank reserves and balances receive a zero per cent required stable funding (RSF) factor (Att C para 29).
  • APRA's liquidity FAQ states that the only Australian dollar assets that qualify as HQLA1 are notes and coin, balances held with the RBA, and Australian Government and semi-government securities, and that no Australian dollar assets currently qualify as HQLA2 [S29]. That FAQ was published on 9 December 2021 and shows no later update date [S29]; the standard itself is the authority.

Watch out: The words "to the extent that these balances may be drawn down in times of stress" (Att A para 9) matter. If a balance at the RBA is tied to a requirement, for example a minimum balance the RBA expects us to hold to support after-hours payments (section 8.4), ask Treasury and your controller whether APRA would treat it as drawable. See the Unverified note in section 8.4.

3 How payments settle: RITS, real-time gross settlement and the others

3.1 The idea in everyday words

When you pay a friend at another bank, your bank owes their bank money. Banks do not hand over notes. Instead, at the end of the chain, the RBA reduces one bank's ESA and increases the other's by the same amount. That is settlement: the point at which the debt between the banks is finally extinguished.

Real-time gross settlement (RTGS) means each payment is settled one by one, in full, as it arrives, out of money the payer already has in its ESA. "Gross" means not netted against other payments; "real time" means not held to the end of the day. The RBA says settlement through RITS "is achieved by the simultaneous crediting and debiting of Exchange Settlement Accounts" and RTGS transactions settled through RITS "are final and irrevocable" [S12]. RTGS was introduced in Australia on 22 June 1998 to reduce the risk of deferred high-value payments [S12].

"Multilateral net" means every bank's many payments to and from all the others are added up, and each bank settles only its single net position once.

3.3 The queue and its liquidity-saving tools

If a payer has too little in its ESA, the payment waits on the System Queue. RITS tests transactions "as they are received", settles those that pass, and keeps testing the rest in a loop [S12]. Two features save liquidity [S12]:

  • Auto-offset: if a payment has waited one minute or more, RITS looks for an offsetting payment going the other way between the same two banks and, if both can settle together leaving both in credit, settles them at once;
  • Targeted bilateral offset: two members can pick transactions to offset against each other.

Unsettled transactions are removed from the queue at the end of each day [S12].

4 Intraday liquidity: what the intraday requirement means in practice

4.1 The requirement

In the standard: "An ADI must actively manage its intraday liquidity positions and risks in order to meet payment and settlement obligations on a timely basis under both normal and stressed conditions, thus contributing to the orderly functioning of payment and settlement systems." (APS 210 para 41)

4.2 In everyday words

Intraday liquidity is the money a bank needs during the day to make payments before its incoming payments arrive. Because RTGS settles each payment in full and the ESA cannot go overdrawn, a bank that pays out A$1bn at 10:00 am but is not due to receive A$1bn until 2:00 pm must either already have A$1bn in its ESA, or borrow it for those four hours. The overdraft is not allowed, so the borrowing is done by an intraday repo with the RBA (section 8.1), or the bank holds more balance overnight.

The RBA states the point directly: ESA holders "must have sufficient funds in their ESA to ensure that their own payments and those of their customers are able to proceed throughout the day", and there are "two main sources of liquidity: overnight ESA balances and borrowing funds from the Reserve Bank under its Liquidity Facilities" [S12].

4.3 What APRA's guide adds

APRA's practice guide (guidance, not a rule) expects management to understand the rules of every payment and settlement system the ADI uses, identify the counterparties that are the main source of large flows, and identify "key times, days and circumstances where liquidity flows and possible intraday credit needs might be particularly high" (APG 210 para 40). It also says the tools should suit the ADI's business model and notes that an ADI relying heavily on collateralised funding markets may need to watch securities settlement systems as closely as RTGS positions (APG 210 para 41).

4.4 What our team does

Document 10 (Monitoring beyond the ratios) already lists intraday liquidity among the things monitored. For this document the team's part is:

  • know which of our payments run through which channel (section 3.2);
  • know who in Treasury or Payments operations runs the intraday position and what MI they produce;
  • be able to evidence that the ADI's intraday management exists and is reviewed (a policy, a daily report, a stress case).

Typical practice: Banks track the intraday low point of the ESA balance, the time of day it occurs, the largest single payments, the peak use of intraday repo, and the time by which a given share of daily payments has settled. These measures are described in the Basel Committee's paper "Monitoring tools for intraday liquidity management". Unverified: I did not read that paper for this document. Writer 14 cites it; check the paper on bis.org before quoting any measure.

6 The cash rate, interest on settlement balances and the monetary policy implementation system

6.1 The basics

The RBA wants the cash rate to sit near the target the Monetary Policy Board sets. To do that it must control the price at which banks trade ES balances. The system it uses to do this is called the monetary policy implementation system.

Before 2020, the RBA kept reserves scarce, so banks borrowed from each other and from the RBA often (a "corridor" with a ceiling and a floor around the target). After the pandemic response (bond purchases and term funding) the supply of reserves swelled: the RBA says those operations "injected nearly $500 billion of reserves into the banking system", and reserves "have since fallen by more than half" as the assets matured [S3].

6.2 The current arrangements (as read on 14 November 2026)

Element What the RBA says Source
Name of the system A demand-driven "ample reserves" system: "the supply of reserves rises and falls in response to changes in demand" [S2]
How reserves are supplied "as many reserves as banks demand via full allotment repurchase agreement (repo) auctions at its open market operations (OMO)" [S2]
Interest paid on reserves (the ES rate) "currently set at 10 basis points below the cash rate target"; the RBA "may adjust this spread" from time to time [S2]
Price of reserves from OMO (the OMO rate) 10 basis points above the cash rate target, effective 9 April 2025 (up from 5 basis points) [S17] [S3]
Overnight standing facility 25 basis points above the cash rate target [S4]

A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. So 10 basis points is 0.10 percentage points.

How the three rates fit together. A bank with spare reserves can leave them at the RBA and earn the ES rate, so no bank will lend in the cash market below that rate: it is a floor. A bank short of reserves can obtain them from OMO at the OMO rate, so no bank will pay much more than that in the market: it is a soft ceiling [S2]. The cash rate trades between the two and near the target.

7 Open market operations and repos

7.1 What a repo is

A repurchase agreement (repo) is a loan secured by securities, written as a sale and a promised buy-back. The RBA explains: "Under a repo, the seller agrees to repurchase an equivalent security at a future time and at a pre-agreed price. The transaction is similar to a secured loan, with the difference between the purchase and repurchase prices (the price differential) akin to the interest earned on a secured loan" [S2].

From the bank's side, the bank sells securities to the RBA now and receives cash in its ESA, then buys them back later at a higher price. The RBA calls the operation a "reverse repo" from its own side, because it is the buyer [S5].

7.2 How the weekly open market operation works [S5]

  1. Announcement. Each Wednesday (or the next good business day) at 9.20 am the RBA announces its dealing intentions on market data services. Terms are typically 7 and 28 days; longer terms may be offered. Repos are normally floating rate, priced as a spread to the cash rate target.
  2. Approach. Eligible counterparties approach the RBA, usually by the deadline of 9.35 am. The minimum size per approach and term is A$20 million in increments of A$1 million.
  3. Allocation. "Subject to the Reserve Bank's discretion, approaches will be allocated in full." That is what "full allotment" means.
  4. Notification. The RBA aims to notify by 10.15 am, but does not guarantee it.
  5. Confirmation and settlement. The bank enters the repo details into Austraclear, the securities depository. Settlement is same-day value and transactions are settled before the Daily Settlement Session closes at 4.30 pm.

The repo price differential on a floating rate repo accrues daily on an actual/365 basis at the cash rate target effective that day plus the agreed spread [S5].

7.3 Who can take part

Counterparties must be members of both RITS and Austraclear, be subject to an appropriate level of regulation (an APRA-regulated ADI qualifies), and be able to manage transactions efficiently and in a timely way [S10]. Counterparty status is at the RBA's absolute discretion [S10].

7.4 Margins (haircuts) in one paragraph

The RBA does not lend the full market value of collateral. It divides the market value by a margin ratio to get the cash it pays: "purchase price = market value / margin ratio, where margin ratio = (1 + margin / 100)" [S8]. With a margin of 10 per cent, a security with market value 100 gets a purchase price of 91 (the RBA's own example gives A$90.91) [S8]. APS 210 calls the same idea a haircut; the RBA's word is margin. The two are applied differently (the margin is a percentage of the purchase price, whereas a haircut is usually a percentage of the market value), so never swap one for the other without converting.

7.5 Margin calls

Each business day the RBA revalues the securities it holds. If its net exposure to a counterparty exceeds A$1 million and more than 1 per cent of the net repurchase amounts, it calls for margin equal to its exposure; it also meets such calls from counterparties [S9]. Calls made before 11 am are due the same day; later calls are due the next business day [S9]. The call is met with eligible securities delivered through Austraclear.

Why we care: posting margin ties up more securities (encumbrance, document 08 and APS 210 para 38). A fall in collateral prices on a day of stress forces more securities out of the liquid asset portfolio on the very day we need them.

8 Standing facilities

8.1 What they are for

"The standing facilities are commonly used to provide payments system participants with liquidity as required to support orderly settlement of payments" [S2]. Unlike OMO (weekly, price set by the RBA), they are available bilaterally, on demand [S3]. In the RITS Regulations, repos under these facilities are called Standing Facility (SF) repos [S4].

8.2 The three SF repos [S2] [S4]

Facility Term Price Notes
Intraday SF repo Same day No price differential (a settlement fee may adjust the repurchase price) Meets payments before funds are received. A repo that runs overnight is repriced at 25 basis points over the cash rate target
Overnight SF repo One night 25 basis points over the cash rate target The RBA "expects and encourages" use "when a RITS member faces unexpected liquidity needs and cannot source liquidity on reasonable terms in private markets"
Open SF repo (being discontinued) No maturity date Rate on surplus ES balances (the ES rate) Used to hold a minimum ESA balance for after-hours payments; being ended in early 2027 (section 6.4)

8.3 Who can use them, collateral and timing

  • Who: eligible counterparties that settle payments across their own ES account. Other eligible counterparties cannot use SF repos [S4] [S10].
  • Collateral: any securities eligible for OMO can be used for intraday and overnight repos. For open repos, locally incorporated LCR institutions can use any OMO-eligible securities [S4].
  • Timing: counterparties can contract until the close of the SWIFT End Session (6.30 pm AEST, 8.30 pm AEDT unless advised). The Domestic Markets Desk is not staffed beyond that time [S4] [S11].
  • Operations: intraday repos against Australian Government and semi-government securities can be executed unilaterally in Austraclear; other collateral must be arranged with the RBA's Domestic Markets Desk [S4].
  • Testing: the RBA offers test repos (typically about A$1 million face value, overnight) of OMO, ELA and overnight SF "to ensure they are operationally prepared to access RBA facilities" [S31].

8.4 Interest on ES balances and the minimum balance

Banks that settle after-hours direct entry or NPP payments across their own ESA must hold a minimum ES balance at close of business, set by the RBA in consultation with the holder [S4]. Interest works like this [S4]:

  • the balance at midnight up to the minimum requirement plus net after-hours receipts earns the rate on surplus ES balances;
  • any shortfall from that level attracts the standing facility rate, 25 basis points above the cash rate target;
  • interest is adjusted in arrears on the first business day of each month.

The RBA states that holders who keep at least the required balance "are not considered to have received liquidity support from the Reserve Bank" [S4]. The size of the requirement is reviewed at least annually, and "The next regular review of the size of these requirements will occur in the coming months" with new requirements to take effect when open repo ends [S1] [S4].

Unverified: whether APRA counts a balance held to meet the RBA's minimum ES requirement as freely drawable HQLA1 under (Att A para 9) is not stated in any source I read. Ask your controller to confirm with APRA through Treasury; until then record the amount so it can be excluded quickly if required.

8.5 After the open repo ends

The RBA says that banks "will still be expected to hold minimum ES balances to support after-hours payments" but that "open repos are no longer needed to meet those" [S3]. It also says it "would retain the ability to reintroduce the facility" [S3].

9 Eligible collateral, margins and self-securitised assets

9.1 What the RBA accepts

The RBA publishes its criteria; securities must meet all of them on application and for the term of each transaction, and the RBA can revoke eligibility at any time [S7]. In summary [S7]:

Security Eligible for Minimum rating or note
Australian Government and semi-government securities Automatically eligible None
New Zealand Government and government-guaranteed securities Reverse repos (as collateral) None
ADI bonds over one year, including covered bonds Reverse repos Two ratings, average at least BBB minus
ADI short-term paper (under one year) Reverse repos One rating, public rating
Corporate bonds and commercial paper Reverse repos BBB (one rating) or BBB minus (two); CP also BBB or A-3

Why this table matters to the LCR: being RBA-eligible does not make a security HQLA. Only notes and coin, RBA balances, and Australian Government and semi-government securities are AUD HQLA1, and no AUD assets currently qualify as HQLA2 [S29]. Example Bank's "other debt securities (non-HQLA)" might be RBA-eligible collateral that is not HQLA. They matter for liquidity because they can be turned into HQLA1 (ESA cash) through a repo.

9.2 Self-securitised assets

A self-securitisation is a pool of the bank's own loans (usually mortgages) packaged into a special purpose vehicle, which issues securities that the bank itself buys. They are not for sale to investors. They exist so that the bank has a security the RBA will lend against. Document 19 (The standards around APS 210) covers the securitisation standard, APS 120.

What the RBA says about them [S7] [S8] [S2]:

  • The RBA "considers self-securitised asset-backed securities to be materially related to the ADI whose receivables have been securitised" [S7].
  • The RBA's eligible-securities page says it can grant a related-party exemption "in repos contracted under Exceptional Liquidity Assistance, but not in open market operations or under the Reserve Bank's Standing Facilities" [S7].
  • Since 1 April 2025 the RBA has required, for new self-securitisations, certificates from a senior officer (Chief Financial Officer, Group Treasurer or equivalent); existing ones need an annual certificate between 31 January and 31 March each year [S7].
  • Self-securitisations that report late incur a 15 per cent penalty haircut [S7]. The RBA tells APRA about late reporting and late certificates [S7].
  • If collections are not remitted at least weekly, an additional discount applies: 3 percentage points (weekly to monthly), 9 (monthly to quarterly) or 18 (less often than quarterly) [S8].
  • The RBA's test repos may include self-securitisations at its discretion [S31].

10 The Committed Liquidity Facility: history, mechanics and why it ended

10.1 The problem it solved

Under Basel III, banks must hold high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) against 30 days of stress. In Australia the only HQLA securities are Commonwealth and state government bonds. The RBA explains that "the supply of AGS and semis has not been sufficient for banks to meet the LCR requirement"; in 2015, "banks would have had to hold around two-thirds of the value of all AGS and semis outstanding" [S19]. Forcing banks to hold that much "would very likely interfere with the properties of the market that qualified it as HQLA in the first place" [S19].

The Basel standards allow an alternative treatment where HQLA supply is short. The Committed Liquidity Facility (CLF) was the RBA and APRA's alternative [S19]. The RBA Board approved it in November 2010 and it was announced the following month [S19]. It was available from 2015.

11 The Term Funding Facility

11.1 What it was

The Term Funding Facility (TFF) was a pandemic-era RBA scheme announced on 19 March 2020. It offered three-year funding to ADIs through repurchase transactions, with two objectives: to lower ADIs' funding costs and so borrowers' interest rates, and to give ADIs access to extra low-cost funding if they expanded lending to businesses, with stronger incentives for small and medium-sized enterprises [S21].

11.2 Key terms [S21] [S22]

Feature Detail

Collateral "all collateral currently eligible for the Reserve Bank's domestic market operations", which "will include self-securitised asset-backed securities"; haircuts applied through margin ratios
Allowance An Initial Allowance of 3 per cent of an ADI's credit to Australian households and businesses, plus an Additional Allowance linked to growth in business lending (one times the increase in large business credit and five times the increase in SME credit)

The September 2020 expansion added a A$57 billion supplementary allowance and moved the last drawdown date to 30 June 2021 [S24]. 11.3 Why a liquidity team still cares

  1. It filled reserves. The RBA says its bond purchases and term funding operations "injected nearly $500 billion of reserves" and that reserves rose and then fell as these matured; reserves fell by around A$110 billion over the year to early 2025, and the RBA says most of this was the final repayment of the TFF in June 2024 [S3] [S17].
  2. It changed how banks think about RBA funding. The TFF showed that RBA funding against self-securitised assets was usable in practice, which is part of why APRA asks banks to hold a pool of them (APG 210 para 67).
  3. It is in the data. Historical ratios and funding mixes from 2020 to 2024 include TFF funding. Where you compare Example Bank's NSFR with history, check whether TFF repayment explains a step change. The NSFR counted funding from central banks with a residual maturity of 6 to under 12 months at 50 per cent ASF and under 6 months at zero (Att C para 14) and (Att C para 15).

Typical practice: If your team keeps long time series, annotate the dates the TFF was drawn and repaid.

12 Exceptional Liquidity Assistance

12.1 What it is, in everyday words

Exceptional Liquidity Assistance (ELA) is emergency cash from the central bank to a bank that has run out of other options. The word "exceptional" is deliberate: it is meant to be very rare.

12.2 What the RBA says [S2] [S4] [S16]

  • "In exceptionally rare circumstances, when an Eligible Counterparty is experiencing acute liquidity difficulties, but is solvent, the Reserve Bank may provide ELA if it is judged to be needed to contribute to the stability of the Australian financial system." [S4]
  • It would "generally be done through a term ELA repo for a short period of time", and the provision and the terms, "including acceptable collateral, is at the absolute discretion of the Reserve Bank" [S4] [S2].
  • Any eligible counterparty, including those that do not settle across their own ESA, may request ELA, but each ELA repo needs the RBA's agreement [S4].
  • Collateral: securities eligible for the RBA's domestic market operations would be acceptable; exemptions from the related-party guidelines (for example for self-securitisations) "would be considered on a case-by-case basis" [S4] [S7].
  • Minutes of the Monetary Policy Board of 3 and 4 November 2025 record that "the provision of ELA is envisaged to be exceptionally rare", that "the operational arrangements for ELA would remain as before", and that a key criterion would be the Board's judgement that ELA "was needed to contribute to the stability of the Australian financial system" [S16].

12.3 What the RBA expects from the bank asking

In support of a request, the RBA says an entity would be expected to [S4]:

  1. "inform their regulator immediately of any liquidity concerns and their intention to request ELA, prior to approaching the Reserve Bank";
  2. "have already made reasonable efforts to access private sector sources of liquidity"; and
  3. "present evidence of their solvency, including an attestation of solvency or positive net worth".

For testing, the RBA runs test ELA repos, and for those tests "the attestation and documentation requirements are waived" [S4].

12.4 What APS 210 and APRA add

In the standard: "An ADI under severe liquidity stress may request Exceptional Liquidity Assistance (ELA) from the Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA). Prior to approaching the RBA, the ADI must notify APRA immediately of the ADI's liquidity concerns and its intention to request ELA. An ADI must provide any information requested by APRA or the RBA in relation to the ADI's ELA request. An ADI must have the operational capability to provide information along with its application for ELA or within other timeframes specified by APRA." (APS 210 para 70)

12.5 What our team should have ready

Typical practice: Because the information must be provided "within other timeframes specified by APRA" (APS 210 para 70), which may be hours, banks usually:

  1. keep a named owner for the ELA data pack and a named deputy;
  2. map each template item to a source system and a query that already exists;
  3. keep a list of the securities the RBA would accept (Treasury), with the latest margin schedule, so the available collateral value and the likely cash can be estimated quickly;
  4. run an annual rehearsal under time pressure, and save the results as evidence for APG 210 para 76;
  5. include the ELA step in the contingency funding plan (APS 210 paras 47 to 51) so that it sits after private-market options, because the RBA expects reasonable efforts to use them first [S4].

12.6 How ELA affects our numbers

  • LCR. ELA is not HQLA. It is a secured loan that, if drawn, becomes an ordinary secured funding outflow for the term of the transaction. If the term extends beyond 30 days it does not fall in the LCR window. The Att A para 5 statement about drawing on the CLF to cover outflows applies to the CLF only, which is zero.
  • NSFR. Assets encumbered for exceptional central bank liquidity operations may be given a lower RSF factor "if approval is sought from APRA and APRA agrees" (Att C para 24). The standard describes exceptional operations as "non-standard, temporary operations conducted by a central bank ... in a period of market-wide financial stress and/or exceptional macroeconomic circumstances" (Att C footnote 22). The ELA test repo and other routine repos are not exceptional operations.

13 The Council of Financial Regulators and a crisis

13.1 Who they are

The Council of Financial Regulators (CFR) is "the main coordinating body for Australia's financial regulators". It has four members: the RBA, APRA, the Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC) and the Treasury [S26]. It coordinates; it does not itself lend or supervise.

13.2 Its role in a crisis

A Memorandum of Understanding on crisis management, agreed by the Council on 24 July 2025, replaced the 2008 version [S25]. Its main points [S25]:

Agency Lead responsibility in a stress
RBA Monitoring systemic risk (financial markets, clearing and settlement facilities, and the payments system). Assessing the scale of stress there, and "evaluating and, where appropriate, implementing response options that involve liquidity support and financial market intervention"
APRA Prudentially supervising banks, insurers and superannuation funds; assessing stress in a supervised institution; deciding any supervisory response or resolution, including directions, appointing a statutory manager to a bank, and administering the Financial Claims Scheme if invoked
ASIC Regulatory implications for markets and investors, disclosure, liaising with market operators
Treasury Advising the Treasurer and the Government, coordinating collective advice on any response including Government action

Principles: "Private sector or market-based solutions are generally the Council's preferred means of crisis management", although a public response may be needed where systemic risk could materialise; communication is coordinated; the Council maintains "playbooks and protocols" for collective responses [S25].

13.3 What it means for us

  • The first call in our own crisis is to APRA (APS 210 para 70) and (Att A para 5), not to the RBA.
  • APRA's practice guide expects APRA and the RBA to be notified "as soon as practicable" if the ADI is facing or considers it is about to face a retail run (APG 210 para 74).
  • The RBA provides liquidity; APRA decides on supervisory action; the Treasurer decides on Government action. A bank's request for ELA does not decide any of those.

Unverified: I did not read the RBA and APRA bilateral memorandum of understanding. Its content, and any liquidity-specific protocol in the CFR playbooks, is not public in what I read. Ask the controller if our crisis playbook cites either.

14.4 Worked calculation 1: the LCR effect of an RBA repo

Setup. Start with Example Bank's base position from the original brief: HQLA A$17.0bn (cash and RBA balances 5.0 plus government and semi-government securities 12.0), outflows 13.45, inflows 1.90, net outflows 11.55, LCR 147.2%, surplus 5.45.

Treasury is considering raising A$2.0bn of cash from the RBA. All margins below are assumptions chosen for the example.

Case A: 28-day OMO repo against non-HQLA bonds.

  1. Collateral: Example Bank's A$3.0bn of "other debt securities" are assumed to be ADI-issued, rated A minus, with 1 to 5 years to maturity, and RBA-eligible. Margin from the RBA's table: 14 per cent [S8].
  2. To receive A$2.0bn the bank pledges market value of 2.0 x 1.14 = A$2.28bn.
  3. HQLA: 17.0 + 2.0 = 19.0 (the bonds were not HQLA before, so nothing leaves the stock).
  4. Outflows: secured funding that is not backed by HQLA at 100 per cent of 2.0 = 2.00, because the repo matures within 30 days (Att A Table 3). Total outflows 13.45 + 2.00 = 15.45.
  5. Inflow cap: 75 per cent of 15.45 = 11.5875, which is far above inflows of 1.90, so inflows stay 1.90 (Att A para 30). Net outflows = 15.45 − 1.90 = 13.55.
  6. LCR = 19.0 / 13.55 = 140.2%. Surplus = 19.0 − 13.55 = 5.45.

Result: the ratio falls from 147.2% to 140.2% while the dollar surplus is unchanged at A$5.45bn. Short repo adds to both the numerator and the denominator by the same amount, which moves any ratio above 100 per cent towards 100.

Case B: the same A$2.0bn raised for 90 days (hypothetical; today's regular OMO terms are 7 and 28 days, and RBA TROs at three and six months are expected in 2027 [S5] [S6]).

  1. HQLA: 17.0 + 2.0 = 19.0.
  2. Outflows inside the 30-day window: none from this repo. Outflows 13.45; net 13.45 − 1.90 = 11.55.
  3. LCR = 19.0 / 11.55 = 164.5%. Surplus 7.45.

Result: term funding that matures outside the window improves the ratio, which is the whole idea behind the NSFR (document 09).

Case C: 28-day repo against Australian Government bonds (HQLA1).

  1. Margin for Australian Government bonds, 1 to 5 years: 2 per cent [S8]. To receive 2.0 the bank pledges market value 2.0 x 1.02 = A$2.04bn.
  2. HQLA: cash and RBA balances 5.0 + 2.0 = 7.0; government and semi-government securities 12.0 − 2.04 = 9.96; total 16.96.
  3. Outflows: HQLA1-backed secured funding at 0 per cent (Att A Table 3). Net outflows stay 11.55.
  4. LCR = 16.96 / 11.55 = 146.8%.

Result: the ratio falls only by the cost of the margin (2.04 − 2.00 = 0.04), because both the cash received and the securities pledged are HQLA1 and there is no outflow.

Check yourself

  1. What is an Exchange Settlement Account, and why does APS 210 treat a balance in it as HQLA1? Answer: It is an account a bank holds at the RBA to settle payments with other banks. A balance in it is settlement money already, with no price risk, so it is HQLA1 "to the extent that these balances may be drawn down in times of stress" (Att A para 9). It also has a zero RSF factor (Att C para 29).

  2. Why must an ESA never be overdrawn, and what does a bank do when it is short for part of a day? Answer: RTGS settles each payment in full from funds already held, and ESAs must be in credit at all times [S12] [S13]. The bank holds more balance, or contracts an intraday SF repo with the RBA, which has no price differential [S4]. This is the practical meaning of APS 210 para 41.

  3. If the cash rate target is 4.85 per cent, the ES rate is 10 basis points below it and the overnight standing facility is 25 basis points above it, what are those two rates, and what is one day's interest on A$4.0bn at the ES rate? Answer: ES rate 4.75 per cent; overnight facility 5.10 per cent. Interest = 4,000,000,000 x 4.75% / 365 = A$520,548 (sections 6 and 14.6).

  4. A repo of Australian Government bonds with market value A$2.04bn at a 2 per cent margin: how much cash does the bank receive? Answer: Cash = market value / (1 + margin) = 2.04 / 1.02 = A$2.0bn [S8].

  5. Example Bank raises A$2.0bn from the RBA for 28 days against non-HQLA bonds. Start from HQLA 17.0 and net outflows 11.55. What is the new LCR and the surplus? Answer: HQLA 17.0 + 2.0 = 19.0; outflows rise by 100 per cent of 2.0, so net outflows are 11.55 + 2.0 = 13.55 (the inflow cap is not binding: 75 per cent of 15.45 = 11.59). LCR = 19.0 / 13.55 = 140.2%; surplus = 5.45, unchanged (Att A Table 3).

  6. The same A$2.0bn is raised for 90 days instead. What is the LCR, and why does it differ from question 5? Answer: 19.0 / 11.55 = 164.5%. No outflow arises inside the 30-day window, so the denominator is unchanged.

  7. Using the NSFR base (ASF 79.75, RSF 58.50), what happens if the RBA repo in question 5 is a six-month repo and the pledged bonds carry an 85 per cent RSF factor? Answer: ASF rises by 2.0 x 50 per cent = 1.00 to 80.75 (Att C para 14); RSF is unchanged because the cash is 0 per cent (Att C para 29) and the bonds stay at 85 per cent (Att C para 23). NSFR = 80.75 / 58.50 = 138.0%.

  8. What must an ADI do under APS 210 para 70 before it approaches the RBA for ELA, and what must it be able to do? Answer: It must notify APRA immediately of its liquidity concerns and its intention to request ELA; provide any information requested by APRA or the RBA; and have the operational capability to provide information with its application or within APRA's timeframe (APS 210 para 70). APRA expects annual testing (APG 210 para 76).

  9. Name two things the RBA expects of a bank requesting ELA besides notifying its regulator. Answer: That it has already made reasonable efforts to access private sector sources of liquidity, and that it presents evidence of solvency, including an attestation of solvency or positive net worth [S4].

  10. Why did the CLF end, and what remains in APS 210? Answer: APRA and the RBA considered that there were enough HQLA for locally incorporated LCR ADIs, so the aggregate CLF was reduced to zero as at 1 January 2023 [S20]. The paragraphs (Att A paras 13 to 21) remain in the standard.

What to remember

  • An ESA balance is settlement money at the RBA: HQLA1, zero RSF, and never allowed to go overdrawn (Att A para 9) and (Att C para 29).
  • RTGS settles payments one at a time and in full; intraday liquidity is the cash needed to bridge gaps during the day (APS 210 para 41).
  • The RBA now supplies reserves through full-allotment weekly OMOs at 10 basis points over the cash rate target, pays the ES rate 10 basis points below it, and lends overnight at 25 basis points over it [S2] [S4] [S17].
  • Open SF repos end in early 2027; Term Repo Operations are expected in 2027. Re-check both (section 16).
  • Being RBA-eligible does not make a security HQLA; the only AUD HQLA1 are notes and coin, RBA balances, and Australian Government and semi-government securities [S29].
  • A short repo adds the same amount to HQLA and to outflows, so the LCR falls toward 100 per cent while the surplus is unchanged; a repo beyond 30 days improves the LCR (Att A Table 3).
  • The CLF is zero since 1 January 2023 but its paragraphs remain (Att A paras 13 to 21).
  • ELA is emergency, discretionary and rare. APS 210 para 70 requires immediate notice to APRA and an operational capability to provide information; test it at least annually (APG 210 para 76).
  • The Council of Financial Regulators coordinates a crisis; the RBA leads on liquidity support and APRA on supervision.
