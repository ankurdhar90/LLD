10 Monitoring beyond the two ratios

Purpose: To teach you everything the Liquidity Reporting team and its neighbours watch besides the liquidity coverage ratio (LCR) and the net stable funding ratio (NSFR): the maturity ladder, funding concentration, limits and early warning indicators, stress tests, the funding strategy, the contingency funding plan, intraday liquidity, collateral, currency, cost allocation, management information, Level 1 and Level 2, and the minimum liquidity holdings (MLH) regime.

You will be able to:

  • Build a contractual and a behavioural maturity ladder from a balance sheet and read the cumulative gap.
  • Explain what each early warning indicator signals, where its data comes from, and how a limit and a trigger differ.
  • Describe how a stress test is built, what a survival horizon is, and how the results feed limits and the contingency funding plan.
  • List the data the team supplies for the funding strategy, the contingency funding plan, collateral, intraday and currency monitoring.
  • Assemble a daily liquidity dashboard and a monthly ALCO pack (ALCO is the asset and liability committee).
  • Say when Level 1 and Level 2 differ, and recognise the MLH regime when you meet it.

Before you start: Read document 02 (Liquidity from zero) and document 03 (Your role, the team and the working year). Documents 08 (Producing the daily LCR) and 09 (Producing the NSFR) will help, but you can read this one first.

Time: About three hours to read; allow another two to work the examples and the questions.

1. Why two ratios are not enough

You will spend much of your time on two numbers: the LCR and the NSFR. Both are valuable. Both also have limits, and the standard knows it.

  • The LCR looks at 30 days. It cannot see a cliff of debt falling due in month four.
  • The NSFR looks at one year and at the shape of the balance sheet. It cannot see today's cash.
  • Both are snapshots at a date. Neither shows how the position will move.
  • Both use regulatory assumptions. The bank's own stress could be worse, or different, from APRA's scenario.

APS 210 therefore asks for more than ratios. The bank must have "a robust framework for comprehensively projecting cash flows arising from assets, liabilities and off-balance sheet items over an appropriate set of time horizons" (APS 210 para 36). Think of the two ratios as the dashboard warning lights, and everything in this document as the rest of the instruments: the fuel gauge, the engine temperature and the map of the road ahead.

2. The maturity ladder

2.1 What it is, in everyday words

Imagine your household finances laid out on a calendar. Rent is due on the 1st. Your salary arrives on the 15th. The car loan instalment goes out on the 20th. If you lined up every payment you expect to make and every payment you expect to receive, week by week, you would see weeks where you are short and weeks where you are comfortable. That calendar is a maturity ladder.

A bank does the same thing, with its balance sheet. Each asset will pay the bank back at some date. Each liability must be paid by the bank at some date. The ladder sorts them into time buckets (for example "at call to 30 days", "31 days to under 6 months") and shows, for each bucket, the difference between cash coming in and cash going out.

  • The net gap in a bucket is inflows minus outflows. A negative gap means more cash must go out than comes in during that bucket.
  • The cumulative gap is the running total of the net gaps from the first bucket. It shows how much funding the bank has needed by the end of each bucket.
Net gap in bucket        = Inflows in bucket - Outflows in bucket
Cumulative gap at bucket = Sum of net gaps from the first bucket to this one

Another name for the ladder is the liquidity gap report or maturity mismatch report. APRA collects a contractual version in its returns (see document 11, Returns to APRA and public disclosure). The standard's wording about projecting cash flows "over an appropriate set of time horizons" is the reason a bank must have one (APS 210 para 36). The standard does not set the buckets; the bank chooses them.

2.2 Contractual and behavioural: two different pictures

The contractual ladder places each cash flow at the date written in the contract.

  • A term deposit that matures in 4 months goes in the 4-month bucket.
  • A deposit that can be withdrawn on demand goes in the "at call" bucket, even if most customers leave it there for years.
  • A 25-year mortgage goes in the "1 year or more" bucket, except for the scheduled repayments that fall due in earlier buckets.

The contractual ladder is objective and easy to audit. It is also brutally pessimistic on the liability side, because every at-call deposit is treated as if it could leave tomorrow.

The behavioural ladder places each cash flow where management expects it to happen, using observed customer behaviour.

  • Most at-call deposits are "sticky". Past data shows that a stable transaction account balance rarely falls fast. A behavioural ladder spreads it over several buckets.
  • Mortgages are often repaid early. A behavioural ladder shortens their life.

The behavioural ladder is more realistic, but it rests on assumptions, and assumptions need owners, evidence and review.

From the controller: The contractual ladder tells you the worst case if everybody asks for their money tomorrow. The behavioural ladder tells you what you think will happen. The gap between them is exactly the amount of judgement in the numbers. Always show both, and always label which one you are showing.

A third view adds the liquid assets that could be turned into cash. Many banks call it the liquidity-adjusted or counterbalancing capacity view. It moves liquid assets (for example government bonds) into the earliest bucket, because they can be sold or lent against (repoed) within days regardless of their final maturity. The LCR uses the same idea: HQLA counts at once, whatever its maturity (Att A para 6).

2.3 Building the ladder: a procedure

Trigger: Daily or weekly production for Treasury; monthly for ALCO; quarterly for the APRA contractual maturity mismatch forms (document 11).

Inputs:

  1. Balance sheet by product, with the residual maturity (time left to the contractual date) of every asset and liability.
  2. Contractual repayment schedules for loans and securities.
  3. Off-balance sheet items: undrawn committed facilities, derivatives, guarantees.
  4. Behavioural assumptions (document owned by Treasury and approved through ALCO), with their date of last review.
  5. The liquid asset register with market values and encumbrance flags (document 07, Data and classification).

Steps:

  1. Load the position at the reporting date and reconcile it to the general ledger. Total assets must equal total liabilities and equity.
  2. Assign each line to a bucket by its residual maturity. Use the bank's fixed bucket definitions, the same every time.
  3. Where a product has no contractual maturity (for example other assets, equity, or at-call deposits on the contractual view), apply the bank's written rule (typically at call, or the last bucket).
  4. Compute inflows and outflows per bucket, then the net gap and the cumulative gap.
  5. Repeat on the behavioural view, replacing contractual dates with the approved behavioural profile.
  6. Add the liquidity-adjusted view by moving unencumbered liquid assets to the first bucket at market value less haircut.
  7. Compare each cumulative gap with its limit and trigger.
  8. Write a movement commentary against the last report.

Controls:

  • Total of all buckets equals the ledger totals (assets and liabilities separately).
  • No line unassigned; no line in two buckets.
  • Bucket totals move by plausible amounts from the last report, or the movement is explained.
  • Behavioural assumptions equal the approved set (version-checked).
  • The liquid assets moved to the first bucket agree to the LCR's HQLA before haircuts (otherwise explain the difference).

Outputs: The ladder table, a chart of the cumulative gap, a limit status and a commentary.

Who reviews: A second analyst (four-eyes rule: one person prepares, a second reviews, the controller signs); Treasury reviews the assumptions; Liquidity Risk reviews the limit status.

Evidence kept: The input extract, reconciliation, the assumption table with version, the ladder file, the review sign-off.

3. Funding concentration

3.1 What it is

Concentration means a large share of something comes from one place. Funding concentration is dependence on one funder, product, market, currency or maturity date. Think of a shop that gets 60% of its sales from one customer. If that customer leaves, the shop is in trouble. A bank that funds itself from a few large depositors or one bond market is in the same position.

The standard asks the liquidity management strategy to cover "the diversity and stability of funding sources" (APS 210 para 29(b)). It also lists "increasing funding costs or concentrations" among the new and emerging concerns that must be brought to the Board and senior management immediately (APS 210 para 33). So concentration is a monitored item, with escalation.

In the standard: APS 210 does not set a numerical concentration limit. A threshold of 1% of liabilities for a "significant" funder comes from the Basel Committee's liquidity monitoring tools, as generally understood (confirm with Liquidity Risk before you cite it as a rule). It decides what to report on; it is not a ceiling.

3.3 Two measures you can compute

Top-N share.

Top-10 share of deposits = Sum of the ten largest depositor balances / Total deposits

Illustration (hypothetical numbers): if the ten largest corporate non-operational depositors together hold A$2.4bn of the A$6.0bn line, the top-10 share of that line is 2.4 / 6.0 = 40%. As a share of all deposits (69.0), it is 2.4 / 69.0 = 3.5%.

Herfindahl-Hirschman index (HHI). The HHI adds up the squares of each source's percentage share. It rises quickly when one source dominates.

HHI = (share1 in %)^2 + (share2 in %)^2 + ...

Illustration (hypothetical): five wholesale funders with shares 40%, 25%, 15%, 10% and 10%. HHI = 1,600 + 625 + 225 + 100 + 100 = 2,650. If the shares were equal (20% each) the HHI would be 5 x 400 = 2,000, the lowest possible for five funders. A higher figure than 2,000 shows concentration. The bank sets its own warning level (typical practice; confirm with your controller).

3.4 Producing the concentration report

Trigger: Monthly for ALCO; daily for the largest-depositor list used in the early warning set.

Inputs: Deposit balances by customer (core banking); wholesale funding by counterparty and maturity (treasury system); customer master for group linking (several accounts owned by one group count as one funder).

Steps:

  1. Extract balances by customer and by product at the reporting date.
  2. Link related customers into groups using the customer master. If grouping is missing, record it as a limitation.
  3. Rank funders; compute top-1, top-5, top-10 and top-20 shares.
  4. Compute the HHI for each funding type.
  5. Flag every funder above the bank's significant-funder threshold.
  6. Compute the maturity concentration of wholesale debt by month for the next 12 months.
  7. Compare with limits and triggers; write the commentary.

Controls: Totals reconcile to the ledger by product; each customer appears once; groups are complete; the top-10 list is reviewed by a second person; privacy rules are observed (customer names are masked outside the team that needs them).

Outputs: Concentration table and chart; list of significant funders; limit status.

Who reviews: Second analyst, then controller; Liquidity Risk gets the pack.

Evidence kept: Extract, grouping file, calculation file, review record.

Watch out: An intermediated deposit (a deposit placed through an agent such as a platform) may belong to many underlying customers or to the intermediary, depending on the structure (Att A para 35). Check how the data is mapped before you rank "customers".

4. Limits and early warning indicators

4.1 What a limit is, in everyday words

A limit is a line the bank agrees not to cross. A speed limit is the obvious example. The standard says the bank "must set limits to control its liquidity risk exposure and vulnerabilities", must review limits and their escalation procedures regularly, and must make them relevant to its location, complexity, products, currencies and markets (APS 210 para 37). When a limit is breached, the bank "must implement a plan of action to review the exposure and reduce it to a level that is within the limit" (APS 210 para 37).

Senior management must also set "specific procedures and approvals necessary for exceptions to policies and limits, including escalation procedures and follow-up actions to be taken for breaches of limits" (APS 210 para 19(g)). And the Board and senior management must be told immediately about "material and/or persistent breaches of limits" (APS 210 para 33). The stress test results must be "explicitly considered in the setting of internal limits" (APS 210 para 65).

4.2 Three layers: regulatory, appetite, trigger

Layers of lines stack up. For Example Bank (all values below are Example Bank conventions):

Layer LCR NSFR Who sets it What happens when crossed
Regulatory minimum 100% (APS 210 para 55) 100% (APS 210 para 60) APRA Immediate notification to APRA (Att A para 5)
Board risk appetite 120% 110% Board Limit breach: plan of action (APS 210 para 37), report to ALCO and Board Risk Committee
Management early-warning trigger (amber) 125% 115% ALCO or management Escalate to Treasury and Liquidity Risk the same day; assess response

The reason for stacking is simple. You want to see trouble coming while you still have room to act. A bank that only reacts at 100% has no room left. The standard says so directly: an LCR ADI "must include an appropriate buffer of HQLA over the LCR requirement in line with its liquidity risk tolerance" (Att A para 4), and the NSFR buffer follows the same logic (Att C para 4).

Example: Example Bank's LCR is 147.2%, HQLA 17.0, net outflows 11.55. The amber trigger at 125% needs HQLA of 1.25 x 11.55 = 14.4375. The headroom to amber is 17.0 - 14.4375 = 2.5625, about A$2.56bn. The Board appetite at 120% needs 1.20 x 11.55 = 13.86, so headroom to appetite is 3.14. The regulatory minimum at 100% needs 11.55, headroom 5.45.

4.4 Early warning indicators

An early warning indicator (EWI) is a measure that moves before trouble arrives, the way a rising temperature warns of a fever. The standard requires the bank to "design a set of early warning indicators to aid its daily liquidity risk management processes in identifying the emergence of increased risk or vulnerabilities in its liquidity risk position or potential funding needs" (APS 210 para 39). The set must help to "identify and escalate any negative trends" and "lead to an assessment and potential response by management" (APS 210 para 39).

Notice the word "daily". The set is part of the daily routine. The indicators fall into groups: balance sheet and funding, customer behaviour, market signals, and operational and reputational signals.

Producing the EWI dashboard.

4.5 Limit breaches in short

When a limit is breached (the full procedure is in document 12, Controls, incidents and escalation):

  1. Tell your controller immediately, and record the time.
  2. Treasury and Liquidity Risk assess the cause and the exposure.
  3. Management prepares a plan of action to bring the exposure back within the limit (APS 210 para 37).
  4. Exceptions require the approvals that senior management has set (APS 210 para 19(g)).
  5. A material or persistent breach goes to the Board and senior management immediately (APS 210 para 33).

Watch out: You never adjust a number to avoid a breach. If a figure looks wrong, correct the data through the change process and record it. Document 03 covers professional conduct.

5. Stress testing

5.1 What a stress test is, in everyday words

A stress test asks "what if?" about something bad. A builder tests a bridge by loading it with more weight than it will ever carry. A bank tests its liquidity by imagining bad events and counting how long its cash lasts. The LCR is one fixed stress test set by APRA: a severe 30-day stress with prescribed rates. The standard requires more. An LCR ADI must conduct stress tests "on a regular basis for a variety of short-term and protracted institution-specific and market-wide stress scenarios (individually and in combination) to identify sources of potential liquidity strain and to ensure that current exposures remain in accordance with the ADI's liquidity risk tolerance" (APS 210 para 62).

The four words that matter in that sentence:

Term Meaning Example
Institution-specific (also called idiosyncratic) Trouble that hits this bank only A credit rating downgrade; a data breach that shakes customer trust
Market-wide Trouble that hits everyone Funding markets shut; government bond prices fall
Combined Both at once The bank is downgraded as markets close
Short-term and protracted Lasting days or lasting months A 10-day shock, or a 90-day squeeze

The standard adds that stress tests "must enable the ADI to analyse the impact of stress scenarios on its Level 1 and Level 2 liquidity positions, as well as on the liquidity positions of individual business lines" (APS 210 para 64). The wider framework must hold "a cushion of unencumbered liquid assets, to withstand a range of stress events, including those involving the loss or impairment of both unsecured and secured funding sources" (APS 210 para 26). Note "secured funding" in that sentence: a stress test must also ask what happens if repo markets stop working, or collateral values fall.

5.2 The building blocks

Every scenario is made of the same parts.

  1. Narrative. A short story of what happens, so people can judge whether it is plausible and severe enough.
  2. Duration. For example 5, 30, 90 or 365 days.
  3. Shocks (assumptions). For each line of the balance sheet, a stressed behaviour: the share of each deposit type that leaves, the share of wholesale debt that cannot be rolled, the share of committed facilities drawn, the haircut on each asset class, the collateral called on a downgrade.
  4. Counterbalancing capacity. The liquid assets and other sources the bank could turn into cash, after haircuts, and the time that takes.
  5. Output. The net cash position day by day (or bucket by bucket), the lowest point, and the survival horizon.

The survival horizon is the number of days the bank can meet every payment before its liquid resources run out. The LCR's horizon is fixed at 30 days (Att A para 1). The bank's own stress tests find out whether the real horizon is longer or shorter under harsher assumptions.

5.3 A worked example: the combined stress

Start from Example Bank's regulatory position: HQLA 17.0, net cash outflows over 30 days 11.55. Suppose Treasury and Liquidity Risk design an internal "combined" scenario that makes outflows 1.5 times as severe as the LCR scenario over 30 days. (The multiplier is invented for training: typical practice; confirm with your controller. Real scenarios shock each line separately, not the total.)

Stressed 30-day net outflow = 1.5 x 11.55            = 17.325
Average daily net outflow   = 17.325 / 30            = 0.5775
Survival horizon            = HQLA / daily outflow   = 17.0 / 0.5775 = 29.4 days

Suppose the internal tolerance says the bank must survive at least 30 days in this scenario. It falls short by 0.6 of a day. The extra HQLA required is 17.325 - 17.0 = A$0.325bn. Management's options: hold A$0.325bn more HQLA; lengthen the maturity of funding that falls in the window; lower the exposure (for example cap undrawn facilities); or accept the tolerance and record the decision. This is exactly what the standard means when it says "stress test outcomes must be used to adjust the ADI's liquidity management strategy, policies and positions" (APS 210 para 63).

Watch out: A straight-line survival horizon (as above) is a teaching shortcut. Real stress paths are uneven: the biggest outflows come early. A proper horizon is read from the day on which the projected cumulative net position first goes below the available resources.

A market-closure scenario (12 months, illustrative). Suppose all wholesale markets are shut for a year and no new term funding can be raised. Assumptions (invented): wholesale debt maturing in the year cannot be rolled; half of retail term deposits are not renewed; 5% of non-bank at-call deposits leave.

Assumption Calculation A$bn
Wholesale debt maturing within 1 year 1.0 + 3.0 + 2.0 6.0
Retail term deposits not renewed 50% x 10.0 5.0
At-call deposits leaving (retail 25.0 + 15.0, SME 5.0, corporate 4.0 + 6.0 = 55.0) 5% x 55.0 2.75
Total funding to be replaced 13.75

Resources: HQLA 17.0, plus other debt securities of 3.0 repoed or sold at an assumed 85% value = 2.55. Total 19.55. Surplus after the stress: 19.55 - 13.75 = A$5.80bn. The scenario is survived. Without the other debt securities the surplus would be 17.0 - 13.75 = 3.25.

5.4 Reverse stress testing

A reverse stress test starts from the end result: "the bank runs out of liquid assets on day 20". It then asks what combination of events would cause it. This finds weaknesses a forward scenario can miss. For Example Bank you could ask: how far would corporate non-operational deposits (A$6.0bn) and financial institution deposits (A$4.0bn) have to fall before HQLA of A$17.0bn is exhausted, if retail behaves as in the LCR? The answer defines a vulnerability that management should know about.

The standard sets four links.

In practice:

  • Limits. If the combined scenario shows a survival horizon of 29.4 days against a 30-day tolerance, the limit set (for example a minimum HQLA surplus or a wholesale maturity cap) is reviewed.
  • Contingency funding plan. The sizes of the stressed outflows set the size of the actions the plan must provide (section 7).
  • Funding strategy. If the stress needs more term funding, the three-year strategy must show it (section 6).
  • APRA. APRA's liquidity FAQ states that APRA "does not require regular reporting of stress test results but may request them at any time", as retrieved from apra.gov.au. The standard still says results must be reported to and discussed with APRA (APS 210 para 65), so keep a current, ready-to-send summary (see document 11 for how to answer APRA requests).

6. The three-year funding strategy

6.1 What it is

A funding strategy is the bank's plan for where its money will come from, and in what mix, over the next few years. The standard requires an ADI to "develop and document a three-year funding strategy, which must be provided to APRA on request" (APS 210 para 44(a)). The Board must review and approve it "at least annually" with "robust assumptions in line with the ADI's liquidity management strategy and business objectives" (APS 210 para 45). It must be updated at least annually "to account for, at a minimum, changed funding conditions and/or a change in the ADI's strategy", and the ADI "must advise APRA of any material changes" (APS 210 para 46). The funding strategy is also one of the five parts of the liquidity risk management framework that the Board must approve (APS 210 para 16(d)).

Two more duties sit with it: to "maintain an ongoing presence in its chosen funding markets and strong relationships with funds providers", and to "regularly gauge its capacity to raise funds quickly" by identifying and monitoring the main factors that affect it (APS 210 paras 44(b) and 44(c)).

In the standard: The requirement to provide the strategy "on request" (APS 210 para 44(a)) and to "advise APRA of any material changes" (APS 210 para 46) are the only two APRA touchpoints. The APRA returns also carry a 3-year funding plan form (see document 11).

Example: Suppose the A$7.05bn were raised as three-month paper. The plan must therefore favour longer tenors and spread maturities to avoid a "wall" of debt in one month.

7. The contingency funding plan

7.1 What it is

A contingency funding plan (CFP) is the bank's emergency playbook for a liquidity shortfall. Think of a fire drill. Nobody writes the evacuation plan while the building is burning. The standard requires "a formal contingency funding plan that clearly sets out the strategies for addressing liquidity shortfalls in stressed situations", with policies for "a range of stress environments", "clear lines of responsibility" and "clear invocation and escalation procedures" (APS 210 para 47).

It also says what the plan must contain.

Requirement Paragraph
Commensurate with the ADI's complexity, risk profile, scope and role (APS 210 para 48)
State each contingency funding source, the amount that can be derived from it, escalation and prioritisation procedures, and the lead time to tap each source (APS 210 para 48)
Flexible enough to respond quickly in a variety of situations (APS 210 para 48)
Closely integrated with ongoing liquidity analysis and stress test assumptions and results (APS 210 para 49)
Address several time horizons, including intraday (APS 210 para 49)
If the ADI has retail deposits, address a retail deposit run and include measures to repay retail depositors as soon as practicable; it must not rely upon closing distribution channels (APS 210 para 50)
Reviewed and tested at least annually; Board reviews and approves at least annually, or more often if circumstances change (APS 210 para 51)
Included in the liquidity risk management framework as a minimum element (APS 210 para 16(e))

7.2 The parts of a typical plan

The standard sets the requirements but not the layout. A typical layout (typical practice; confirm with your controller):

  1. Triggers and stages. Stage 0 normal; Stage 1 early warning; Stage 2 stress; Stage 3 crisis. Each stage has quantitative triggers (from the early warning indicators and limits) and a named decision maker.
  2. Governance. A crisis committee, who chairs it, who attends, meeting frequency, decision rights and a record of decisions.
  3. Contingency funding sources. A table of actions with the amount available, the lead time and any cost or franchise damage. For example: sell or repo the liquid asset portfolio; draw on committed lines the bank holds; issue secured funding against mortgages; raise retail deposit rates; reduce lending; use central bank facilities. Exceptional Liquidity Assistance (ELA) from the RBA is the last resort and has its own notice rule (APS 210 para 70; see section 7.5).
  4. Retail run plan. Operational steps to pay out depositors as fast as practicable, branch and digital capacity, cash availability, communications. The plan must not rely on closing channels to retail depositors (APS 210 para 50).
  5. Communications. Who speaks to staff, customers, investors, the media, APRA and the RBA, with draft messages.
  6. Data and monitoring. The information pack the committee needs, and who produces it at what time (section 7.4).
  7. Testing. An annual test or simulation, with lessons recorded (APS 210 para 51).

From the controller: The CFP is only as good as its lead times. A source that "can raise A$2bn" is useless if the paperwork takes five days and the run lasts three. When you update the data pack, ask Treasury to confirm each lead time in writing.

7.5 Telling APRA and the RBA

The standard has three notice rules close to the plan. They appear again in the notifications table in document 11.

  • "An ADI must inform APRA as soon as possible of any concerns it has about its current or future liquidity position, and its plans to address those concerns." If it "experiences a severe liquidity stress, it must notify APRA immediately and advise the action that is being taken to address the situation" (APS 210 para 14).

The July 2025 version of the standard added that last operational capability requirement. For our team that means the data pack above must be producible quickly and reliably.

8. Intraday liquidity

8.1 What it is

Intraday liquidity is the money a bank can use during the business day to make payments as they fall due. Think of paying a builder at 10:00 am before your customers pay you at 3:00 pm. You need cash (or credit) to cover the gap during the day, even if the day ends with you in surplus.

Banks make and receive large payments through accounts at the Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) called Exchange Settlement Accounts (ESAs). If payments out run ahead of payments in, the ESA balance may fall low during the day. If it cannot cover a payment on time, the bank fails to settle, which can delay other banks too.

The standard requires an ADI to "actively manage its intraday liquidity positions and risks in order to meet payment and settlement obligations on a timely basis under both normal and stressed conditions, thus contributing to the orderly functioning of payment and settlement systems" (APS 210 para 41). The liquidity management strategy must include an approach to intraday liquidity (APS 210 para 29(d)), and the contingency funding plan must address time horizons "including intraday" (APS 210 para 49).

8.2 Measures Treasury and the team track

Measure Meaning Source
Opening balance Cash at the RBA at the start of the day Settlement data
Lowest balance in the day, and the time The tightest point Settlement data
Peak intraday usage Opening balance less lowest balance (cash used during the day) Calculated
Intraday sources available Own balance plus what can be borrowed against collateral Collateral system and RBA arrangements
Time-specific obligations Payments that must be made by a set time Payments team
Largest payments in and out The big items driving the profile Payments system
Throughput How much value is paid early versus late in the day Payments system

Example: On a given day Example Bank opens with A$4.8bn in its ESA (invented figure). At 14:30 the balance falls to A$1.9bn, after A$6.0bn of payments out and A$3.1bn in. Peak intraday usage = 4.8 - 1.9 = A$2.9bn. If the intraday limit is A$3.5bn, utilisation is 2.9 / 3.5 = 82.9%. The next day's pack shows both numbers and the time.

8.3 What changes in stress

Intraday needs usually rise in stress: other banks delay payments to a stressed bank, and the stressed bank's own counterparties demand more collateral or pre-funding. The CFP data pack therefore includes the intraday position (section 7.4).

The RBA arrangements for intraday funding (as generally understood, intraday repo against eligible securities) should be confirmed on the RBA's website before you rely on them in a document.

Treasury (or payments operations) manages intraday liquidity. We:

  • receive the daily intraday statistics and add them to the dashboard;
  • reconcile the closing ESA balance to the RBA balance used in the LCR;
  • record limit utilisation and breaches;
  • keep a history for trend analysis and stress calibration.

Controls: closing balance in the intraday feed equals the HQLA RBA balance at end of day; peak usage data has a time stamp; a missing day is shown as missing. Evidence kept: feeds, reconciliation, dashboard.

9. Collateral and encumbrance

9.1 What it is

Collateral is an asset that one party hands to another as security for a promise. A pawn shop holds your watch as collateral for a loan. When an asset is pledged, it is encumbered: it is not free to be sold. An asset that is not pledged and is free to be sold is unencumbered. Only unencumbered assets count in the stock of HQLA (APS 210 para 55).

The standard requires that an ADI "must actively manage its collateral positions, differentiating between encumbered and unencumbered assets", and "must monitor the legal entity and physical location where collateral is held and how it may be mobilised in a timely manner" (APS 210 para 38).

9.2 What we monitor

  1. Inventory of eligible assets by type, legal entity, custodian and location.
  2. Encumbrance. What is pledged, to whom, and for how long (repo counterparties, central counterparties, the RBA, derivative counterparties, secured funding programmes).
  3. Mobilisation time. How long to move an asset to where it is needed, including cut-off times.
  4. Margin calls. Initial margin (collateral posted at the start to cover possible future losses) and variation margin (collateral exchanged as values change day to day).
  5. Downgrade triggers. Contracts requiring extra collateral if the bank's rating falls. Example Bank has A$0.2bn of collateral to post on a three-notch downgrade, counted at 100% in the LCR.
  6. Operational requirements. HQLA must be unpledged, available for conversion into cash at any time, and under the control of the function charged with managing liquidity risk (Att A paras 22 to 23). If an asset becomes ineligible (for example after a rating downgrade) the ADI may keep it in the stock for an additional 30 calendar days (Att A para 25).

The same data supports the LCR (HQLA numerator, secured funding and lending lines) and the NSFR (encumbrance buckets, see document 09).

Example: Suppose A$2.0bn of Example Bank's Australian Government bonds is posted to a counterparty. Reported HQLA falls from 17.0 to 15.0. If the collateral team does not tell the LCR team, the dashboard overstates the buffer by A$2.0bn, the LCR would show 17.0 / 11.55 = 147.2% instead of the true 15.0 / 11.55 = 129.9%, and the headroom to the 125% amber trigger would drop from A$2.56bn to A$0.56bn (15.0 - 14.4375 = 0.5625).

Encumbrance ratio. A common indicator is:

Encumbrance ratio = Encumbered assets / Total assets

Suppose Example Bank had encumbered assets of A$2.0bn (the pledged bonds) in total assets of A$100.0bn. The ratio would be 2.0 / 100.0 = 2.0%. The bank sets its own limit (typical practice; confirm with your controller).

9.3 Procedure: the daily collateral and encumbrance report

Trigger: every business day, before the daily LCR is finalised. Inputs: the collateral system's position (pledged and received assets, with counterparty and term); the treasury system's holdings; the custodian statements. Steps: (1) Reconcile holdings between the treasury system and the custodian. (2) Mark each asset encumbered or unencumbered, with the end date. (3) Check that the unencumbered liquid assets feeding the LCR match. (4) Compute the encumbrance ratio and the amount of margin posted and received. (5) Update the downgrade-trigger collateral amount. (6) Report exceptions. Controls: holdings agree to the custodian; every pledge has a counterparty and an end date; nothing is HQLA and pledged at the same time; changes from yesterday are explained. Outputs: inventory and encumbrance table; exceptions. Who reviews: second analyst; Treasury confirms. Evidence kept: extracts, reconciliations, exception log.

10. Currency

The standard says an ADI "active in multiple currencies" must hold liquid assets "consistent with the distribution of its liquidity needs by currency", must "assess its aggregate foreign currency liquidity needs and determine an acceptable level of currency mismatches", and must analyse a strategy for each material currency, "considering potential constraints in times of stress" (APS 210 para 43).

A currency mismatch is a difference between the currency of assets and of liabilities, for example United States dollar funding used to lend Australian dollars. A bank normally closes the gap with foreign exchange swaps, but in stress that market can shut or become costly. That is why a locally incorporated ADI must keep its LCR at 100% on both an Australian dollar (AUD) basis and an all-currencies basis (APS 210 para 55). AUD obligations need AUD liquid assets.

Example Bank is single-currency. It has one currency, so its AUD LCR equals its all-currencies LCR (147.2% on both). The currency requirement (APS 210 para 43) therefore needs little work for Example Bank today, but you must know the topic because:

  • a bank with foreign currency business reports an LCR for each significant currency in its return (document 11);
  • the stress tests must analyse currencies separately (APS 210 para 43);
  • an offshore subsidiary changes how Level 2 is built (section 13).

If you work for a bank with several currencies, expect to produce, daily, an LCR by currency, a table of net cash outflows by currency before the inflow cap, and a currency mismatch limit report. Keep rules for converting amounts to AUD consistent with the accounting policy.

11. Cost allocation (funds transfer pricing)

11.1 What it is

If a family shares one bank account, the person who spends the most is not always the person who earns the most. Funds transfer pricing (FTP) is the bank's internal system for charging each business line for the funding and liquidity it uses and rewarding those that provide it. The standard requires the ADI to "develop and implement a costs and benefits allocation process for funding and liquidity that appropriately apportions the costs of prudent liquidity management to the sources of liquidity risk and provides appropriate incentives to manage liquidity risk" (APS 210 para 42).

Without it, a business that writes long loans funded by cheap at-call deposits looks very profitable while Treasury quietly carries the liquidity cost. With FTP, the cost goes to the product that creates the risk.

11.2 A worked example

Holding HQLA costs money: the bank earns less on liquid assets than on loans. Suppose the margin lost is 0.8% a year (invented for training). For each product, the liquid assets that the LCR requires set a liquidity charge.

Product Amount LCR rate HQLA needed Annual cost at 0.8% Charge in basis points
Stable retail deposit (credit to the deposit taker) A$100m 5% 5m 0.04m 4 bps
Corporate non-operational deposit A$100m 40% 40m 0.32m 32 bps
Undrawn committed corporate credit facility A$5,000m 10% 500m 4.0m 8 bps

A basis point (bps) is one hundredth of one percent. Check one line: 40m x 0.8% = 0.32m; 0.32m / 100m = 0.32% = 32 bps. A corporate non-operational deposit is therefore a more expensive funding source than a stable retail one, once the liquidity it consumes is counted. For a facility: 10% x 5,000m = 500m; 500m x 0.8% = 4.0m; 4.0m / 5,000m = 0.08% = 8 bps.

The NSFR adds a second charge for stable funding that long assets need. The size of both charges is a management choice and is set by Treasury and Finance (typical practice; confirm with your controller).

11.3 The team's part

We supply the data on which the charges depend: balances by product with their LCR run-off or inflow rate and NSFR factor, updated monthly. We do not set the prices. We check that the rates in the FTP engine match the current APS 210 rates (for example the Att A Table 3 rates) after any change in the standard.

Control: an annual reconciliation of the FTP rate table to the LCR and NSFR rate tables, signed by the controller. Evidence kept: the reconciliation and the approval of the FTP policy.

12. Management information

12.1 What the standard asks

The standard requires "a reliable management information system that provides the Board, senior management and other appropriate personnel with timely and forward-looking information on the liquidity position of the ADI" (APS 210 para 40). Three words in that sentence set the test: reliable (the numbers are right and can be traced), timely (they arrive when needed) and forward-looking (they show where the position is heading, not only where it is).

Related duties:

  • Senior management must "establish a set of reporting criteria specifying the scope, manner and frequency of reporting for various recipients (such as the Board, senior management and the asset/liability committee) including the parties responsible for preparing the reports" (APS 210 para 19(f)).
  • The Board "must review regular reports on the liquidity position of the ADI and, where necessary, information on new or emerging liquidity risks" (APS 210 para 18).
  • Senior management must "continuously review information on the ADI's liquidity developments and report to the Board on a regular basis" (APS 210 para 19(i)).
  • The Board and senior management must be "informed immediately of new and emerging liquidity concerns", listed in the standard (APS 210 para 33).

Management information (MI) is the umbrella term for the reports, dashboards and packs that carry this information. 13. Level 1 and Level 2

13.1 What the words mean

A bank can be one company, or a group of companies (a parent and its subsidiaries, which may include securitisation vehicles and offshore banks).

  • Level 1 means the bank on its own as a single legal entity (the ADI itself).
  • Level 2 means the consolidated group of which the ADI is a member: all the companies added together, as if one.

The standard says that a reference to an ADI means both: "an ADI on a Level 1 basis" and "a group of which an ADI is a member on a Level 2 basis" (APS 210 para 3). Where an ADI is the holding company of a group, it "must ensure that the requirements in this Prudential Standard are met on a Level 2 basis, where applicable" (APS 210 para 4). Where the ADI is a subsidiary of an authorised non-operating holding company (NOHC), the NOHC must ensure Level 2 compliance (APS 210 para 4).

Example Bank has one legal entity and one currency, so Level 1 equals Level 2 (and the AUD LCR equals the all-currencies LCR). You will compute one set of numbers. A bank with subsidiaries computes two sets, and the Level 2 numbers need extra care.

13.3 Why this matters for the other topics in this document

  • Stress tests must show impact on Level 1 and Level 2 positions and on business lines (APS 210 para 64).
  • The liquidity risk management framework must set out the organisational structure at both levels (APS 210 para 25).
  • Returns to APRA are made at Level 1 and at Level 2 (document 11).
  • Limits should be set at each level where cash can be trapped.

Watch out: Intragroup balances must be eliminated at Level 2 but remain at Level 1. A deposit from a subsidiary is a financial institution deposit at Level 1 and disappears at Level 2. If you are ever on a team that produces both, a reconciliation between the levels is a standard control.

14. The minimum liquidity holdings regime, in brief

Example Bank is an LCR ADI, so this regime does not apply to it. APRA decides which locally incorporated ADIs are LCR ADIs and which are MLH ADIs (minimum liquidity holdings ADIs) (APS 210 para 52). You still need to know the basics, because you may meet an MLH ADI (a smaller bank, a customer or a counterparty), because APRA's forms include MLH items, and because an ADI's classification can change.

14.1 The rule

An MLH ADI "must maintain a minimum holding of nine per cent of its liabilities in specified liquid assets, in accordance with Attachment B" (APS 210 para 57). APRA may require a higher holding if it has concerns about the ADI's liquidity risk profile or the quality of its liquidity risk management (APS 210 para 58). The ADI must meet the requirement "on a continuous basis, absent a situation of financial stress" (APS 210 para 54).

MLH ratio = Liquid assets (market value) / Liabilities
Requirement: at least 9%

14.3 A short worked example

An MLH ADI has total on-balance sheet liabilities of A$1,800m and irrevocable commitments of A$200m. Liabilities for MLH purposes (Att B para 1): 1,800 + 200 = 2,000m. Requirement: 9% x 2,000 = A$180m. It holds eligible liquid assets at market value of A$210m. Ratio = 210 / 2,000 = 10.5%. Headroom = 210 - 180 = A$30m. If its trigger ratio is 11% (it must be set above 9%) (Att B para 5), the ADI is below its trigger: 11% x 2,000 = 220m, so it is A$10m short of the trigger even though it meets the requirement. It manages to the trigger, and prepares to tell APRA immediately if the liquid assets may fall below the requirement (Att B para 6).

Watch out: There is no 30-day outflow scenario in the MLH regime. It is a simple stock test. That makes it easy to compute and easy to game with timing. APRA's form for it (ARF 210.2) is listed in document 11.

Check yourself

  1. What is the difference between a contractual and a behavioural maturity ladder, and which one is shown to a Treasurer when he asks "what do we expect?" Answer: The contractual ladder places each cash flow at its contractual date (an at-call deposit is in the first bucket). The behavioural ladder places each cash flow where management expects it to happen, based on observed customer behaviour. The Treasurer asking "what do we expect" wants the behavioural ladder, but should also see the contractual one to understand how much judgement is in the numbers (APS 210 para 36).

  2. A bank has the following in four buckets (A$bn): assets 10, 8, 6, 76; liabilities and equity 50, 14, 8, 28. Calculate the net gap and cumulative gap in each bucket. Answer: Net gaps: 10 - 50 = -40; 8 - 14 = -6; 6 - 8 = -2; 76 - 28 = +48. Cumulative: -40; -46; -48; 0. The last figure is zero because assets (100) equal liabilities and equity (100).

  3. Explain the difference between a limit, a trigger and an early warning indicator. Answer: A limit is a line the bank agrees not to cross; a breach requires a plan of action to reduce the exposure (APS 210 para 37). A trigger is a lower, earlier line (typically set by management) that starts escalation before the limit is reached. An early warning indicator is a measure designed to move before trouble arrives, to identify and escalate negative trends (APS 210 para 39). The levels are the bank's choice.

  4. Example Bank's HQLA is A$17.0bn and net cash outflows are A$11.55bn. The amber trigger is 125%. How much headroom is there to the trigger? Answer: HQLA needed at 125% = 1.25 x 11.55 = 14.4375. Headroom = 17.0 - 14.4375 = A$2.5625bn, about A$2.56bn. (The LCR is 17.0 / 11.55 = 147.2%.)

  5. Three funders supply 50%, 30% and 20% of a bank's wholesale funding. Calculate the HHI and say what number it would be if the shares were equal. Answer: HHI = 50^2 + 30^2 + 20^2 = 2,500 + 900 + 400 = 3,800. With equal shares of 33.3% each, HHI = 3 x 33.3^2 = 3 x 1,111 = 3,333 (about 3,333). The actual figure is higher, which shows concentration.

  6. HQLA is A$17.0bn. An internal scenario produces net outflows of A$20.4bn over 30 days. What is the straight-line survival horizon, and how much HQLA is needed for 30 days? Answer: Daily outflow = 20.4 / 30 = 0.68. Horizon = 17.0 / 0.68 = 25 days. HQLA needed for 30 days = 20.4, so the shortfall is 20.4 - 17.0 = A$3.4bn.

  7. Which APS 210 paragraphs require (a) the three-year funding strategy to be provided to APRA on request, (b) advice to APRA of material changes to the strategy, and (c) stress test results to be reported to the Board and APRA? Answer: (a) (APS 210 para 44(a)); (b) (APS 210 para 46); (c) (APS 210 para 65).

  8. What must a retail run plan within the contingency funding plan not rely on? Answer: It must not rely upon closing distribution channels to retail depositors. It must include measures to repay retail depositors as soon as practicable (APS 210 para 50).

  9. A bank has A$3,000m of liabilities and no irrevocable commitments, and is an MLH ADI. How much must it hold, and when must it tell APRA? Answer: 9% x 3,000 = A$270m (APS 210 para 57) (Att B para 1). It must inform APRA immediately when it becomes aware that its liquid assets may fall below the requirement, and advise the remedial action (Att B para 6).

  10. Example Bank's government bonds of A$2.0bn are pledged to a counterparty and the collateral team forgot to tell you. What is the true LCR, and what error would the reported LCR show? Answer: True HQLA = 17.0 - 2.0 = 15.0 (encumbered assets do not count). True LCR = 15.0 / 11.55 = 129.9%. Reported (wrong) LCR = 17.0 / 11.55 = 147.2%, an overstatement of 17.3 percentage points (147.2 - 129.9). (APS 210 para 38) (Att A para 23).

What to remember

  • The LCR and NSFR are snapshots; the maturity ladder, stress tests and indicators show timing and resilience (APS 210 para 36).
  • Always label a ladder contractual, behavioural or liquidity-adjusted, and show which assumptions it uses.
  • Limits, triggers and early warning indicators stack: regulatory minimum, Board appetite, management trigger. A breach of a limit needs a plan of action (APS 210 para 37).
  • Stress tests are regular, varied (institution-specific, market-wide, combined) and their results feed limits, strategy and the contingency funding plan (APS 210 paras 62 to 66).
  • The funding strategy is three years, Board-approved at least annually, provided to APRA on request, with material changes advised (APS 210 paras 44 to 46).
  • The contingency funding plan must be tested and approved by the Board at least annually, and must plan for a retail run without closing channels (APS 210 paras 50 and 51).
  • Collateral: always separate encumbered from unencumbered, and know where it is and how fast it moves (APS 210 para 38).
  • Management information must be reliable, timely and forward-looking, and every report needs a reader and a decision (APS 210 para 40).
  • Example Bank is single-entity and single-currency, so Level 1 equals Level 2 and the AUD LCR equals the all-currencies LCR; the MLH regime does not apply to it.
  • Limits, triggers, cut-off times and dashboard layouts are the bank's own choices: typical practice, confirm with your controller.
