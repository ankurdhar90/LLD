03 Your role, the team and the working year

Purpose: To tell you what the Liquidity Reporting team is accountable for, who else touches the liquidity numbers and what each of them does, what Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA) Prudential Standard APS 210 Liquidity requires of the Board and senior management, what happens and when across the working year, how your first 90 days will run, and how you are expected to behave when the numbers matter.

You will be able to:

  • Describe in a few sentences what Liquidity Reporting is accountable for, and what it is not.
  • Name the other functions (Treasury, Liquidity Risk, Finance, the Asset and Liability Committee, the Board, internal audit, APRA and the Reserve Bank of Australia) and say who prepares, reviews, approves and is informed for each recurring output.
  • Explain the three lines of defence and where your work sits.
  • Explain what APS 210 paras 15 to 20 put on the Board and senior management, and why that makes the accuracy of your numbers a Board-level matter.
  • Read the working-year calendar and find, for any APS 210 timed obligation, its paragraph.
  • Follow the first-90-days plan and know what "good" looks like, and the rules of conduct that apply when you are tempted to take a shortcut.

Before you start: Read document 02 (Liquidity from zero) first. You need to know what a bank, a liquid asset, the liquidity coverage ratio (LCR) and the net stable funding ratio (NSFR) are. This document refers to later documents by number, but you do not need to have read them yet.

Time: About 2 hours. Read it in full in your first week, then come back to the calendar (section 8), the 90-day plan (section 9) and the conduct section (section 11) as you go.

1. What this team is accountable for

I am your controller. Let me tell you in plain words what we are here to do.

An Australian bank must keep enough cash, and assets that can quickly become cash, to survive a bad month. It must also fund itself with money that is stable enough to last a year. APRA, the government agency that supervises banks, writes the rules (APS 210) and checks that banks follow them. Document 02 explained why.

Our team is Liquidity Reporting. We sit in Finance. We are the people who turn the bank's data into the numbers that prove, every day and every month, whether the bank is meeting those rules. In particular, we are accountable for:

# What we are accountable for In plain words
1 The LCR position Producing the liquidity coverage ratio, in Australian dollars and in all currencies, accurately and on time (Example Bank convention: daily by 10:00 am for the previous business day). The minimum is 100% (APS 210 para 55).
2 The NSFR position Producing the net stable funding ratio monthly. The minimum is 100% at all times (APS 210 para 60).
3 The liquidity returns to APRA Preparing, validating, reconciling and submitting the regulatory returns made under Reporting Standard ARS 210.0 (document 11).
4 The classification rules Maintaining the written rules that decide which category and rate each balance receives, and getting changes approved (document 07).
5 The data and its controls Making sure the numbers we use reconcile to the bank's general ledger (the master accounting record) and to source systems, and keeping the evidence.
6 The management information Giving Treasury, Liquidity Risk, the Asset and Liability Committee (ALCO), the Board Risk Committee and senior management timely, forward-looking information on the position (APS 210 para 40).
7 The explanation Being able to say why a ratio moved, line by line, on any day, and being ready when APRA, an auditor or the Board asks.
8 Raising the alarm Escalating promptly when a number is near a trigger, near a limit, or wrong.

What the team is not accountable for:

  • We do not decide how much liquidity to hold or where to invest it. That is Treasury, within limits set by management and the Board.
  • We do not set the bank's liquidity risk appetite. The Board does, on advice (APS 210 para 16(a)).
  • We do not provide the independent challenge of the liquidity position. That is the independent oversight function, Liquidity Risk (APS 210 para 28).
  • We do not change a number to make it look better. See section 11.

From the controller: People sometimes think a reporting team just "does the numbers". In a bank, the numbers are the control. When APRA, the Board or the auditors ask "are we compliant?", the answer is what you and I have produced and signed. Everything else in the bank's liquidity framework depends on those numbers being right.

From the controller: Notice the pattern. The person who prepares never approves their own work. The people who manage the liquid assets (Treasury) never grade themselves. The reviewers are different from the preparers at every step. That is on purpose, and it is how we meet the requirement for "adequate internal controls to ensure the integrity of its liquidity risk management processes" (APS 210 para 19(d)).

4. The three lines of defence

Banks organise their controls in three layers. It is a useful picture, because it shows where you fit.

Line Who Its job At Example Bank
First line The people who take the risk, run the business and own the controls over their own work Manage liquidity day to day, run controls, produce accurate numbers Treasury (manages the assets and funding) and Liquidity Reporting (produces the numbers and the control evidence)
Second line An independent risk function that sets the rules and challenges the first line Set policy and limits, monitor, challenge, report to the Board Liquidity Risk (APS 210 para 28 requires it to be operationally independent and to have the authority to challenge Treasury)
Third line Independent assurance, reporting to the Board's Audit Committee Test whether the first and second lines work Internal audit

(Guide Ch 8, "Governance".)

Typical practice: In some banks Liquidity Reporting is treated as part of the first line (it produces the numbers), in others as a separate "control" function in Finance. What does not vary is that the second line is independent of the people running the liquidity, as APS 210 para 28 requires. Confirm with your controller where we sit in Example Bank's policy.

What this means for you:

  1. You are a control. If you produce a number that is wrong, the second and third lines may not notice. Take that seriously.
  2. You are not the only control. If you spot a problem, tell people. Do not try to quietly fix it.
  3. Independence matters. Liquidity Risk must be free to question our numbers and Treasury's decisions. Give them what they ask for, promptly and completely.

5. What APS 210 requires of the Board and senior management

You will not be asked to set policy. But you should know what the standard requires of those who do, because your numbers are what they rely on. The section of the standard headed "Board and senior management responsibilities" is APS 210 paras 15 to 20.

5.1 The paragraphs, and what they mean for your numbers

Para What it requires What it means for the numbers you produce
APS 210 para 15 The Board is "ultimately responsible for the sound and prudent management of the liquidity risk" of the ADI. An ADI must maintain a liquidity risk management framework "commensurate with" the level of liquidity risk it runs. The Board relies on our figures. A wrong figure is a failure in the Board's own framework, not only ours.
APS 210 para 16 The framework must include at a minimum: (a) a Board-approved liquidity risk tolerance statement; (b) a Board-approved liquidity management strategy and policy; (c) operating standards (policies, procedures and controls) for identifying, measuring, monitoring and controlling liquidity risk; (d) a Board-approved funding strategy; and (e) a contingency funding plan. Our procedures (documents 07 to 09 and 11) are part of the "operating standards" in (c). They must be written down, followed and kept up to date.
APS 210 para 17 The Board must ensure that (a) senior management and other relevant personnel have the necessary experience, and (b) the framework and practices are documented and reviewed at least annually. Your training and sign-off (document 14) support (a). Our procedure documents are reviewed at least annually to support (b).
APS 210 para 18 The Board must review regular reports on the ADI's liquidity position, and information on new or emerging liquidity risks where necessary. The monthly and quarterly reports must be complete, correct and timely.
APS 210 para 19 Senior management must at a minimum: (a) develop the strategy, policies and processes in line with the Board's tolerance; (b) ensure sufficient liquidity at all times; (c) set the structure, responsibilities and controls for managing and overseeing liquidity in all legal entities, branches and subsidiaries; (d) ensure adequate internal controls over the integrity of the processes; (e) ensure stress tests, contingency funding plans and holdings of liquid assets are effective and appropriate; (f) set reporting criteria: scope, manner and frequency of reporting for each recipient (Board, senior management, the asset and liability committee) and who prepares the reports; (g) set procedures and approvals for exceptions to policies and limits, with escalation and follow-up for breaches; (h) closely monitor trends and market developments; (i) continuously review liquidity developments and report to the Board regularly. (d) is why we have the four-eyes rule and reconciliations. (f) is why the calendar in section 8 and the table in section 3 exist. (g) is why you must follow escalation procedures when a limit is breached.
APS 210 para 20 Senior management and the Board must demonstrate a thorough understanding of the links between funding liquidity risk and market liquidity risk, and how credit, market, operational and reputation risks affect the liquidity strategy. Be ready to explain a movement in terms of cause (for example, a market fall reduced the value of securities), not only arithmetic.

In the standard: Funding liquidity risk is "the risk that an ADI may not be able to meet its financial obligations as they fall due". Market liquidity risk is "the risk that liquidity in financial markets, such as the market for debt securities, may reduce significantly" (APS 210 para 20).

5.2 The rest of the framework, in a nutshell

APS 210 paras 21 to 35 set out the framework further. In brief:

Paras Topic What to know
APS 210 paras 21 to 24 The liquidity risk tolerance The level of risk the bank is willing to accept; documented; reviewed at least annually (APS 210 para 22); set so the bank can withstand a prolonged stress (APS 210 para 23); states the trade-off between risk and profit (APS 210 para 24). Example Bank's Board risk appetite of an LCR of at least 120% and an NSFR of at least 110% is an Example Bank convention that expresses this tolerance.
APS 210 para 25 Organisational structure The framework must set out the structure on both Level 1 and Level 2 basis and the roles of management. (Level 1 is the bank alone, Level 2 is the bank with its subsidiaries; for Example Bank they are the same.)
APS 210 para 26 A cushion of unencumbered liquid assets The framework must ensure the bank can withstand stress events, including loss of both unsecured and secured funding.
APS 210 para 27 Integration The liquidity framework must be integrated into the overall risk management process.
APS 210 para 28 Independent oversight See section 4.
APS 210 paras 29 to 32 The liquidity management strategy Must include policies on composition and maturity of assets and liabilities, diversity of funding, currencies, and intraday liquidity; quantitative and qualitative targets; appropriate for the bank; communicated throughout the organisation.
APS 210 para 33 Immediate information to the Board The bank must have policies, procedures and controls so that the Board and senior management "are informed immediately of new and emerging liquidity concerns", such as rising funding costs or concentrations, increases in funding requirements, loss of alternative sources, material or persistent breaches of limits, a significant fall in the cushion of unencumbered liquid assets, or external market changes.
APS 210 para 34 Business units Senior management must be satisfied that all business units that affect liquidity know the strategy and operate within it.
APS 210 para 35 Review The framework is reviewed on an ongoing basis, and comprehensively as part of the review of the risk management framework under Prudential Standard CPS 220 Risk Management.
APS 210 paras 36 to 43 Management of liquidity risk Cash flow projections (APS 210 para 36); limits (APS 210 para 37); collateral (APS 210 para 38); early warning indicators (APS 210 para 39); management information (APS 210 para 40); intraday liquidity (APS 210 para 41); cost allocation (APS 210 para 42); currencies (APS 210 para 43). Document 10 covers these.
APS 210 paras 44 to 46 Funding strategy Three-year strategy, Board-approved at least annually.
APS 210 paras 47 to 51 Contingency funding plan Formal plan, including a retail deposit run, reviewed and tested at least annually.
APS 210 paras 62 to 66 Stress testing Regular tests, results reported to the Board and APRA.

Do not try to learn all this now. Document 04 walks through every paragraph in order, and document 10 teaches the monitoring tasks.

5.3 What this means for you, in four sentences

  1. The Board and senior management have a legal duty to manage liquidity. They cannot do it without correct numbers.
  2. APS 210 expects "adequate internal controls to ensure the integrity" of the processes (APS 210 para 19(d)); your reconciliations, reviews and evidence are part of this.
  3. APS 210 expects senior management to establish "procedures and approvals necessary for exceptions to policies and limits, including escalation procedures" (APS 210 para 19(g)); you follow them and never bypass them.
  4. APS 210 expects the Board and senior management to be "informed immediately" of emerging concerns (APS 210 para 33); you are one of the people who will often see the concern first.

7. How your numbers travel

It helps to see where your work goes after you sign it.

Source systems (core banking, treasury, collateral, general ledger)
        |
        v
Liquidity Reporting: load, reconcile, classify, calculate, review, sign
        |
        +--> Treasury (daily: manage the portfolio and funding against the number)
        +--> Liquidity Risk (daily: challenge, monitor triggers and limits)
        |
        v
Monthly ALCO pack (management actions)
        |
        v
Quarterly Board Risk Committee report, then the Board (APS 210 para 18)
        |
Regulatory returns to APRA (document 11), public disclosure (document 11)

Two things follow.

  1. One set of numbers. The number Treasury sees on Tuesday morning, the number in the ALCO pack, the number in the Board report and the number in the APRA return must all agree, or the differences must be explained. If they differ, the Board loses trust in the numbers, and APRA may too. The check that makes them agree is reconciliation (documents 08, 09 and 11).
  2. The first person to see a problem is usually one of us. Because we calculate the ratios, we are often the first to see a ratio fall, a data feed break or a classification look wrong. APS 210 expects the Board and senior management to be "informed immediately of new and emerging liquidity concerns" (APS 210 para 33). In practice that starts with a message from one of us to the controller.

11. Professional conduct

The numbers we produce are used by the Board and the regulator. Some rules of conduct follow directly from that.

11.1 Accuracy

  • Take your time to check. Re-add the totals. Recompute the ratio from the totals. Check the signs (outflows positive, inflows positive, net outflows = outflows minus capped inflows).
  • Check units. A$bn is not A$m. If a number looks wrong by a factor of a thousand, it probably is.
  • Use the rate in the standard, not the rate you remember. Look it up. The rates are in Att A Table 3 and the inflow paragraphs (Att A paras 54 to 66), the ASF and RSF factors are in Att C paras 11 to 15 and Att C paras 29 to 42, and the cap is in Att A para 30.
  • When something is uncertain, say so, in writing.

11.2 Evidence

  • Keep, for every number you produce, enough to reproduce it: the data extract with its date and time, the classification rules version, the working paper, the review note, the sign-off, and any manual adjustment with its approval.
  • Save to the agreed location with the agreed name. No numbers on a personal drive, a desktop or in a personal email.
  • The standard expects the bank to be able to "provide analysis and evidence to justify the assumptions" for derived cash flows (Att A para 32). Good evidence is how.

11.3 Never adjust a number to hit a limit

This is the most important rule in this document.

Watch out: You must never change a figure, a classification, a rate, a date or a data record so that a ratio reaches a limit, passes a trigger, or looks better than it is. Not by a little. Not "just for today". Not because someone senior asks you to. Not because you are sure it will be fixed tomorrow.

Examples of what this rule forbids:

  • Moving a deposit from "less stable" to "stable" without evidence that it meets the conditions in Att A para 37.
  • Leaving an item out of the outflows because "it is not material".
  • Counting an encumbered government bond as HQLA, or leaving it in the stock after the eligible period has passed (Att A paras 22 to 23; Att A para 25).
  • Treating a term deposit as locked in when the bank would let the customer withdraw it (Att A paras 41 to 42: if the ADI allows withdrawal despite the clause, the whole category must be treated as demand deposits).
  • Rounding or timing choices that move a ratio over a limit.
  • Changing a data extract date or time to catch a favourable position.
  • Entering a "temporary" manual adjustment with no approval.
  • Asking someone else to do any of the above.

If you think the rule gives a wrong answer, or the rule is unclear, the answer is to raise it. A rule can be changed through the approval process (document 07), with evidence and the proper sign-off. A number cannot be changed by hand.

Why this matters so much: the ratios are measures of whether the bank can survive. A ratio that has been nudged is a bank that believes it is safer than it is. That is the situation that leads to harm for depositors and to action by the regulator.

From the controller: If anyone, however senior, ever asks you to adjust a number to hit a limit, say, calmly: "I can't do that without the change being approved. Shall I raise it with the controller?" Then tell me. You will have my full support. This is not a grey area.

11.4 Escalation

Escalation means telling the right person, quickly, what you have found. You do not need to be sure it is a problem. You need to tell someone early.

Typical practice: The triggers, the route and the times below are Example Bank conventions, set out for training. Confirm them with your controller.

Procedure: escalating a concern about a number, a trigger or a limit

Element What it says
Trigger You notice any of: a ratio at or below an early-warning trigger (LCR 125% amber, NSFR 115% amber), below the Board risk appetite (LCR 120%, NSFR 110%), or below the 100% minimum; a limit breach; a data or calculation error; a material unexplained movement; a system outage on a reporting day; a request from APRA or an auditor.

11.5 Confidentiality

  • Liquidity information is highly sensitive. A bank's liquidity position, its triggers and especially any concern about it can affect confidence in the bank. A leak could itself start a run (document 02).
  • Share on a need-to-know basis, and only through the agreed channels. Do not discuss the position or the triggers outside the team and the named recipients. That includes friends, family, social media, and casual conversations in lifts, cafés and public transport.
  • Do not forward working papers to personal email or devices.
  • Do not respond to any request from outside the bank (including media, analysts, other banks, or someone who says they are from APRA) yourself. Refer it to the controller.
  • Follow the bank's code of conduct and its confidentiality and information-handling policies. They apply to you from your first day. Ask your controller for the current versions. (They are Example Bank's own, not part of APS 210.)

11.6 Working with others

  • Treasury and Liquidity Risk are not adversaries. They are the people who use our numbers, and the people whose independence the standard protects (APS 210 para 28). Give them the information they ask for completely and promptly, and never alter a number because one of them would prefer it.
  • Auditors may ask you anything about your work. Answer truthfully and completely. If you do not know, say so and find out.
  • APRA contact goes through the controller and the senior executive. If APRA contacts you, take the person's details, say you will arrange for the right person to respond, and tell the controller at once.

11.7 Speaking up

If you see something that does not look right, whether in our work or anyone else's, say so. The bank has a policy that protects people who raise concerns in good faith. Ask your controller where to find it. (It is the bank's policy, not part of APS 210.)

Check yourself

1. In one sentence each, say who prepares, who reviews and who signs the daily LCR at Example Bank, and why they must be different people.

Answer: An analyst prepares it, a second Liquidity Reporting person reviews it, and the controller signs it. They must differ so that no one grades their own work (the four-eyes rule), which supports the "adequate internal controls to ensure the integrity" of the liquidity processes that senior management must ensure (APS 210 para 19(d)).

2. Which APS 210 paragraph says the independent oversight function must be able to challenge Treasury, and which team is it at Example Bank?

Answer: APS 210 para 28 says the liquidity risk management oversight function must be operationally independent, with the skills and authority to challenge the treasury and other liquidity risk management businesses. At Example Bank it is Liquidity Risk.

3. Who is "ultimately responsible" for the liquidity risk of an ADI under APS 210, and which paragraph says so?

Answer: The Board of directors (APS 210 para 15).

4. Name the five things the liquidity risk management framework must include at a minimum, with the paragraph.

Answer: (a) a Board-approved liquidity risk tolerance statement; (b) a Board-approved liquidity management strategy and policy; (c) operating standards (policies, procedures and controls); (d) a Board-approved funding strategy; and (e) a contingency funding plan (APS 210 para 16).

5. For each of these, say how often APS 210 requires it: (a) reviewing the liquidity risk tolerance; (b) reviewing and testing the contingency funding plan; (c) reviewing and approving the funding strategy. Give the paragraphs.

Answer: All three are at least annually: (a) APS 210 para 22; (b) APS 210 para 51 (and the Board reviews and approves it at least annually, or more often as circumstances require); (c) APS 210 para 45 (the funding strategy is also reviewed and updated at least annually, APS 210 para 46).

6. The LCR drops below 100%. Which paragraph requires APRA to be told, and how quickly?

Answer: Att A para 5 requires the ADI to inform APRA "immediately" in the event of an actual breach of its LCR requirement or if it becomes aware of circumstances that may result in a breach. APS 210 para 14 also requires APRA to be notified immediately of a severe liquidity stress. In practice we escalate to the controller at once, and the senior executive informs APRA.

7. (Calculation) Example Bank's HQLA is A$14.1bn and net cash outflows are A$12.0bn. Calculate the LCR. Is it above the 100% minimum? Is it above the 120% Board appetite? How much more HQLA is needed to reach 120%?

Answer: LCR = 14.1 ÷ 12.0 = 1.175 = 117.5%. It is above 100% (surplus 14.1 − 12.0 = A$2.1bn) but below the 120% Board appetite (an Example Bank convention). To reach 120%, HQLA must be 12.0 × 1.20 = 14.4. The extra needed is 14.4 − 14.1 = A$0.3bn. (Other things unchanged.) Because it is below the 125% amber trigger and the 120% appetite, you would escalate under the procedure in section 11.4.

8. (Calculation) Today the 30-day window covers the next 30 calendar days. Example Bank has A$2.0bn of wholesale debt that falls due 35 days from now, part of the A$3.0bn in the 30-day to 6-month bucket. In 5 days it will enter the window. Using Example Bank's base numbers (HQLA 17.0, outflows 13.45, inflows 1.90), and assuming nothing else changes, what will the LCR be once it has entered the window, and what does this tell you about the forward-looking view?

Answer: The A$2.0bn is wholesale debt due within the window, so it gets a 100% outflow: 2.0 × 1.00 = 2.0. New outflows = 13.45 + 2.0 = 15.45. The cap on inflows is 75% × 15.45 = 11.5875, higher than 1.90, so it does not bind. Net outflows = 15.45 − 1.90 = 13.55. LCR = 17.0 ÷ 13.55 = 1.2546 = 125.5%. The ratio falls from 147.2% to 125.5%, just above the 125% amber trigger, with no change in anything the bank does today. This is why the team projects forward 30 to 90 days (document 08): a debt rolling into the window changes the ratio, and Treasury needs to know weeks ahead.

9. A senior colleague asks you to reclassify A$1.0bn of less stable retail deposits as stable "just this once" so that the LCR passes the amber trigger. What do you do, and why?

Answer: You decline to change the classification, and tell the controller. Changing a classification to hit a trigger is the one thing you must never do (section 11.3). A rate can be changed only through the approval process with evidence that the deposits meet the conditions in Att A para 37. To show the effect if it were true: moving A$1.0bn from 10% to 5% run-off would reduce outflows by 1.0 × (0.10 − 0.05) = A$0.05bn, so the outflows would fall to 13.40 and net outflows to 11.50, a small flattering effect that is not real.

10. Which of these APS 210 obligations does not apply to Example Bank: (a) APS 210 para 14, (b) APS 210 para 60, (c) APS 210 para 67, (d) Att A para 5? Why?

Answer: (c) APS 210 para 67, the local operational capacity assessment, applies only to a foreign ADI. Example Bank is locally incorporated. (a), (b) and (d) all apply.

What to remember

  • Liquidity Reporting turns data into the numbers that show whether the bank meets APS 210. The numbers are a control, and the Board relies on them.
  • Treasury manages the liquidity. Liquidity Risk independently challenges (APS 210 para 28). We measure and report. Internal audit tests us. Nobody approves their own work.
  • The Board is ultimately responsible for liquidity risk (APS 210 para 15); senior management must have adequate internal controls over the integrity of the processes (APS 210 para 19(d)) and set the reporting and escalation rules (APS 210 para 19(f) and (g)).
  • Timed obligations: continuous (APS 210 para 12; APS 210 para 54; APS 210 para 55; APS 210 para 60); immediate (APS 210 para 14; APS 210 para 33; APS 210 para 70; Att A para 5); at least annual (APS 210 para 17; APS 210 para 22; APS 210 paras 45 to 46; APS 210 para 51); on request (APS 210 para 44(a)). Daily production and due dates are the bank's choice or come from Reporting Standard ARS 210.0.
  • Escalate early and in facts. Keep evidence for every number. Look up the rate; do not remember it.
  • Never adjust a number, a rate or a classification to hit a limit. Raise it instead.
  • Keep liquidity information confidential and share it only through agreed channels.
