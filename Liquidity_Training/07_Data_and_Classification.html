07 Data: where the numbers come from and how each balance is classified

Purpose: To teach you where every number in the liquidity returns starts, what facts the bank must hold about each deposit, loan, security and contract, and how to turn each record into the right LCR run-off or inflow rate and the right NSFR factor. This is the document you will use most when someone asks, "How do we treat this product?"

You will be able to:

  • Name the bank's source systems and say what each one is the master record for.
  • List, for each product, the data fields APS 210 needs and the paragraph that needs each one.
  • Walk any deposit, security, loan, facility, secured transaction, derivative or debt issue down a decision tree to its LCR category and rate and its NSFR category and factor.
  • Run the data quality checks (completeness, validity, reconciliation to the ledger, movement) and say what to do when a field is missing.
  • Explain how classification rules are written down, approved and changed.

Before you start: Read document 02 (Liquidity from zero) so that the words liquidity coverage ratio, net stable funding ratio, run-off and balance sheet are familiar. Keep document 05 (companion to Attachment A) and document 06 (companion to Attachments B and C) open beside you. For the full theory behind each rate, the guide chapters are cited as you go.

Time: About six hours: two to read, two to follow the decision trees with a pen, and two to do the Check yourself questions.

1. Why data comes first

Every liquidity number is a balance multiplied by a rate. The rate comes from a rule in APS 210 (the prudential standard on liquidity that APRA, the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority, issues). The balance comes from a computer system. If the balance is wrong, or if the record is put in the wrong category, the answer is wrong, even when your arithmetic is perfect.

Think of a post office sorting letters. The sorter does not care what the letter says. He cares only about the postcode. A letter with the wrong postcode goes to the wrong town. In liquidity work the "postcode" of each record is a small set of facts: who the customer is, how long until the money moves, whether the asset is pledged, and whether the customer can withdraw early. Your job is to make sure every record carries the right postcode, and that you can prove it.

From the controller: In my first year I found that most of our reporting errors were not maths errors. They were records sitting in the wrong bucket. A deposit marked "corporate" that was really a super fund. A bond marked "unencumbered" that had been repoed the day before. Learn the data first, and the formulas become easy.

APS 210 expects this discipline. The bank must have "operating standards" (policies, procedures and controls) for identifying, measuring, monitoring and controlling liquidity risk (APS 210 para 16(c)). Senior management must make sure the bank has adequate internal controls to ensure the integrity of its liquidity risk management processes (APS 210 para 19(d)). Data classification is one of those processes.

Example Bank is a locally incorporated LCR ADI. An ADI is an authorised deposit-taking institution, which is what the law calls a bank, building society or credit union that may take deposits. An LCR ADI is one that must meet the liquidity coverage ratio (LCR) under Attachment A (APS 210 para 55). Example Bank has one legal entity and one currency, so Level 1 (the ADI on its own) equals Level 2 (the whole group), and the Australian-dollar LCR equals the all-currencies LCR (APS 210 paras 3 and 4).

2.1 How a record travels

A record moves through five stages. You work mainly in stages 3 to 5.

  1. Capture. A customer opens an account or Treasury does a trade. The source system records it.
  2. Extract. At close of business each system produces a file or table of positions as at a stated time. This is called a feed.
  3. Stage and validate. The feeds land in a staging area. Automated checks test completeness and validity (section 9).
  4. Classify. Rules map each record to an LCR category and rate and an NSFR category and factor (sections 6 and 7).
  5. Calculate and report. The weighted amounts are summed into the LCR and NSFR (documents 08 and 09), and flow into the returns to APRA (document 11).

2.2 The grain: why account level, not totals

You cannot calculate the LCR from the balance sheet totals. You need the record at the level where the facts differ: each account, each loan, each security holding, each trade. The reason is simple. Two retail deposit accounts of A$100,000 can have different rates (5% and 25%) because one belongs to a salary customer and the other is an on-line account with a promotional rate. The total balance cannot tell you that.

Example: Example Bank shows "Retail deposits, less stable (at call): A$15.0bn". That one line hides millions of accounts. Each account has been scored against the scorecard (section 6.2) and the result is that the whole line takes 10%. If a block of promotional accounts scored 3 or more, part of the line would take 25% instead.

3. Basic ideas you need before the data dictionary

3.1 Carrying value and market value

The carrying value is the amount shown in the bank's accounting records. The market value is what you could sell the item for today. The NSFR uses carrying value (Att C paras 7(a) and 17(a)). The LCR values liquid assets at market value (Att A para 9 for HQLA1; Att A paras 10 and 12 for haircuts on HQLA2). Be sure you take the right one.

3.2 Residual maturity

The residual maturity is the time left until the money is due. A 5-year bond issued four years ago has a residual maturity of one year. Do not use the original term. Always count from the reporting date.

The two ratios look at maturity differently, and a mistake here is common.

Question LCR (30 days) NSFR (one year)
Which date counts for a liability? Earliest date the customer can get the money, including options at the investor's discretion (Att A para 45) Earliest date the funds may be redeemed (Att C para 8)
Which date counts for an asset or inflow? Latest possible date, based on the counterparty's contractual rights (Att A para 60) Latest possible date the asset may mature, assuming any extension option is exercised (Att C para 20)
What if there is no maturity? Funding with undetermined maturity is inside the window (Att A para 45); loans with no specific maturity give no inflow except minimum payments (Att A para 60) An asset without a defined maturity, or one subject to periodic review, is treated as one year or more (Att C para 21)

The pattern is the same in both ratios: for money going out, assume the worst (earliest); for money coming in, assume the worst (latest). That is the caution built into the rules.

3.4 Counterparty

The counterparty is the other side of the contract: the customer, the borrower, the lender. The rules sort counterparties into groups, and the group often decides the rate. The groups are:

  • Natural persons (individual people). Their deposits are retail (Att A para 34).
  • Small and medium enterprises (SMEs) that meet the test in Att A para 46 (see section 6.1).
  • Non-financial corporations, sovereigns (governments), central banks, public sector entities (PSEs, for example state government agencies) and multilateral development banks (MDBs, for example the World Bank).
  • Banks and other ADIs subject to prudential supervision.
  • Other financial institutions. APS 210 says a financial institution "includes a money market corporation, finance company, friendly society and the trustee of a superannuation/pension fund, public unit trust/mutual fund or cash management trust" (APS 210 para 10(b)).
  • Other legal entity customers (a catch-all for committed facilities).

3.5 Encumbered and unencumbered

An asset is encumbered when it is pledged, for example as security for a repurchase agreement (repo, a sale of a security with a promise to buy it back). A pledged asset cannot be sold freely. Assets in the LCR liquid stock must be unencumbered (Att A paras 22 and 23). The NSFR penalises encumbered assets by the length of the encumbrance (Att C para 23).

3.6 Performing and non-performing

A loan is performing if the borrower is paying as agreed. A non-performing loan is in default or arrears as determined under the credit risk standard APS 220 (Att C footnote 27). Only fully performing loans give LCR inflows (Att A para 54).

5. How to use the decision trees

A decision tree is a list of questions. You answer them in order and stop at the first step that fits. Each step ends in a category, a rate and a paragraph. Always write the paragraph next to the result. When the auditor asks "why 25%?", the paragraph is your answer.

Ask these six questions of every record, in this order:

  1. Which side is it? An asset, a liability (including capital) or an off-balance sheet item such as an undrawn facility?
  2. Who is the counterparty? Person, SME, non-financial corporate, government body, bank, other financial institution?
  3. When does the cash move? Inside 30 days (LCR)? Under 6 months, 6 months to under 1 year, or 1 year or more (NSFR)?
  4. Is it secured, pledged or netted? What is the collateral, and for how long is it encumbered?
  5. Does it pass the special tests? Financial Claims Scheme cover, scorecard, operational deposit tests, HQLA tests, performing status, revocability?
  6. Which paragraph gives the rate?

Watch out: The LCR and the NSFR classify the same record differently. The same A$10m deposit may get 40% in the LCR and 50% in the NSFR. Classify for each ratio separately and store both results on the record.

6. Decision trees for deposits

Deposits are the biggest and most error-prone group. Work through trees D1 to D6 in order.

6.1 Tree D1: Who is the customer?

  1. If the funds reach the bank through an intermediary (for example a platform or adviser who places clients' money with the bank), go to step 2. Otherwise go to step 3.
  2. Test the four intermediary conditions. The deposit may be treated as the end person's only if, during the next 30 days, all of these hold, and the bank can demonstrate it (Att A para 35): - (a) the person keeps all legal rights over withdrawal or other movement of the funds; - (b) the person uses those rights in practice and cannot transfer them to the intermediary; - (c) there is clear and prominent disclosure to the person that the funds will be placed with the ADI; and - (d) the intermediary or an associated entity can neither make investment decisions for the person on the deposit nor withdraw funds without specific directions from the person (fees, expense reimbursements and taxes aside).

If all four hold, treat the funds as coming from the person and go to step 3 for the person. If any fails, it is a financial institution deposit: go to tree D5 and take 100% (Att A para 35). 3. If the depositor is a natural person (an actual human being, alone or jointly), it is a retail deposit. Go to tree D2 (Att A para 34). 4. If the depositor is a sole proprietorship, partnership, company, trust or other entity, it is wholesale (Att A para 34), unless step 5 turns it into a qualifying SME deposit. 5. SME test. An SME deposit may be treated as retail only if both are true (Att A para 46): - (a) the customer is a non-financial SME, and its deposits and other extensions of funds are managed as retail exposures with liquidity risk similar to retail accounts; and - (b) the total aggregated funding raised from that customer is less than A$2 million, on a consolidated basis where applicable.

If both are true, treat it as retail: go to tree D2. If the bank has no liability products to apply the SME definition, the definition in para 40 of APS 113 may be used instead (Att A footnote 7). If either fails, it is wholesale: go to tree D4 and D5. 6. Check whether the customer is a financial institution. Under APS 210, "a financial institution includes a money market corporation, finance company, friendly society and the trustee of a superannuation/pension fund, public unit trust/mutual fund or cash management trust" (APS 210 para 10(b)). The word "includes" means the list is not exhaustive: banks and other ADIs, insurers and fund managers are also financial institutions in practice. Record the sub-type. This sets both the LCR rate (100% for non-operational) and the NSFR factor (0% under six months).

Watch out: A super fund is a trustee, so it is a financial institution, even though the members are people. Our practice-case customers show this. Do not be fooled by a name like "Teachers Retirement Fund Pty Ltd": check what it is (APS 210 para 10(b)).

Typical practice: The SME flag is set in the customer master by a monthly batch that sums all liabilities and funding of the customer group. A new facility can push a customer over A$2 million overnight, so the batch result should be refreshed daily for customers near the line. Confirm with your controller.

6.2 Tree D2: Stable or less stable? The scorecard

Retail and qualifying SME deposits split into stable and less stable portions (Att A para 36).

  1. Find the portion fully covered by the Financial Claims Scheme (FCS). The FCS is the Australian Government's deposit guarantee, A$250,000 per account holder per ADI. It is per person across all accounts at the bank, not per account. So add up all of the customer's deposits first. Joint accounts count by account holder. The first A$250,000 in total is covered; any more is not.
  2. Is the covered portion in a transactional account (for example salary credited automatically), or does the depositor have other established relationships with the ADI that make withdrawal highly unlikely? If yes to either, the covered portion is stable: LCR 5% (Att A paras 37 and 53).
  3. Everything else is less stable (Att A para 38). That includes the part above the FCS limit and the covered part of accounts that fail step 2.
  4. Score each less stable account or product balance against the scorecard (Att A para 40, Table 1):
Criterion Score
Deposit balance is greater than any government deposit guarantee limit (in its absence, AUD 250,000) 2
Deposit is an on-line account 2
Absence of an established customer relationship 1
Deposit is heavily rate-driven 1
  1. Add the scores and read the rate (Att A Table 2):
Total score LCR run-off rate
0 to 2 10%
3 or more 25%

The reason for each criterion is in Att A para 39: high-value deposits, customers with no relationship, deposits where the internet is integral to the account, and promotional-rate deposits are the ones more likely to leave first in a stress.

Example: A customer with A$40,000 in an on-line saver at a promotional rate, who has no other product. Balance above A$250,000? No (0). On-line? Yes (2). No relationship? Yes (1). Rate-driven? Yes (1). Total 4, so 25%. The same on-line account for a customer with a home loan and a normal rate scores 2 only, so 10%.

Typical practice: The standard's stable test (step 2) looks only at FCS cover plus a transactional account or other relationships. It does not mention on-line accounts there. Example Bank's convention is that an on-line account is never stable: the whole balance is put through the scorecard, so every on-line balance scores at least 2 and takes at least 10%. This is a cautious bank policy, not a rule: under the standard alone, a fully FCS-covered on-line account held by a customer with an established relationship could be stable (Att A para 37). Confirm with your controller before applying it to a new product.

6.3 Tree D3: Term deposits (fixed-term and notice deposits)

  1. Is the residual maturity or withdrawal notice period 30 days or less? If yes, the depositor can get the money inside the window. Treat it like any other retail deposit: go back to tree D2 and apply 5%, 10% or 25% (Att A para 41 applies only to periods greater than 30 days).
  2. If the residual maturity or notice period is greater than 30 days, does the depositor have a legal right to withdraw within 30 days? If no, the outflow is excluded: LCR 0% (Att A para 41).
  3. If the depositor has such a right, is there a significant penalty for early withdrawal that is materially greater than the loss of interest? If yes, excluded: 0% (Att A para 41). If no, treat as a demand deposit: go to tree D2.
  4. Pool test. If the bank allows a depositor to withdraw despite a clause saying there is no legal right, the entire category of these funds must be treated as demand deposits (Att A para 42). One exception: depositors in genuine hardship may withdraw without tainting the pool, but only if the bank has a policy defining hardship (Att A para 43).
  5. NSFR: use the earliest date funds may be redeemed (Att C para 8). Under one year: stable 95% or less stable 90%. One year or more: 100%.

Watch out: Step 4 is a trap that is easy to miss. If a branch manager lets customers break "no early withdrawal" term deposits as a favour, the bank can lose the 0% treatment on the whole product category. Keep an exceptions log of every early withdrawal on these products, with the reason code (hardship or not), and review it monthly (Att A paras 42 and 43).

6.4 Tree D4: Is a wholesale deposit operational?

An operational deposit is money a business keeps with the bank because it needs the bank's payment and settlement services to run its day-to-day business (Att A para 47).

  1. Does the customer have a substantive dependency on the ADI, and is the deposit required for those activities (Att A para 47)? If no, go to tree D5.
  2. Is the activity clearing, custody or cash management, where the customer relies on the ADI as an independent third-party intermediary to carry out its normal banking activities over the next 30 days (Att A para 48)? If no, go to D5.
  3. Are the services provided to an institutional customer under a legally binding agreement, and is termination subject to either a notice period of at least 30 days or significant switching costs borne by the customer if the deposits are moved before 30 days (Att A para 48)? If no, go to D5.
  4. Is the deposit (Att A para 49): - (a) a by-product of the underlying services; - (b) not offered in the wholesale market for the sole purpose of offering interest income; and - (c) held in specifically designated accounts and priced without giving an economic incentive to leave excess funds in them?

If any answer is no, go to D5. 5. Split the balance into the operational amount and the excess. "Any excess balances that could be withdrawn without jeopardising these clearing, custody or cash management activities do not qualify" (Att A para 50). The excess goes to tree D5. 6. Is the whole deposit amount fully covered by deposit insurance? Operational and fully covered: 5%. Operational and not fully covered: 25% (Att A Table 3). 7. NSFR: operational deposits get 50% (Att C para 14(b)). Superannuation fund deposits that meet the operational definition also get 50% (Att C para 14(c)).

In the standard: The operational test is applied to all counterparties, including banks and super funds. The 25% rate is "from all counterparties not fully covered by deposit insurance" (Att A Table 3). So a custody deposit from a super fund trustee can be operational (25%), while the same fund's ordinary at-call savings are not (100%).

Typical practice: Operational status is set by the relationship manager and product owner, who hold the service agreements, and is tested annually by Liquidity Risk. Your daily job is only to use the flag and operational amount supplied; you keep the evidence file reference on the record. Confirm with your controller.

7.1 Tree S: Securities and the liquid asset stock

This tree answers two questions: "Is it HQLA, and what class?" and "What NSFR factor?" Run both.

Part 1: HQLA class (LCR numerator).

  1. Is the security on the bank's balance sheet, owned by the bank? If it was borrowed and the bank does not keep beneficial ownership, it is not an asset for the NSFR (Att C para 25). For the LCR, collateral received in a reverse repo may count only if it is held at the ADI, not rehypothecated, and legally and contractually available for the bank's use (Att A para 23).
  2. Are they notes and coin? HQLA1 (Att A para 9(a)).
  3. Are they central bank balances that can be drawn down in a stress? HQLA1 (Att A para 9(b)). Example Bank's Exchange Settlement Account balance at the RBA is in this group.
  4. Is it a marketable security that is a claim on, or guaranteed by, a sovereign, central bank, PSE, the Bank for International Settlements, the International Monetary Fund, the European Central Bank, the European Union or an MDB, and does it meet all four conditions? (Att A para 9(c)) - (i) a zero per cent risk weight under APS 112; - (ii) traded in large, deep and active repo or cash markets with low concentration; - (iii) a proven record as a reliable source of liquidity even in stress; and - (iv) not an obligation of a financial institution or any of its associated entities.

If all four hold, it is HQLA1. Included at market value, no haircut (Att A para 9). 5. Is it a sovereign or central bank debt security with a non-zero risk weight? It can be HQLA1 if issued in domestic currency by the sovereign in the country where the liquidity risk is taken or the bank's home country (Att A para 9(d)), or, if in foreign currency, only up to the bank's stressed net cash outflows in that currency in that jurisdiction (Att A para 9(e)). 6. HQLA2A (15% haircut) if recognised by APRA or the relevant regulator (Att A para 10): - sovereign, central bank, PSE or MDB paper at a 20% risk weight, with deep markets, a proven record (price decline of no more than 10% or haircut rise of no more than 10 percentage points over 30 days in a stress period) and not issued by a financial institution (Att A para 10(a)); or - corporate debt (including commercial paper) not issued by a financial institution, plain vanilla, or covered bonds not issued by the ADI or an associated entity, rated at least AA- (or the IRB equivalent) with deep markets and a proven record (Att A para 10(b)). 7. HQLA2B if recognised (Att A para 12): residential mortgage-backed securities (RMBS) meeting criteria (i) to (vii), 25% haircut (para 12(a)); corporate debt rated A+ to BBB-, 50% haircut (para 12(b)); common equity shares meeting criteria (i) to (vi), 50% haircut (para 12(c)). 8. Per APRA's liquidity FAQ, in Australian dollars only notes and coin, balances with the RBA, and Australian Government and semi-government securities are HQLA1, and no AUD assets currently qualify as HQLA2. So for Example Bank, any AUD security that is not one of those is not HQLA, however good its rating. 9. Operational requirements (Att A paras 22 and 23). Every asset in the stock must meet all of these: - available to be converted into cash at any time (para 22(a)); - unencumbered and under the control of the function charged with managing liquidity risk (typically the treasurer), who has continuous authority and the legal and operational capability to sell it (para 22(b)); - control evidenced either by a separate pool managed solely as a source of contingent funds, or by showing the function can sell the asset at any time in the 30-day stress and use the proceeds without conflicting with a stated business or risk strategy (para 22(c)); - not pledged to secure, collateralise or credit enhance any transaction, and not designated to cover operational costs such as rents and salaries (para 23). 10. If an asset becomes ineligible (for example a rating downgrade), it may stay in the stock for an additional 30 calendar days to allow replacement (Att A para 25). Record the date it became ineligible and the day it must leave. 11. Value and apply the haircut and caps. HQLA is held at market value; haircuts are 0% for HQLA1, 15% for HQLA2A, 25% for RMBS HQLA2B, 50% for other HQLA2B. Total HQLA2 may be at most 40% of the stock, and HQLA2B at most 15% (Att A para 6), calculated on adjusted amounts after unwinding secured transactions maturing within 30 days (Att A paras 7 and 8). Document 08 shows the cap calculation. 12. No double counting. If an asset is in the stock, its maturity cash must not also be counted as an inflow (Att A para 31).

8. Twelve worked classification examples

Each example shows the facts, the reasoning in steps, and the result for both ratios. All amounts are invented. Check every multiplication yourself.

Example 1: A stable retail transaction account

Facts. Mei holds A$180,000 in a transaction account into which her salary is paid. It is her only deposit at the bank.

Reasoning.

  1. Natural person, so retail (tree D1, Att A para 34).
  2. FCS cover: A$250,000 per person per bank. Her total is A$180,000, so all of it is covered.
  3. The account is transactional (salary credited), so the covered balance is stable (Att A para 37(b)).
  4. LCR rate for stable deposits: 5% (Att A Table 3).
  5. NSFR: stable demand deposit, under one year: 95% (Att C para 12(a)).

Result. LCR outflow 5% x A$180,000 = A$9,000. NSFR available stable funding 95% x A$180,000 = A$171,000.

Example 2: An on-line saver above the guarantee limit

Facts. Raj has A$320,000 in an on-line saver at a normal (non-promotional) rate. He has had a home loan with the bank for eight years. He has no other deposits.

Reasoning.

  1. Retail (tree D1).
  2. Example Bank convention: on-line accounts are never stable, so the whole balance is less stable and is scored (tree D2).
  3. Scorecard: balance above A$250,000? Yes, 2. On-line? Yes, 2. No established relationship? No (he has a home loan), 0. Rate-driven? No, 0. Total 4.
  4. Score of 3 or more gives 25% (Att A Table 2).
  5. NSFR: less stable retail, under one year: 90% (Att C para 13(a)).

Result. LCR outflow 25% x A$320,000 = A$80,000. NSFR 90% x A$320,000 = A$288,000.

Watch out: If Raj's balance were A$200,000 the score would be 2 (on-line only), so the rate would be 10% and the outflow A$20,000. The A$250,000 line is a cliff for scoring. That is why the balance must be the customer's total deposits, because the FCS limit is per person.

Example 3: Term deposits and the "no right to withdraw" test

Facts. Three cases of the same product, a 12-month term deposit that says the customer has no legal right to withdraw early. Each is A$50,000. The customer has no other relationship with the bank, so the deposit is less stable.

Reasoning and result.

  • (a) Matures in 90 days. Residual maturity is over 30 days and there is no right to withdraw, so the outflow is excluded: LCR 0% (Att A para 41). NSFR: less stable retail under one year, 90% x A$50,000 = A$45,000 (Att C para 13(a)).
  • (b) Matures in 20 days. The depositor gets the money inside the window, so tree D2 applies. Scorecard: balance not above the limit, 0; not on-line, 0; no relationship, 1; not rate-driven, 0. Total 1, so 10%. LCR outflow 10% x A$50,000 = A$5,000. NSFR still A$45,000.
  • (c) The pool test. In the past quarter, branches let 60 customers out of the 90-day product, and only 4 of them were genuine hardship cases under the bank's hardship policy. The other 56 are early withdrawals despite the clause. The entire category of these funds must be treated as demand deposits (Att A para 42). The hardship cases do not count against the bank (Att A para 43). Case (a) is now scored as a demand deposit: 10%, so A$5,000.

Example 4: Who is the customer?

Facts. Two deposits. (1) A wealth platform has placed A$20m of its clients' money with the bank in a pooled account. The platform agreement lets the platform move the money to other banks without asking clients. (2) Smith Hardware Pty Ltd, a non-financial company managed by a business banker, has deposits of A$3.4m and no other funding from the bank.

Reasoning, deposit (1).

  1. It arrives through an intermediary (tree D1 step 1).
  2. Test the conditions in Att A para 35. Condition (b), the person exercises the withdrawal rights in practice and cannot transfer them to the intermediary, fails. Condition (d), the intermediary cannot withdraw without specific directions, fails.
  3. So the deposit is from a financial institution: tree D5: 100%.
  4. NSFR: it is funding from a financial institution under six months (at call): 0% (Att C para 15(a)).

Result. LCR outflow A$20m (100%). NSFR A$0.

Reasoning, deposit (2).

  1. Not a natural person; it is a company, so wholesale unless the SME test is met.
  2. Total aggregated funding is A$3.4m, which is not under A$2 million, so it fails Att A para 46(b). Wholesale.
  3. Not operational (no custody, clearing or cash management dependency evidenced).
  4. Non-financial corporation, not fully insured (A$3.4m exceeds the FCS cover): 40% (Att A Table 3).
  5. NSFR: funding under one year from a non-financial corporate: 50% (Att C para 14(a)).

Result. LCR outflow 40% x A$3.4m = A$1.36m. NSFR 50% x A$3.4m = A$1.70m.

If Smith Hardware's total funding had been A$1.4m and it had been managed as a retail exposure, it would have qualified as an SME treated as retail (Att A para 46) and gone through tree D2.

Example 5: An operational deposit with excess, and a super fund

Facts. (1) A corporate customer holds A$40m in a cash management account under a binding agreement with 90 days' notice of termination. Liquidity Risk's analysis shows A$30m is needed to run the payment activity; the rest is excess. Not fully insured. (2) A superannuation fund trustee holds A$80m at call in a savings product. It is not an operational relationship.

Reasoning (1).

  1. Services: cash management, institutional customer, binding agreement, 90 days' notice (at least 30 days): tests in Att A paras 47 and 48 pass.
  2. By-product, designated account, no incentive to leave excess funds: Att A para 49 passes.
  3. Split: A$30m operational, A$10m excess (Att A para 50).
  4. Operational, not fully insured: 25%. The excess is a non-operational deposit from a non-financial corporation, not fully insured: 40%.
  5. NSFR: operational 50% (Att C para 14(b)); the excess is under one year from a non-financial corporate, 50% (Att C para 14(a)).

Result. LCR outflow (25% x A$30m) + (40% x A$10m) = 7.5 + 4.0 = A$11.5m. NSFR 50% x A$40m = A$20.0m.

Reasoning (2).

  1. A trustee of a superannuation fund is a financial institution (APS 210 para 10(b)).
  2. Not operational. Non-operational deposit from a financial institution: "all other non-operational deposits": 100% (Att A Table 3).
  3. NSFR: funding from a financial institution under six months: 0% (Att C para 15(a)).

Result. LCR outflow A$80m. NSFR A$0.

Variant: if A$30m of the fund's money sat in a custody account that passes all the operational tests, that A$30m would be 25% (A$7.5m) and 50% ASF (A$15m), and the remaining A$50m would stay at 100% and 0%.

Example 6: A government bond, valued two ways, and the same bond pledged

Facts. The bank holds A$100m face value of Australian Government bonds. The market value today is A$98.5m. The accounting carrying value (amortised cost) is A$100.3m. The bonds are in Treasury's liquid asset portfolio and are unencumbered.

Reasoning.

  1. A marketable claim on the Australian Government, zero risk weight, deep market: HQLA1 (Att A para 9(c)).
  2. HQLA1 is counted at market value with no haircut (Att A para 9).
  3. Operational tests pass: available, unencumbered, controlled by Treasury, in a designated pool (Att A paras 22 and 23).
  4. NSFR: unencumbered HQLA1: 5% of carrying value (Att C para 30; Att C para 17(a)).

Result. HQLA A$98.5m. RSF 5% x A$100.3m = A$5.015m.

The same bond pledged. Treasury repos the bonds for 14 days to fund cash.

  • LCR: the bonds are encumbered, so they leave the stock of HQLA (Att A para 23). The repo cash borrowed is secured funding backed by HQLA1, so the run-off rate is 0% (Att A Table 3). The stock falls by A$98.5m and there is no outflow to offset it. Treasury must weigh that loss of liquid assets against the funding it raises.
  • NSFR: encumbered with under six months remaining: the same factor as unencumbered: 5% (Att C para 23). RSF stays A$5.015m.
  • If the repo ran for nine months: encumbered for six months to under one year. An HQLA asset encumbered for that period takes 50% (Att C paras 23 and 34(b)). RSF 50% x A$100.3m = A$50.15m.
  • If it ran for 15 months: 100% (Att C paras 23 and 37(a)): A$100.3m.

Example 7: A bond that looks good but is not HQLA

Facts. (1) The bank holds A$20m (carrying value; market value A$19.4m) of AAA-rated residential mortgage-backed securities issued by another lender. They are in Australian dollars. Remaining term is four years. (2) The bank holds A$50m of senior bonds issued by another bank, maturing in 20 days.

Reasoning (1).

  1. RMBS can be HQLA2B only if they meet the criteria in Att A para 12(a) and are recognised by APRA. Per APRA's liquidity FAQ, no AUD assets currently qualify as HQLA2. So these are not HQLA.
  2. Not in the stock: no LCR numerator. No inflow either, because they do not mature inside 30 days.
  3. NSFR: unencumbered securities with remaining maturity of one year or more that do not qualify as HQLA: 85% (Att C para 36(c)).

Result. LCR: nothing. NSFR RSF 85% x A$20.0m = A$17.0m.

Reasoning (2).

  1. Bonds issued by a bank are an obligation of a financial institution, so they are not HQLA1 (Att A para 9(c)(iv)).
  2. They are maturing securities not included in the stock of HQLA: inflow 100% (Att A para 62), so A$50m.
  3. NSFR: other non-HQLA with residual maturity under one year: 50% (Att C para 34(f)).

Result. LCR inflow A$50m. RSF 50% x A$50m = A$25m.

Example 8: Mortgages

Facts. A loan of A$600,000 to Dana, an individual, secured on her home (owner-occupied). LVR 78% as defined under APS 112. 24 years remaining. Fully performing. Unencumbered. Monthly repayment A$3,300 due within the next 30 days.

Reasoning.

  1. Fully performing, due date inside 30 days: inflow possible (Att A paras 54 and 60).
  2. Retail customer: 50% (Att A para 61).
  3. NSFR: standard residential loan to an individual, one year or more, LVR 80% or below, unencumbered: 65% (Att C para 35(a)).

Result. LCR inflow 50% x A$3,300 = A$1,650. NSFR RSF 65% x A$600,000 = A$390,000.

What changes the answer.

  • LVR 85%: no longer "80% or below", so it is another performing loan of one year or more: 85% (Att C para 36(b)). RSF 85% x A$600,000 = A$510,000.
  • 90 days past due: not fully performing, so no LCR inflow (Att A para 54). NSFR 100% (Att C para 37(c)).
  • Securitised into a vehicle that holds the loan for 2 years: encumbered for one year or more: 100% (Att C para 23).

Example 9: A loan to another bank, and a reverse repo

Facts. The bank has A$200m outstanding with another ADI, maturing in 20 days. Four versions.

Reasoning and result.

  • (a) Unsecured loan. Fully performing, due inside 30 days, borrower is a financial institution: inflow 100% (Att A para 62(a)) = A$200m. NSFR: loan to a financial institution under six months, not secured by HQLA1: 15% (Att C para 33(b)) = A$30m.
  • (b) Reverse repo, collateral is Australian Government bonds, freely rehypothecable, not used to cover short positions. Table 4: HQLA1 collateral gives 0% inflow (Att A para 55). The bank is assumed to roll it over. The collateral received may count in the stock of HQLA if held, not rehypothecated and available (Att A para 23). NSFR: loan to a financial institution under six months, secured by HQLA1 with free rehypothecation: 10% (Att C para 31) = A$20m.
  • (c) Reverse repo secured by corporate bonds that are not HQLA. "All other collateral": inflow 100% (Att A Table 4) = A$200m. NSFR 15% = A$30m.
  • (d) Reverse repo with no maturity date. NSFR 100% unless the bank shows APRA a different effective maturity (Att C footnote 28) = A$200m.

Example 11: Derivatives and collateral

Facts. (LCR) Counterparty A has a valid master netting agreement: payables within 30 days A$30m, receivables A$22m. Counterparty B has no master netting agreement and the trades are not same-day FX swaps: payables A$5m, receivables A$4m. The bank would have to post A$12m more collateral on a 3-notch downgrade. The largest absolute net 30-day collateral flow in the past 24 months was A$9m. A$2m of excess non-segregated collateral could be called at any time.

(NSFR) Derivative contracts with negative replacement cost, net per eligible netting agreements: A$60m. Variation margin posted against them: A$35m. Contracts with positive replacement cost, net: A$18m, with no qualifying cash variation margin received. Initial margin posted in cash: A$10m.

Reasoning (LCR).

  1. Counterparty A: net payable 30 - 22 = A$8m. Outflow 100% = A$8m.
  2. Counterparty B: no netting allowed (Att A footnote 5). Payables A$5m outflow at 100% = A$5m; receivables A$4m inflow at 100% = A$4m (Att A para 65).
  3. Downgrade collateral A$12m at 100%. Largest net flow A$9m at 100%. Excess collateral A$2m at 100% (Att A Table 3).

Result (LCR). Outflows 8 + 5 + 12 + 9 + 2 = A$36m. Inflows A$4m.

Reasoning (NSFR).

  1. NSFR derivative liabilities = 60 - 35 = A$25m (Att C para 10).
  2. NSFR derivative assets = A$18m (Att C para 27).
  3. Liabilities exceed assets, so the net A$7m takes 0% ASF (Att C para 15(c)), and there is no 100% RSF item (Att C para 37(b)).
  4. RSF 20% of derivative liabilities before deducting variation margin: 20% x A$60m = A$12m (Att C para 37(d)).
  5. Initial margin: 85% x A$10m = A$8.5m (Att C para 36(a)).
  6. The A$35m variation margin asset is not given an RSF (Att C footnote 12).

Result (NSFR). RSF A$20.5m; ASF A$0 from the net derivative liability.

10. When an attribute is missing

Sometimes a record arrives without a field you need. The rule of thumb is: take the conservative category and record it. "Conservative" means the answer that makes the LCR or NSFR look worse. Higher outflow, lower inflow, lower available funding, higher required funding. Never fill a gap with the favourable answer because "it is probably fine".

10.1 The procedure

  1. Detect. The completeness and validity checks in section 9 list the records.
  2. Default. Apply the conservative category from the table below, so the calculation can proceed.
  3. Log. Write each case to the data exceptions register: date, system, record, amount, missing field, default applied, owner asked, due date.
  4. Ask. Contact the data owner for the true value.
  5. Fix at the source. The correct value is entered in the source system. Do not fix it by hand in the report: a fix in the report vanishes tomorrow and leaves no trail.
  6. Reverse the default once the true value arrives, and record the effect on the ratio.
  7. Report. Include the count and amount of defaulted records in the daily pack. If defaults exceed the materiality level in Example Bank's data quality procedure, tell the controller the same day. Repeated defaults on the same field signal a systemic problem: raise it with the system owner and Liquidity Risk.

11. How classification rules are documented, approved and changed

The rules in this document are the standard's. Example Bank also needs its own written rulebook that turns each rule into a precise test on data fields. The rulebook is the evidence that the bank has policies, procedures and controls for identifying, measuring and monitoring liquidity risk (APS 210 para 16(c)), and that the liquidity risk management framework and practices are documented and reviewed at least annually (APS 210 para 17(b)).

Step Who

Independent challenge Liquidity Risk (APS 210 para 28 requires an operationally independent oversight function able to challenge Treasury and other liquidity risk management businesses)

11.3 Change procedure

Typical practice: The following is typical; confirm with your controller.

  1. Trigger. A new product; a change to APS 210 or APRA's guidance; an error found; a change to a source system; the annual review; a regulator query.
  2. Draft the new or amended rule record.
  3. Impact analysis. Run the old and new rules in parallel on the latest month-end and the last 20 business days. Record the effect on LCR, NSFR and each line.
  4. Review and challenge. Second preparer, controller and Liquidity Risk.
  5. Approve at the right level. Keep minutes.
  6. Implement in the system or spreadsheet under change control (document 12). Test with the rule's test cases.
  7. Communicate to Treasury, product owners and everyone who reads the daily LCR.
  8. Record the effective date. Past results are not restated unless an error is being corrected (see document 12 for errors).
  9. Tell APRA if needed. If a new treatment depends on an interpretation of the standard that is not obvious, ask APRA before relying on it. Note that APRA assesses the suitability of the assumptions behind derived cash flow items, and the ADI must be able to provide analysis and evidence to justify them (Att A para 32). If the bank uses a previous exemption or exercise of discretion by APRA under an older version of the standard, it must contact APRA (APS 210 para 72).

11.4 Annual review

At least once a year, the owner re-reads every rule against the current standard, runs the test cases, reviews the judgements register and the exceptions register for recurring problems, and presents the results to ALCO. This supports the Board's duty to ensure the liquidity risk management framework is documented and reviewed at least annually (APS 210 para 17(b)) and the requirement that it be subject to effective review on an ongoing basis (APS 210 para 35).

11.5 Evidence kept

  • The rulebook with version history and the minutes of approvals.
  • Impact analyses and test results.
  • The data exceptions register and the judgements register.
  • Daily data quality check results.
  • Source-system extracts for each reporting date.

Check yourself

  1. Priya has A$60,000 in a salary account and A$100,000 in a 6-month term deposit with 20 days to run, with no right to withdraw early. She has no other deposits. What are the LCR outflow and the NSFR available stable funding for her two deposits?

Answer: She is a natural person: retail. Her total is A$160,000, under the A$250,000 FCS cover. The salary account is transactional: stable (Att A para 37(b)). The term deposit matures in 20 days, so it is inside the window (Att A para 41 applies only above 30 days). She has an established relationship through the salary account, so it is stable too (Att A para 37(a)). LCR: 5% x 160,000 = A$8,000. NSFR: stable, under one year, 95% x 160,000 = A$152,000 (Att C para 12(a)).

  1. A customer with no other products has A$30,000 in an on-line saver at the standard rate. What is the score, the rate and the outflow?

Answer: Balance above limit: no (0). On-line: yes (2). No established relationship: yes (1). Rate-driven: no (0). Total 3, so 25% (Att A Table 2). Outflow 25% x 30,000 = A$7,500. NSFR (less stable, 90%) = A$27,000.

  1. A company has A$2.5m of deposits and no other funding with the bank. It is not operational and is not fully insured. What are the rates and amounts?

Answer: Total funding is not below A$2m, so it is not a qualifying SME (Att A para 46). Non-financial corporate, not fully insured: 40% (Att A Table 3) = A$1.0m. NSFR: funding under one year from a non-financial corporate: 50% (Att C para 14(a)) = A$1.25m.

  1. A super fund trustee holds A$20m at call, not operational. What is the LCR outflow and the ASF?

Answer: The trustee of a superannuation fund is a financial institution (APS 210 para 10(b)). 100% of A$20m = A$20m outflow (Att A Table 3). ASF 0% (Att C para 15(a)).

  1. The bank holds government bonds with market value A$49.2m and carrying value A$50.0m, unencumbered. What goes into the HQLA and what is the RSF?

Answer: HQLA1 at market value: A$49.2m (Att A para 9). RSF is 5% of carrying value: 5% x 50.0 = A$2.5m (Att C para 30).

  1. A$20m is undrawn on a committed credit facility to another bank. What are the LCR and NSFR amounts?

Answer: Banks and other ADIs: 40% (Att A Table 3) = A$8.0m. NSFR 5% (Att C Table 1) = A$1.0m.

  1. A hedge fund has an undrawn committed A$10m facility described as a "credit line". How is it treated?

Answer: Facilities to hedge funds are captured in their entirety as a liquidity facility to a financial institution (Att A para 52): 100% = A$10m LCR outflow. NSFR 5% = A$0.5m.

  1. A counterparty without a master netting agreement owes the bank A$5m and the bank owes it A$6m on derivatives within 30 days. What goes in the LCR?

Answer: No netting without a valid master netting agreement (Att A footnote 5). Outflow A$6m at 100%; inflow A$5m at 100% (Att A para 65).

  1. The bank lets 20 customers break a 6-month "no early withdrawal" term deposit product for reasons other than hardship. What is the effect?

Answer: The entire category of these funds must be treated as demand deposits (Att A para 42). The outflow is no longer 0% for deposits more than 30 days from maturity. The product falls into the stable or less stable rates by customer (5%, 10% or 25%). Hardship withdrawals under a defined hardship policy would not have this effect (Att A para 43).

  1. A feed arrives with 400 deposit records missing the customer type, total A$80m. What do you do and what is the effect?

    Answer: Apply the conservative default: financial institution, non-operational, at call: 100% outflow, 0% ASF. Log the records, ask the data owner, and report the default count. The default produces A$80m of outflow. If the true class turns out to be stable retail at 5% (A$4m), the default overstates the outflow by A$76m. The movement analysis will show the jump, so you can explain it.

What to remember

  • Every liquidity number is a balance times a rate, and the rate depends on the record's attributes. Wrong attribute, wrong number.
  • Always ask six questions: which side, who, when, secured or not, special tests, which paragraph. Write the paragraph down.
  • The LCR uses market value for the liquid stock; the NSFR uses carrying value.
  • Deposits: natural person is retail; intermediated funds are financial institution deposits unless all four conditions are met (Att A para 35); a super fund trustee is a financial institution (APS 210 para 10(b)); SME under A$2m treated as retail (Att A para 46).
  • The scorecard: above the limit 2, on-line 2, no relationship 1, rate-driven 1. Total 0 to 2 is 10%; 3 or more is 25%.
  • A term deposit over 30 days with no withdrawal right is excluded, but one pattern of early withdrawals taints the whole category (Att A paras 41 to 43).
  • In Australian dollars, only notes and coin, RBA balances, and Australian Government and semi-government securities are HQLA, all HQLA1.
  • When data is missing, take the conservative category, log it, fix it at the source, and tell the controller if it is large.
  • Classification rules are written down, approved, versioned and tested. Never change a rate quietly.
