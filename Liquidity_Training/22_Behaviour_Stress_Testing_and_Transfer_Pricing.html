22 Behaviour, stress testing and funds transfer pricing: the methods

Purpose: To teach you the methods behind three things that document 10 (Monitoring beyond the two ratios) only describes: how customers' behaviour is measured and turned into cash flow assumptions, how a stress test is designed, run and used, and how the cost of liquidity is charged back to the products that cause it. Every method starts with a very small example and then runs on Example Bank.

You will be able to:

  • Explain why the standard needs both contractual and behavioural cash flows, and build a behavioural maturity ladder from a contractual one, step by step.
  • Measure deposit stickiness from history (run-off rates, cohorts, core versus volatile balances, decay curves, concentration) and model early withdrawal, facility drawdown, prepayment and rollover.
  • Design stress scenarios (name-specific, market-wide, combined, short and protracted), set and document the assumptions, and compute a survival horizon and a counterbalancing capacity table.
  • Run a reverse stress test and a sensitivity analysis of the LCR, and say how results feed limits, early warning indicators and the contingency funding plan.
  • Price a mortgage, a term deposit and a committed facility with matched-maturity funds transfer pricing, a liquidity premium curve and a charge for contingent liquidity.
  • Describe what model governance and validation should look like for these methods.

Before you start: Read document 02 (Liquidity from zero) and document 10 (Monitoring beyond the two ratios), especially its sections 2 (the maturity ladder), 4 (limits and early warning indicators), 5 (stress testing) and 7 (contingency funding plan). 0. How to read this document

What is a source and what is a method. Two kinds of statement appear below, and they are labelled so you can tell them apart.

  • Rules and supervisory expectations come from APS 210 (the standard, which binds the bank) or from APG 210 (APRA's practice guide, which does not bind but shows what APRA expects to see) or from the Basel Committee papers. They carry a citation such as (APS 210 para 36) or (APG 210 para 16) or a source mark such as [S2].
  • Methods (how to calculate a decay rate, how to build a liquidity premium curve) are not set by any of those documents. They are labelled Typical practice. Every percentage, curve and scenario shock in this document is invented for training. None of them is an Example Bank policy or an APRA number. Confirm real figures with your controller.

Typical practice: The methods in sections 2 to 13 are what banks commonly do. APRA does not prescribe a method for measuring stickiness, building a decay curve, setting an early warning band or pricing liquidity. It prescribes outcomes: sound processes, documented assumptions, limits, a cost allocation process, and use of the results (APS 210 para 36; APS 210 para 37; APS 210 para 39; APS 210 para 42; APS 210 paras 62 to 66).

1. Contractual and behavioural cash flows

1.1 The idea in everyday words

Imagine a friend lends you A$100 "whenever you need it back". In the contract, the A$100 is repayable the moment your friend asks. In practice your friend has not asked for it in three years, and is unlikely to, unless they lose their job. The contractual view says "A$100 can leave tomorrow". The behavioural view says "A$100 will probably stay for years, but we should plan for the chance it leaves".

A bank has millions of these relationships. Its deposits are written as "repayable on demand", but the balance as a whole stays for years.

  • A contractual cash flow is the cash that the legal terms say must be paid or received on a given date, or at the earliest date the other party can demand it.
  • A behavioural cash flow is the cash the bank expects to be paid or received, based on how customers actually act: how many leave early, how many renew, how many repay a loan ahead of schedule.

1.2 Why the standard needs both

APS 210 requires "a sound process for identifying, measuring, monitoring and controlling liquidity risk", including "a robust framework for comprehensively projecting cash flows arising from assets, liabilities and off-balance sheet items over an appropriate set of time horizons" (APS 210 para 36). The paragraph does not say contractual or behavioural. It needs both, for two different jobs.

Question View that answers it Why
What is the worst case if every customer exercises every right on day one? Contractual Objective, auditable, comparable. It is the legal truth
What will probably happen on an ordinary day or month? Behavioural Realistic, so funding plans and prices are not absurdly expensive
What happens when behaviour turns bad? Behavioural plus a stress overlay A stress test starts from behaviour and shocks it
What does APRA's own LCR assume? A prescribed stress, not either view Fixed run-off rates (Att A Table 3)

APG 210 reinforces the point. It expects cash flow forecasts "with sufficient granularity such that they capture all material sources of potential liquidity risk" (APG 210 para 11), expects a bank to estimate flows "under normal conditions and for a range of stress scenarios" (APG 210 para 9), and says the assumptions for "assets, liabilities and off-balance sheet items with uncertain cash flows" are "of particular importance" in stress (APG 210 para 37) [S2].

From the controller: If someone shows you a ladder, ask two questions before you read a number. Is it contractual, behavioural or stressed? And which assumption version is it using? Document 10 section 2 makes the same point with Example Bank's ladders, and this document explains where the behavioural assumptions come from.

The Basel Committee's principles say the same in international terms. Principle 5 is that a bank should have a sound process for identifying, measuring, monitoring and controlling liquidity risk [S4]. The first sentence of APS 210 para 36 uses the same words.

2. Measuring deposit stickiness from history

2.1 Start with one account

A customer has A$10,000 in an at-call account. Over six month-ends the balance is 10,000, 9,800, 10,100, 9,900, 10,050 and 9,950. Contractually, all A$10,000 can leave tomorrow. The lowest balance in the history is A$9,800, so 98% of the starting balance never left. One account tells you little, but the same arithmetic on millions of accounts is the method.

2.2 The run-off rate

A run-off rate is the share of a balance that leaves in a period.

Run-off rate = (Opening balance - Closing balance) / Opening balance

A pool opens at A$100 and closes 30 days later at A$94. The run-off rate is (100 - 94) / 100 = 6.0%. Looking at a pool over its first ten days, balances of 100.0, 99.6, 99.5, 99.0, 98.9, 98.2, 97.9, 97.0, 96.2, 95.4 and 95.0 give a ten-day run-off of 5.0% and a worst single day of 0.9% of the opening balance.

Two things matter beyond the average.

  1. The distribution. Take the monthly net outflow of a stable pool over 12 months (as a percentage of the pool): 0.6, 0.8, 0.7, 0.9, 1.1, 0.7, 0.8, 1.3, 0.9, 0.7, 0.8 and 1.0. The mean is 0.858% and the standard deviation 0.198%. The worst month was 1.3%. Mean plus two standard deviations is 1.253%. A bank that plans for the average will be caught in the bad months, so it also records the worst month and a high percentile.
  2. The data window. APG 210 warns that "historical analysis of ADI deposit loss is informative", but available Australian data "is unlikely to represent a stress event at the severity required for prudent liquidity risk management" (APG 210 para 18) [S2]. Normal-time history sets the behavioural base. It does not set stress run-off. Stress assumptions are judgement, informed by history from other places and times.

Typical practice: Banks measure run-off over several windows (1 day, 5 days, 30 days, 90 days) and for each product and customer segment, because a single average hides the segments that leave first.

2.3 Why some deposits are stickier than others

APG 210 lists factors that influence stickiness: "size, interest-rate sensitivity and the nature of the deposit channel, e.g. direct, internet or broker" (APG 210 para 16) [S2]. The LCR itself encodes the same idea in the scorecard (Att A Table 1): a balance above the guarantee limit scores 2, an on-line account 2, no established relationship 1, a heavily rate-driven deposit 1. A total of 0 to 2 gives a 10% run-off and 3 or more gives 25% (Att A Table 1; Att A paras 36 to 40). Your own history is how you test whether those regulatory splits fit your customers.

2.4 Vintage (cohort) analysis

A cohort (or vintage) is a group of accounts that opened in the same period. Track each cohort's balance over time, indexed to 100 at the start. It answers a question that an average cannot: do customers who join in a rate promotion leave faster than customers who join for a salary account?

2.5 Decay curves

A decay curve describes how a balance falls over time with a simple formula. The commonest is exponential decay:

Surviving share at month t = exp(-lambda x t)
Half-life (months)          = ln(2) / lambda

Here lambda is the monthly decay rate, exp is the exponential function and ln the natural logarithm. A way to estimate lambda from a cohort is to fit a line through the logarithm of the surviving share. The least squares estimate through the origin is lambda = - sum(t x ln S(t)) / sum(t squared), using months 1 to 6.

Cohort lambda per month Half-life (months) Predicted survival after 1 month after 12 months
Jan 0.0195 35.6 98.1% 79.2%
Apr 0.0262 26.4 97.4% 73.0%
Jul 0.0381 18.2 96.3% 63.3%

Read the Jul row: the model says that after a year only 63.3% of that cohort's balance would still be here, and half would be gone by month 18. You can now test the number a Treasury profile uses. Document 10's behavioural profile for stable retail deposits (A$25.0bn) puts 1.0 in the first 30 days, 1.5 in the next bucket, 2.5 in the next and 20.0 beyond a year. That profile implies cumulative leaving of 4.0%, 10.0% and 20.0% at the end of the first three buckets, which is the kind of figure a decay curve fitted to a very sticky cohort would give.

Typical practice: Exponential decay is easy to fit and explain but assumes the rate of leaving never changes. Many banks use two or three curves (a fast, a medium and a slow part of the same pool) or a floor (a core amount that never leaves in the model). Whatever the choice, state it, validate it (section 15) and record the fit period.

Watch out: A short history flatters. Six months of cohort data cannot show what happens at month 18 and cannot show a crisis. The curve extrapolates the past. Label the data window on every curve.

2.6 Core versus volatile balances

The core part of a pool is the amount that has stayed for a long time. The volatile part is the amount that comes and goes. Month-end balances of a pool (A$m) over 12 months: 1,000, 1,040, 1,010, 985, 1,020, 1,075, 1,060, 990, 1,005, 1,030, 1,090, 1,045.

Method Core Volatile Core share of the average
Floor method: core = lowest balance in the window 985.0 average 1,029.17 less 985 = 44.17 95.7%
Statistical method: core = average less two standard deviations (sd 33.70) 961.77 1,029.17 less 961.77 = 67.40 93.5%

The largest one-month fall in the series is A$70m. The floor method is simple but one unusual low month sets the answer. The statistical method adapts but depends on the assumed spread. Both give the same message: about 94 to 96% of this pool behaves as core.

2.7 Concentration

A deposit pool with a few giant depositors is stickier on paper than in practice, because one decision removes a large piece. Take a A$600m pool whose ten largest depositors hold (A$m) 60, 45, 40, 30, 25, 20, 18, 15, 12 and 10.

For behaviour, the lesson is this: model large depositors by name (APG 210 para 13 asks a bank to watch the concentration of expected inflows, and the standard's liquidity strategy covers "diversity and stability of funding sources", APS 210 para 29), and do not let a pool average hide them.

3. Early withdrawal of term deposits and drawdown of facilities

3.1 Term deposits: the contract says no, the bank sometimes says yes

A term deposit is a deposit locked for a fixed time, with a penalty for early withdrawal. In the LCR, deposits with more than 30 days left are excluded from outflows only if "the depositor has no legal right to withdraw deposits within the 30-day horizon", or if early withdrawal "results in a significant penalty that is materially greater than the loss of interest" (Att A para 41). If the bank lets customers withdraw anyway, "the entire category of these funds must be treated as demand deposits" (Att A para 42). A bank may allow withdrawal for hardship under a hardship policy without losing the treatment (Att A para 43).

Watch out: That is why a behavioural early-withdrawal model must never be used to allow routine breaks. The data tells you what customers try. The bank's practice (and what the data records as hardship) decides whether the entire pool falls under Att A para 42.

Method. Measure the share of original balances broken before maturity, by period. Example: a A$50m book of 12-month deposits. The share of original balances broken early, by quarter, is 0.5%, 0.8%, 1.2% and 1.5%. Cumulative early breaks over the year are 4.0%, or A$2.0m.

Item Calculation Result
Observed early breaks over 12 months 0.5 + 0.8 + 1.2 + 1.5 4.0% = A$2.0m
Stress: four times the first quarter's rate 4 x 0.5% 2.0% = A$1.0m in the first quarter
Stress: four times the whole year 4 x 4.0% 16.0%

A stress multiple of 4 is invented for training (Typical practice). It is a judgement about severity.

3.2 Facilities: how much of the undrawn is drawn

An undrawn committed facility is a promise to lend that the customer has not yet used. In a crisis customers draw on it. APG 210 says the bank should "ascertain a level of expected cash outflows under normal conditions and then estimate the scope for an increase in these flows during periods of stress" (APG 210 para 26), and that management must analyse trigger events "even if there has been no historical evidence of such flows" (APG 210 para 21) [S2]. The LCR uses fixed rates (Att C Table 1 for the NSFR; Att A Table 3 for the LCR; Att A paras 51 to 52 for liquidity facilities).

Example pool: limits A$1,000m, drawn A$400m, undrawn A$600m. In the worst month of history customers drew 6.0% of the undrawn amount.

Step Calculation Result
Worst month of history 6.0% of undrawn A$36m
Stress multiple (invented) 6.0% x 2.5 15.0% of undrawn
Stress draw 15.0% x 600 A$90m
LCR rate for a corporate credit facility 10% x 600 A$60m
Use the higher for the internal stress max(90, 60) A$90m

The lesson is that history sets a floor for the internal stress and the regulatory rate sets another. The internal stress uses whichever is more severe, and the bank documents why.

Typical practice: Draw behaviour differs by type: a corporate liquidity back-stop is drawn when markets close; a retail credit card limit is drawn slowly; an undrawn facility to a hedge fund or a securitisation vehicle must be treated "in their entirety as a liquidity facility to a financial institution" in the LCR (Att A para 52), so assume 100% in your own stress too.

4. Prepayment and rollover of loans

4.1 Prepayment

A prepayment is a borrower repaying part or all of a loan ahead of the contract schedule. It brings asset cash flows forward. The usual measure is the conditional prepayment rate (CPR), the share of the balance prepaid in a year, and its monthly form, the single monthly mortality (SMM):

SMM = 1 - (1 - CPR) ^ (1/12)

Example: a A$1,000m mortgage pool with a CPR of 10% and a scheduled principal repayment of 0.35% of the balance each month.

Item Result
SMM for a CPR of 10% 0.8742% a month
Balance after 12 months with scheduled principal and prepayments A$862.9m
Repaid over 12 months A$137.1m (13.71%)
Repaid by the schedule alone A$41.2m

Prepayment is more than three times the scheduled repayment. The contractual ladder understates inflows from mortgages.

Watch out: Prepayment is helpful for liquidity only if the bank would let the customer leave. In a stress, borrowers do not repay early: they are more likely to be stuck. The LCR gives no credit for prepayment: it counts only contractual inflows from fully performing loans (Att A paras 60 to 62), and it assumes the bank keeps lending (Att A para 61). Use prepayment in the behavioural ladder, not in a stress.

4.2 Rollover

Rollover means a short loan is renewed at maturity so the cash does not come back. A business loan of A$100m falls due. If 70% of such loans renew on the bank's history, the behavioural inflow is only A$30m. The LCR makes the same kind of assumption in a prescribed way: it counts 100% of fully performing contractual inflows but assumes the bank keeps lending, 50% of inflows to retail, SME and most corporate customers, so net inflow is 50% (Att A para 61; Att A para 62). For an interbank loan the LCR assumes no new lending, so the inflow is 100% (Att A para 62).

5. Building a behavioural maturity ladder from a contractual one

Document 10 section 2 built Example Bank's contractual and behavioural ladders. This section shows how the behavioural one is made, then extends it with the behaviours from sections 3 and 4. The buckets are the document 10 buckets: B1 at call to 30 days, B2 31 days to under 6 months, B3 6 months to under 1 year, B4 1 year or more.

Procedure.

Trigger: quarterly refresh of assumptions, and whenever Treasury or Liquidity Risk asks for a new version. Inputs: the contractual ladder; the approved behavioural assumption table (with version and date); the history behind each assumption (sections 2 to 4). Steps:

  1. Take the contractual ladder and check that assets equal liabilities and equity (A$100.0bn).
  2. For each at-call deposit line, replace the single "at call" entry with the behavioural profile (cumulative share leaving by bucket).
  3. For term deposits, apply early withdrawal and then the renewal rate.
  4. For loans, apply the renewal rate to short loans and prepayment to long ones.
  5. Keep wholesale debt, other liabilities and equity contractual unless the policy says otherwise.
  6. Check that each line still sums to its balance and that the ladder still closes to zero.
  7. Add off-balance sheet draws below the closing line as a memo (they are not balance sheet items yet).
  8. Compare the cumulative gap with its limit and write the commentary. Controls: every line sums to its balance; the ladder closes to zero; the assumption version matches the approved one; a second person recomputes one line by hand. Outputs: the behavioural ladder and a one-line statement of every assumption changed. Who reviews: second analyst; Treasury confirms the assumptions; Liquidity Risk challenges. Evidence kept: the contractual input, the assumption table with version, the calculation file, the sign-off.

6. Designing stress scenarios

6.1 What the standard requires

An LCR ADI must conduct stress tests "on a regular basis for a variety of short-term and protracted institution-specific and market-wide stress scenarios (individually and in combination)" (APS 210 para 62). Results must adjust strategy, policies and positions and feed contingency plans (APS 210 para 63). The tests must show the effect on the Level 1 and Level 2 positions and on the positions of individual business lines (APS 210 para 64). Scenarios and assumptions must be "well documented and reviewed together with the stress test results" (APS 210 para 65).

APG 210 adds what APRA would expect to see [S2]:

  • Stress tests analyse effects on "cash flows, liquidity position, profitability and solvency" (APG 210 para 79).
  • A "broad range of scenarios" at different levels, including "firm-wide, as well as customer type, product, business, currency and entity-specific stress events" (APG 210 para 83).
  • Forward-looking scenarios, not only a replay of past episodes (APG 210 para 82), and a warning against a "failure of imagination" (APG 210 para 81).
  • Longer horizons, noting "the increased importance of assumptions as the time horizon of a scenario is lengthened" (APG 210 para 84).
  • A list of adverse assumptions to include where relevant, among them erosion in the value of liquid assets, run-off of retail funding, secured and unsecured wholesale funding availability, margin calls, draws on committed lines, rating triggers, access to central bank facilities and "the operational ability of the ADI to monetise assets" (APG 210 para 86).

The Basel Committee's Principle 10 is the international source: a bank "should conduct stress tests on a regular basis for a variety of short-term and protracted" scenarios [S4]. Principle 12 asks for "a cushion of unencumbered, high quality liquid assets" held as insurance [S4], which is the counterbalancing capacity in section 8.

6.5 The daily path

A total is not enough, because liquidity runs out on a day. Outflows are front-loaded: customers who are going to leave tend to leave early. A simple shape for month 1 is a declining weight on each day: weight(day) = exp(-0.10 x day), scaled so the 30 weights add to 1. Day 1 gets 10.0% of the month's outflow, day 30 gets 0.55%, and the first seven days carry 53.0%. Months 2 and 3 are spread evenly. Wholesale debt due within 30 days is spread evenly over days 1 to 30. The three later lumps fall on their dates. Inflows are spread evenly.

Typical practice: The decay rate of 0.10 a day is invented. Banks calibrate the front-loading from past events, from the stress periods of other banks, and from judgement.

8. Survival horizon and the counterbalancing capacity table

8.1 Basics

The survival horizon is the number of days a bank can pay everything that falls due before its liquid resources run out (document 10 section 5.2). The counterbalancing capacity (CBC) is the set of resources the bank can turn into cash, after haircuts and after the time it takes. The horizon is the first day the cumulative net outflow is greater than the CBC available on that day.

Small example 1: CBC is 100 and outflows are a flat 10 a day: horizon 10 days. If 4 a day comes in, the net is 6 and the horizon is 100 / 6 = 16.7 days.

Small example 2 (front-loaded): CBC is 100 and daily outflows are 35, 25, 15, 10, 8, 6, 5, 4 and 2 (total 110). The cumulative outflow is 35, 60, 75, 85, 93, 99, 104 and so on. It passes 100 on day 7. A flat average of 110 / 9 = 12.2 a day would have said 8.2 days. Both are wrong in different ways, which is why the path method is used.

Watch out: Document 10 section 5.3 shows a straight-line horizon of 29.4 days for a simple multiple of the LCR. It is called a teaching shortcut there. The path method in this section is the proper form, and the scenarios in section 6 are different scenarios from that example, so the two numbers do not conflict.

9. Reverse stress testing

9.1 Basics

A forward stress test chooses the shock and finds the result. A reverse stress test chooses the result ("the buffer is gone by day 30" or "the LCR falls to 100%") and asks what shock would cause it. It finds the cliff edge.

Typical practice: Reverse stress testing is a common way for banks to examine how bad things must get. APS 210 and APG 210 do not use the words "reverse stress test" in the paragraphs read for this document, so this section is method, not a rule. APG 210 para 81 (a "failure of imagination" gives "a false sense of security") is the nearest supervisory support [S2].

11. Using results: limits and the contingency funding plan

11.1 What the standard requires

  • Stress test results "must be explicitly considered in the setting of internal limits" (APS 210 para 65), and the bank "must set limits to control its liquidity risk exposure and vulnerabilities" and review them regularly (APS 210 para 37).
  • Outcomes "must be used to adjust the ADI's liquidity management strategy, policies and positions and to develop effective contingency plans" (APS 210 para 63).
  • The contingency funding plan "must be closely integrated with the ADI's ongoing analysis of liquidity risk and with the assumptions used in its stress tests and the results of those stress tests" (APS 210 para 49), and the ADI "must decide how to incorporate the results of stress tests in assessing and planning for related potential funding shortfalls" (APS 210 para 66).
  • Results, vulnerabilities and resulting actions "must be reported to, and discussed with, the Board and APRA" (APS 210 para 65).
  • The plan must state each source, the amount, "the lead time needed to tap additional funds from each", and be reviewed and tested at least annually (APS 210 para 48; APS 210 para 51).
  • The funding strategy, an "ongoing" plan of three years, rests on "robust assumptions" (APS 210 paras 44 to 46). APG 210 expects it to include sensitivity analysis and second-order effects (APG 210 para 50; APG 210 para 52) [S2].
  • APG 210 adds that a bank would "regularly test key assumptions, such as the ability to sell or repo certain assets" and review the plan after each exercise (APG 210 para 58), and that realistic timelines for transferring liquidity and collateral are built into modelling (APG 210 para 56) [S2].

12. Early warning indicator calibration

12.1 Basics

An early warning indicator (EWI) is a measure that moves before trouble (document 10 section 4.4). APS 210 para 39 requires a set "to aid its daily liquidity risk management processes" that helps to identify negative trends and leads "to an assessment and potential response by management". APG 210 para 38 lists examples, among them "a reduction in the rate of deposit growth, unusual deposit loss or a material increase in notices given on minimum notice deposit periods", "a decrease in the weighted-average maturity of liabilities" and "negative publicity" [S2].

Calibration means choosing the amber and red lines. A line set too close to normal rings every week and people stop listening. A line set too far gives no warning. Calibration balances false alarms against lead time.

12.2 A worked example

The indicator is the five-day net change in retail deposits, as a percentage of the balance, observed weekly. Twenty weeks of normal history (the calibration window) and then six weeks leading up to a run (the "event" is week 26):

Weeks 1 to 10 0.12, -0.05, 0.20, -0.18, 0.08, -0.10, 0.15, -0.22, 0.05, -0.12
Weeks 11 to 20 0.18, -0.08, -0.15, 0.10, -0.25, 0.02, 0.14, -0.20, 0.06, -0.09
Weeks 21 to 26 -0.35, -0.90, -1.40, -2.20, -3.50, -5.00

Over weeks 1 to 20 the mean is -0.017% and the standard deviation is 0.1439%. Candidate amber lines at the mean less k standard deviations:

Method (Typical practice):

  1. Collect at least a year of history for the indicator, including any stress episode you have.
  2. Compute the mean and spread in normal times. Do not include the episode in the baseline.
  3. Try several lines and count false alarms and lead time on past episodes.
  4. Choose a line; add the red line from the CFP triggers or from a stress result (for example, the daily outflow at which survival falls below tolerance).
  5. Record the calibration window, the line and the date. Review at least annually (APS 210 para 37).
  6. Give each indicator a named owner, an escalation path, and an "as at" time.

Watch out: History here has a single episode, so the calibration is weak evidence. Add judgement from stress results: for example, if S1 says the bank survives only if retail outflows stay below some daily level, that level becomes the red line.

Typical practice: An indicator can be turned from a statistic into a stress-linked trigger: choose the daily outflow rate at which the survival horizon in the combined scenario would fall below the Board's tolerance, and use that as the red line.

13. Funds transfer pricing from basics

13.1 Why it exists

Imagine a family business with a shop, a bakery and a delivery van. The bakery is paid up front by customers in cash. The shop buys stock and waits months to be paid. If the family ignores the timing, the bakery looks poor and the shop looks rich, when really the bakery is lending cash to the shop. A fair accounts system charges the shop for the cash it uses and credits the bakery for the cash it supplies.

A bank is the same. Deposit-gathering teams supply money. Lending teams use money. Without an internal price, the lender looks profitable on loans funded by cheap short-term money, and the gatherer looks unprofitable on deposits that fund the whole bank.

Funds transfer pricing (FTP) is the internal system that charges each use of funds, and credits each source of funds, at an internal rate. The standard requires it in outcome form: "a costs and benefits allocation process for funding and liquidity that appropriately apportions the costs of prudent liquidity management to the sources of liquidity risk and provides appropriate incentives to manage liquidity risk" (APS 210 para 42).

APG 210 describes what APRA expects to see [S2]:

  • The process "will seek to align the risk-taking incentives of individual business lines with the liquidity risk exposures their activities create for the ADI as a whole", including for new product approvals, on and off balance sheet (APG 210 para 43).
  • Senior management incorporates funding and liquidity costs, benefits and risks "in the internal pricing, performance measurement and new product approval process for all significant business activities" (APG 210 para 44).
  • Costs are "explicitly attributed" with "a liquidity charge assigned, as appropriate, to positions, portfolios or individual transactions", reflecting "anticipated holding periods of assets and liabilities, their market liquidity risk characteristics" (APG 210 para 45).
  • When modelling the cost of a liquidity buffer, assets are costed "on a basis that reflects prudent funding over a longer term" (APG 210 para 46).
  • The framework is reviewed periodically, and "for larger, more complex ADIs this review is expected to be conducted on at least an annual basis" (APG 210 para 47).

The Basel Committee's Principle 4 is the international source: a bank "should incorporate liquidity costs, benefits and risks in the internal pricing, performance measurement" and new product approval [S4].

From the controller: FTP is not a Liquidity Reporting task. Treasury (or a funding and pricing team) runs it, Finance books it, and Liquidity Risk challenges it. You will meet it because the buffer cost it needs comes from your LCR and NSFR numbers, and because management reports are built on it.

13.2 The first building block: matched-maturity transfer pricing

Matched maturity means that each loan is charged the cost of borrowing for the same length of time that the loan is expected to last, and each deposit is credited for the same length of time it is expected to stay. A five-year fixed loan is funded in the model with five-year money, so a swing in short-term rates does not make the loan look better or worse than it is.

The price has two parts:

FTP rate = Base rate for the tenor + Liquidity premium for the tenor
  • The base rate is a market curve for the tenor with no bank credit or liquidity cost in it (for example a swap curve).
  • The liquidity premium is the extra cost a bank pays to borrow for that tenor in unsecured wholesale funding (and the extra it would earn for supplying long money). It rises with the tenor, because lenders ask for more to lock money up.

13.3 The liquidity premium curve

A liquidity premium curve gives the premium for each tenor. Typical practice: it is built from the bank's own cost of raising term funding, for example the spreads paid on its recent senior bonds and term deposits over the base curve, adjusted for market conditions, and then smoothed. It is approved by ALCO and refreshed regularly (at least annually, APG 210 para 47).

A product with a 0% run-off, such as a retail term deposit beyond 30 days, carries no buffer charge.

Typical practice: There is no single standard way to allocate the buffer cost. Some banks use the LCR run-off rates as above, some use their own stress scenario shocks (so section 6 feeds pricing), and some split the cost between the stressed outflow and the NSFR's stable funding need. Whatever the choice, it must be explained and reviewed. Allocating the cost of the whole HQLA, including the surplus above the requirement (5.45 here), is a choice: some banks charge only the part needed for the requirement and treat the surplus as a head office cost.

13.5 Worked example 1: pricing a mortgage

A new standard residential mortgage of A$500,000 is priced at 6.10%. The contract is 25 years, but the bank's behavioural analysis (prepayment, section 4.1) says the average life is about 4 years.

Step Working Result
1. Pick the tenor Behavioural life, not the contract 4 years
2. Base rate at 4 years Halfway between 4.40 (3y) and 4.60 (5y) 4.50
3. Liquidity premium at 4 years Halfway between 0.60 and 0.80 0.70
4. FTP charge 4.50 + 0.70 5.20
5. Margin over funding 6.10 - 5.20 0.90 (A$4,500 a year on A$500,000)
6. Contingent liquidity charge A mortgage does not run off within 30 days in the LCR, so nil (Att A) 0

If the contractual 25 years were used instead, the curve is capped at five years (FTP 5.40) and the margin would be 0.70. The difference of 20 basis points shows why the choice of life matters. After this the business adds credit costs, operating costs and capital to see whether the loan is worth writing.

(Whether a 139 basis point charge is realistic depends on the allocation choice in section 13.4.)

13.7 Worked example 3: pricing a committed facility

A committed facility has no balance until it is drawn. Matched-maturity funding cost applies only once money is drawn. But the bank must hold liquid assets against the chance of a draw. APG 210 treats this as the "creation of contingent exposures" that need a liquidity charge (APG 210 para 45) [S2].

A A$100m undrawn facility, 12 months, with a commitment fee of 0.30% a year on the undrawn amount (A$0.300m):

Facility type LCR rate Buffer charge (% a year of the undrawn) Charge (A$m a year) Fee (A$m) Net (A$m)
Corporate credit (general working capital) 10% 0.139 0.139 0.300 +0.161
Corporate liquidity (back-stop to maturing debt) 30% 0.417 0.417 0.300 -0.117
Hedge fund or a vehicle that finances the bank's own assets (Att A para 52) 100% 1.390 1.390 0.300 -1.090

On these inputs the 0.30% fee covers the buffer cost of a credit facility, does not cover a liquidity facility, and falls far short of a facility to a hedge fund. That is the information a pricing decision needs. Note the classification rules: a facility is a liquidity facility only for the amount of the customer's debt maturing within 30 days that it backstops, and a general working capital or revolving facility is a credit facility (Att A para 51).

Watch out: A second charge sometimes added is for the NSFR, where a loan or commitment needs stable funding (Att C Table 1: 5% of undrawn committed facilities). Matched-maturity pricing already charges the term cost of funding for the balance. If you also charge the NSFR on top of a term FTP rate, the bank pays twice. With the curve above, the cost gap between 3-year and 3-month funding is 0.85 percentage points. A 65% required stable funding factor applied again on a mortgage would add 55.3 basis points of double count; a 5% factor on an undrawn facility adds 4.25 basis points. Decide which method the bank uses and apply it once.

15. Model governance and validation

Everything in sections 2 to 13 is a model: inputs, assumptions and a calculation that produces a decision number. A model can be wrong without a single arithmetic mistake, because the assumption is wrong. APS 210 does not have a model chapter for liquidity, and the APG 210 paragraphs I read do not use the word "validation". What they do require is process: a sound process for measuring risk (APS 210 para 36), regular review of limits and procedures (APS 210 para 37), documented and reviewed stress scenarios and assumptions (APS 210 para 65), testing of key assumptions (APG 210 para 58), and a review of the framework at least annually (APS 210 para 17; APS 210 para 35; see document 12). The rest is Typical practice.

15.2 Validation: five questions

Validation is an independent check that a model is fit for its use. Five questions, Typical practice:

  1. Is the idea sound? Does the method suit the product? (Is exponential decay right for a rate-driven pool?)
  2. Is the data right? Complete, accurate, reconciled to the ledger, and the window long enough?
  3. Is the calculation right? An independent person (or script) recomputes the results from the inputs. This document's check22.py is an example of the habit.
  4. Does it work against what actually happened? Back-testing (section 15.3).
  5. How much does it matter? Sensitivity (section 10) and benchmarking against another method, another bank's disclosed figure or the LCR.

15.3 A back-testing example

The stable retail pool (25.0) is modelled with a behavioural net outflow of 1.0% a month (A$0.25bn). Over the next 12 months the actual net outflows were (percent): 0.6, 0.8, 0.7, 0.9, 1.1, 0.7, 0.8, 1.3, 0.9, 0.7, 0.8 and 1.0.

Measure Result
Months in which the actual exceeded the assumption 2 of 12 (1.1 and 1.3)
Average actual 0.858%
Bias (assumption less actual) +0.142 points (the assumption was cautious on average)
Mean absolute error 0.208 points
Worst month against the assumption 0.3 points over (1.3% against 1.0%)
Worst month in A$ 0.325bn against 0.25bn assumed; 0.075bn over

Invented tolerance rule: "three or more exceedances in 12 months triggers a review". Two exceedances is a pass with a note. The worst month matters more for stress: the monthly figure is for ordinary behaviour and the LCR's 5% over 30 days (1.25bn on this pool) is the stress.

Procedure for the annual validation (Typical practice): Trigger: annual cycle, a material model change, or a back-test breach. Inputs: model documentation, data extract, results, back-test history. Steps: review the design; recompute results independently; run the back-test; run sensitivities; compare with a benchmark; write findings with ratings; agree actions. Controls: the validator is not the builder; findings tracked to closure. Outputs: a validation report and an action list. Who reviews: Liquidity Risk; report to ALCO and the Board Risk Committee. Evidence kept: report, recomputation files, the action tracker.

Watch out: A model that "passed" last year can fail this year, because customers change. If a competitor launches a high-rate on-line saver and your cohorts start leaving faster (section 2.4), re-validate now.

Check yourself

  1. A pool opens at A$100m and is A$94m after 30 days. What is the run-off rate, and why is one pool-wide rate not enough? Answer: (100 - 94) / 100 = 6.0%. Different segments and cohorts leave at different speeds, and the early leavers are hidden in an average (section 2.4).
  2. Cash and liquid assets available are A$100m. Outflows are 12 a day and inflows 3 a day, flat. How many days does the bank survive? Answer: 100 / (12 - 3) = 11.1 days.
  3. The July cohort has a monthly decay rate of 0.0381. What are its half-life and survival after 12 months? Answer: Half-life ln 2 / 0.0381 = 18.2 months. Survival after 12 months exp(-12 x 0.0381) = 63.3%.
  4. A mortgage pool has a prepayment rate of 10% a year. What is the monthly rate, and why should it not be used in a stress? Answer: 1 - (0.90)^(1/12) = 0.8742% a month. In a stress customers do not repay early, and the LCR counts only contractual inflows from fully performing loans (Att A paras 60 to 62).
  5. In the worst month of history 6% of undrawn commitments were drawn. The stress uses 2.5 times that. Undrawn is A$600m and the LCR rate is 10%. What does the internal stress use? Answer: 6% x 2.5 = 15%, A$90m, higher than the LCR's A$60m, so use A$90m.
  6. Using the Example Bank curves, a mortgage with a 4-year behavioural life is priced at 6.10%. What is the margin over the FTP rate? Answer: FTP = 4.50 + 0.70 = 5.20, margin 0.90 percentage points.
  7. A bank charges 0.30% a year on an undrawn A$100m corporate liquidity facility. The buffer charge is 0.4171%. Is the facility profitable on liquidity grounds? Answer: No. Net = 0.30 - 0.4171 = -0.117% of 100m, a loss of A$0.117m a year before other costs.
  8. Why can no single wholesale line break Example Bank's 100% LCR, but a retail line can? Answer: The surplus over 100% is 5.45. Retail stable would need a 26.8% run-off and retail less stable 46.3%. SME, corporate operational and corporate non-operational would need more than 100% of their balances (119.0%, 161.3% and 130.8%) (section 9.2).
  9. A back-test shows 2 months in 12 above the assumed 1.0% outflow. The average actual is 0.858%. What does it say, and what does it not say? Answer: The assumption was cautious on average by 0.142 points and passed the invented 3-in-12 rule, but it does not show how the pool behaves in a crisis, because history from calm periods is not a stress (APG 210 para 18).
  10. Which paragraph of APS 210 says stress results must be considered in setting limits, and which paragraph says the funding shortfall planning in the contingency funding plan must use them? Answer: (APS 210 para 65) for limits; (APS 210 para 66) for the contingency funding plan.

What to remember

  • The standard needs both contractual and behavioural cash flows. Contractual is the legal worst case. Behavioural is the expected case. Label every ladder (APS 210 para 36).
  • Measure behaviour from history using run-off rates, cohorts, decay curves, core and volatile balances and concentration. But normal-time history is not a stress (APG 210 para 18).
  • Never use prepayment or renewal inflows in a stress. Never let early withdrawal become routine for term deposits (Att A para 42).
  • A stress scenario is a story, a duration, a set of shocks and a counterbalancing capacity. Run name-specific, market-wide and combined cases over short and protracted horizons (APS 210 para 62).
  • The survival horizon is read from a daily path, not a straight line. The LCR can pass while the bank's own combined stress fails.
  • Reverse stress and sensitivity tell you which assumptions matter most. For Example Bank the large stable retail pool is the biggest single sensitivity.
  • Stress results must reach limits, the contingency funding plan and the Board (APS 210 para 37; APS 210 para 63; APS 210 para 65; APS 210 para 66). A plan is only as good as its lead times.
  • Funds transfer pricing credits sources and charges uses at matched maturity plus a liquidity premium, and adds a charge for contingent liquidity (APS 210 para 42). Apply each charge once.
  • Every behavioural, stress and pricing model needs an owner, a version, independent validation and a back-test. Every number in this document is invented for training and rebuilt by check22.py.
