04 APS 210 companion: the main body

Purpose: To let you open the main body of APS 210 (Prudential Standard APS 210 Liquidity, July 2025) at any paragraph and find, straight away, what it says in plain English, what it means for our team, and what proof we keep.

You will be able to:

  • Find the training entry for any paragraph from 1 to 72 of APS 210, and for the front pages of the standard.
  • Restate each paragraph in your own plain words, including every limb (a), (b), (c).
  • Say which duties fall on the Liquidity Reporting team, which fall on Treasury, Liquidity Risk, senior management or the Board, and which fall on APRA.
  • Name the evidence the team keeps to show compliance with each duty.
  • Use the obligation register at the end to check that every duty has an owner, a frequency and evidence.

Before you start: Read document 02 (Liquidity from zero) and document 03 (Your role, the team and the working year). You do not need to read this document in order. Use it as a reference beside the standard.

Time: About 3 hours to read once through. After that, use it as a look-up.

How to use this companion

APS 210 is the rulebook (called a prudential standard) that APRA, the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority, has set for how banks manage liquidity. "Liquidity" means having enough cash, or things quickly turned into cash, to pay what you owe when it falls due. If you have never thought about this, read document 02 first.

The standard has a main body (paragraphs 1 to 72) and three attachments. This document covers the main body. Document 05 covers Attachment A (the liquidity coverage ratio). Document 06 covers Attachments B and C.

Each entry has five parts:

  • What it says: the paragraph restated in plain English, with every limb kept.
  • In everyday words: the idea in the simplest terms.
  • What you do about it: the job this creates for the Liquidity Reporting team, or who owns it if it is not ours.
  • Evidence we keep: what proves we comply.
  • Learn more: the documents in this pack and the chapter of the knowledge guide (called "the Guide", file LIQUIDITY_GUIDE.md) to read next.

Three terms you will see everywhere:

  • Example Bank is the fictional bank we use for training. It is a locally incorporated Australian ADI with one legal entity and one currency.
  • ADI means authorised deposit-taking institution: a bank, building society or credit union that APRA has allowed to take deposits.
  • Board means the board of directors, the people ultimately responsible for the bank.

From the controller: Many duties in the main body sit with the Board, senior management or Liquidity Risk, not with us. You still need to know them, because the numbers you produce are what they rely on. When an entry says "No action for our team", it means we do not do the task, not that we can ignore it.

Typical practice: Names of teams, committees, deadlines and limits used in the "What you do about it" parts are Example Bank conventions. They are typical practice; confirm with your controller. They are not in APS 210.

Front matter of the standard

Determination page: who made APS 210 and what it did

What it says: The first page is a legal instrument called "Banking (prudential standard) determination No. 3 of 2025". It is made by Sean Carmody, who signs as a delegate of APRA (an Executive Director in the Policy and Advice Division), on 13 April 2025. It does two things. First, under subsection 11AF(3) of the Banking Act 1959 it revokes (cancels) the earlier determination, No. 13 of 2022, including the earlier version of APS 210 made under it. Second, under subsection 11AF(1) of the Banking Act it determines (makes) the new Prudential Standard APS 210 Liquidity, in the form set out in the schedule. The new standard applies to all ADIs and authorised NOHCs to the extent provided in paragraphs 2 to 5 of the standard (APS 210 paras 2 to 5). The instrument commences on 1 July 2025. A short interpretation note says that APRA means the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority, and that "ADI", "authorised NOHC" and "prudential standard" have the meanings given in subsection 5(1) of the Banking Act. The schedule says the standard is the document that follows. The page carries the legislation register stamp "Authorised Version F2025L00653 registered 06/06/2025".

In everyday words: This page is the legal "stamp" that turns the document into binding law. It cancels the old 2022 version and starts the new July 2025 version on 1 July 2025. An authorised NOHC is a non-operating holding company: a company that sits above a bank group but does not run a bank itself.

What you do about it: Make sure that every document we cite, and every procedure we write, refers to the July 2025 version (F2025L00653). When someone quotes APS 210, check they are not quoting the 2022 version, which no longer applies. When APRA issues a new version, your controller will ask you to compare it with this companion and update the entries.

Evidence we keep: A copy of the current authorised version from the Federal Register of Legislation (legislation.gov.au), saved with the date we downloaded it, and the version history kept in the policy library.

Learn more: Document 03 (Your role, the team and the working year); Guide Ch 2 (How a standard is made: section 11AF).

Objectives and key requirements box

What it says: The box says the standard requires an ADI to adopt prudent practices in managing its liquidity risks and to maintain enough liquidity to meet its obligations as they fall due across a wide range of operating circumstances. It then lists three key requirements. An ADI must: (1) have a risk management framework to measure, monitor and manage liquidity risk that is commensurate with (matches) the nature, scale and complexity of the institution; (2) maintain a portfolio of liquid assets sufficient in size and quality to withstand a severe liquidity stress; and (3) maintain a robust funding structure appropriate for its size, business mix and complexity.

In everyday words: The standard asks three things of a bank. Have a sound system for watching liquidity. Hold enough easy-to-sell assets to survive a bad month. Fund yourself in a steady, sensible way. The first is good management, the second is the liquidity coverage ratio (LCR), and the third is the net stable funding ratio (NSFR).

What you do about it: Keep these three ideas in mind as the "why" behind every task. The LCR you produce daily supports requirement 2. The NSFR you produce monthly supports requirement 3. The monitoring, limits and reports in paras 36 to 43 support requirement 1 (APS 210 paras 36 to 43). Your sign-off pack should make it easy for a reader to see all three.

Evidence we keep: The liquidity risk management framework documents, the daily LCR and monthly NSFR records, and the Board and ALCO (asset and liability committee) papers that show each of the three requirements being met.

Learn more: Document 02 (Liquidity from zero); Guide Ch 2 (Objectives); Guide Ch 1 (The two Basel ratios on one page).

Authority

APS 210 para 1: Authority

What it says: This prudential standard is made under section 11AF of the Banking Act 1959 (the Banking Act) (APS 210 para 1).

In everyday words: The Banking Act is the law. Section 11AF gives APRA the power to write binding standards like this one. That is why the standard is not just advice: breaking it is breaking a legal requirement.

What you do about it: No action for our team; owned by APRA, which makes the standard. Know that APS 210 has legal force, so errors in the numbers we report can have regulatory consequences.

Evidence we keep: A copy of the authorised standard from legislation.gov.au (see the determination page entry above).

Learn more: Document 03 (Your role, the team and the working year); Guide Ch 2 (The Banking Act 1959 and what an ADI is).

Application

APS 210 para 2: Who the standard applies to

What it says: APS 210 applies to all authorised deposit-taking institutions (ADIs) under the Banking Act (APS 210 para 2).

In everyday words: Every bank, building society and credit union that takes deposits under an APRA licence must follow it. Example Bank is one of them.

What you do about it: No action for our team beyond knowing that Example Bank is in scope. Example Bank is a locally incorporated ADI. That matters later, because some rules apply only to locally incorporated ADIs and some only to foreign ADIs (a foreign ADI is a branch in Australia of an overseas bank).

Evidence we keep: Example Bank's authorisation to carry on banking business, held by Compliance.

Learn more: Document 03 (Your role, the team and the working year); Guide Ch 2 (The Banking Act 1959 and what an ADI is).

APS 210 para 3: Level 1 and Level 2

What it says: A reference to an ADI in the standard means two things (APS 210 para 3):

  • (a) the ADI on a Level 1 basis, which means the ADI by itself; and
  • (b) a group of which the ADI is a member, on a Level 2 basis, which means the whole group added together.

In everyday words: The standard is applied twice: once to the bank alone, and once to the bank plus its subsidiaries as one big picture. Example Bank has one legal entity, so its Level 1 and Level 2 numbers are the same.

What you do about it: For Example Bank, run one calculation and label it as both Level 1 and Level 2. State on each report that Level 1 equals Level 2 because there are no subsidiaries. If Example Bank ever acquires or sets up a subsidiary, tell your controller at once, because we would then need two sets of numbers.

Evidence we keep: The entity structure chart, and a note on each LCR and NSFR working paper confirming the basis (Level 1 equals Level 2).

Learn more: Document 10 (Monitoring beyond the two ratios); Guide Ch 2 (Level 1 versus Level 2).

APS 210 para 4: Who ensures Level 2 compliance

What it says: If an ADI to which the standard applies is (APS 210 para 4):

  • (a) the holding company of a group of bodies corporate, the ADI must make sure the standard's requirements are met on a Level 2 basis, where applicable; or
  • (b) a subsidiary of an authorised NOHC, the authorised NOHC must make sure the requirements are met on a Level 2 basis, where applicable.

In everyday words: Someone at the top of the group must make sure the whole group meets the rules, not just the bank. If the top is a bank, the bank does it. If the top is a non-operating holding company, that company does it.

What you do about it: For Example Bank (single entity), no extra work. If the structure changes, the controller will decide who prepares the Level 2 consolidation, and we would need group-wide data feeds.

Evidence we keep: The group structure chart and the Board-approved statement of which entity carries the Level 2 duty.

Learn more: Document 03 (Your role, the team and the working year); Guide Ch 2 (Authorised NOHC).

APS 210 para 5: Securitisation carve-out

What it says: Take an ADI, or a member of its Level 2 group, that is the originating ADI in a securitisation and meets the operational requirements for regulatory capital relief under Prudential Standard APS 120 Securitisation (APS 120). That ADI may leave out the assets and liabilities of the relevant special purpose vehicles (SPVs) when it calculates its minimum liquidity holdings (MLH) and its net stable funding ratio (NSFR), as applicable. But for the liquidity coverage ratio (LCR), the ADI must include the cash flows from the SPVs' assets and liabilities related to its securitisation activities in its reported amounts (APS 210 para 5).

In everyday words: A securitisation is when a bank bundles loans (such as mortgages), sells them to a separate company (a special purpose vehicle) and that company raises money from investors. If the bank has properly passed the risk on, it may ignore that company's balance sheet for the NSFR. For the 30-day LCR it must still count the cash going in and out, because that cash can still affect the bank in a stress.

What you do about it: Example Bank has no securitisation in the base position. If we ever have one, ask the controller to confirm in writing whether it meets the APS 120 operational requirements. Then build two treatments into the calculations: leave the SPV out of the NSFR, but keep its cash flows in the LCR.

Evidence we keep: The written APS 120 assessment and the Board or ALCO approval of the treatment; a note on each working paper showing how the SPV was treated in the LCR and the NSFR.

Learn more: Document 07 (Data and classification); Guide Ch 4 (Securitisation); Guide Ch 2 (The securitisation carve-out).

APS 210 para 6: Commencement

What it says: The standard commences (starts) on 1 July 2025 (APS 210 para 6).

In everyday words: The July 2025 rules have applied since 1 July 2025.

What you do about it: Use the July 2025 rules for every calculation from that date. If you are asked to recalculate a period before 1 July 2025, check which version applied then and tell your controller.

Evidence we keep: The version of the standard that was in force on each reporting date, kept in the policy library.

Interpretation

APS 210 para 7: Defined terms in bold

What it says: Terms defined in Prudential Standard CPS 001 Defined terms appear in bold the first time they are used in the standard (APS 210 para 7).

In everyday words: APRA keeps one shared dictionary (CPS 001) for all its standards. A word in bold means "look it up in that dictionary".

What you do about it: When a bold term is unclear, check CPS 001 before assuming a meaning. Keep the bank's own glossary aligned with CPS 001 for terms that affect classification.

Evidence we keep: The glossary or data dictionary, with a note of the CPS 001 version it follows.

Learn more: Document 02 (the glossary of the 40 words); Guide Ch 10 (Glossary).

APS 210 para 8: APRA's powers are exercised in writing

What it says: Whenever the standard lets APRA use a power or discretion, APRA must use it in writing (APS 210 para 8).

In everyday words: If APRA gives us a concession or sets a higher requirement, it will be a written document. A phone call or a chat in a meeting does not count.

What you do about it: If anyone says "APRA told us we could treat this differently", ask for the written determination. Do not change any classification or rate until the controller has seen the letter. File each written decision where the whole team can find it.

Evidence we keep: A register of all written APRA decisions, exemptions and higher-minimum requirements, with the letters.

Learn more: Document 11 (Returns to APRA and public disclosure); Guide Ch 2 (APRA's powers under the standard).

APS 210 para 9: References to laws and standards are to the current version

What it says: Unless the standard says otherwise, a reference in it to an Act, Regulations or Prudential Standard means that instrument as in force from time to time (APS 210 para 9).

In everyday words: When APS 210 mentions another rule, such as CPS 220 or APS 120, it means the latest version of that rule, even if it changes after July 2025.

What you do about it: When a linked standard changes, tell the controller so we can check whether our treatment changes. Keep a list of the linked standards (CPS 001, CPS 220, APS 120, the Banking Act, the Corporations Act 2001).

Evidence we keep: The list of linked instruments and the date each was last checked.

Learn more: Document 03 (Your role, the team and the working year); Guide Ch 2 (The standards that touch liquidity).

Definitions

APS 210 para 10: Associated entity and financial institution

What it says: Two definitions apply (APS 210 para 10):

  • (a) an associated entity is an associated entity within the meaning of section 50AAA of the Corporations Act 2001; and
  • (b) a financial institution, for the avoidance of doubt, includes a money market corporation, a finance company, a friendly society and the trustee of a superannuation or pension fund, of a public unit trust or mutual fund, or of a cash management trust.

In everyday words: "Associated entity" is a company closely tied to the bank under the Corporations Act. A "financial institution" is wider than just a bank. It includes super funds, managed funds, cash management trusts, finance companies, friendly societies and money market firms. This matters because deposits from financial institutions are treated as less reliable in a stress, so they run off faster in the LCR and earn less stable funding in the NSFR.

What you do about it: Make sure the customer master data has a field that flags each wholesale customer as a financial institution or not, using this list. Check new customer types against it. A superannuation fund's at-call deposit is a financial institution deposit, not a corporate one. Review the flag whenever a customer's business description changes.

Evidence we keep: The customer classification rules and a sample test showing financial institutions are flagged correctly; the list of associated entities.

Learn more: Document 07 (Data and classification); Guide Ch 4 (Counterparty types used in APS 210); Guide Ch 10 (Glossary).

Key principles

APS 210 para 11: Sound management and a robust framework

What it says: An ADI is responsible for the sound management of its liquidity risk. It must have a robust (strong, dependable) framework to manage that risk accordingly (APS 210 para 11).

In everyday words: The bank cannot hand this problem to anyone else. It must have a proper, written, working system for managing liquidity.

What you do about it: Treat our daily LCR and monthly NSFR production as a part of that framework. That means documented procedures, a second-person review and saved evidence. Keep our procedure documents current and tell Liquidity Risk if any step is manual or fragile.

Evidence we keep: Procedure documents (documents 08 and 09 are the pack's versions), review sign-offs, and the framework document approved by the Board.

Learn more: Document 12 (Controls, incidents and escalation); Guide Ch 8 (Governance).

APS 210 para 12: Enough liquidity at all times, and a survival buffer

What it says: An ADI must at all times keep enough liquidity to meet its obligations as they fall due. It must also hold a minimum level of liquid assets to survive a severe liquidity stress (APS 210 para 12).

In everyday words: The bank must always be able to pay what is due today and must also keep a safety stock of easy-to-sell assets in case a bad month hits. The LCR measures the safety stock.

What you do about it: Produce the LCR every business day so management knows the buffer is there "at all times" (APS 210 para 55). Flag any day on which the ratio moves toward an early-warning level so Treasury can act before the minimum is reached.

Evidence we keep: Signed daily LCR working papers for every business day, and a log of days on which a trigger level was reached.

Learn more: Document 08 (Producing the daily LCR); Guide Ch 5 (What the LCR is, who must meet it).

APS 210 para 13: Stable funding on an ongoing basis

What it says: An ADI must make sure its activities are funded with stable sources of funding on an ongoing basis (APS 210 para 13).

In everyday words: Do not fund long-term loans with money that can disappear next week. Long-term lending should be matched with money that stays put. The NSFR measures this.

What you do about it: Produce the NSFR monthly and compare it with the Board appetite (Example Bank convention: at least 110%) and the early-warning trigger (Example Bank convention: 115% amber). Report month-on-month movements and explain them.

Evidence we keep: Signed monthly NSFR working papers and the movement analysis.

Learn more: Document 09 (Producing the NSFR, end to end); Guide Ch 6 (NSFR).

APS 210 para 14: Telling APRA about liquidity concerns

What it says: An ADI must inform APRA as soon as possible of any concerns it has about its current or future liquidity position, and of its plans to address those concerns. In particular, if an ADI experiences a severe liquidity stress, it must notify APRA immediately and say what action is being taken (APS 210 para 14).

In everyday words: No surprises. If the bank is worried, even about the future, it tells its regulator early and says what it is doing. In a real crisis it tells APRA at once.

What you do about it: If your numbers show a forecast breach, a sharp fall in the ratio or a failed reconciliation that could hide a breach, escalate to the controller immediately. The controller will take it to Liquidity Risk and the senior management who contact APRA. Do not contact APRA yourself. Our job is to make sure the facts reach decision-makers fast and are right.

Evidence we keep: Escalation emails and incident log entries with times; the notification to APRA (kept by whoever sent it) and the action plan.

Learn more: Document 11 (Returns to APRA and public disclosure); Document 12 (Controls, incidents and escalation); Guide Ch 2 (Notification duties).

Board and senior management responsibilities

APS 210 para 15: The Board is ultimately responsible

What it says: The Board of directors is ultimately responsible for the sound and prudent management of the ADI's liquidity risk. The ADI must keep a liquidity risk management framework that matches the level and extent of liquidity risk it faces from its activities. For a foreign ADI, the Board's responsibilities in the standard are carried out by the senior officer outside Australia (APS 210 para 15).

In everyday words: The directors carry the final responsibility. For a foreign bank branch there are no local directors, so the senior officer overseas takes that role. Example Bank has a Board.

What you do about it: Make sure our reports reach the Board clearly. Check that the numbers sent to the Board Risk Committee (quarterly, Example Bank convention) match the signed daily and monthly figures.

Evidence we keep: Board and Board Risk Committee papers and minutes showing liquidity reports were received.

Learn more: Document 03 (Your role, the team and the working year); Guide Ch 8 (Governance).

APS 210 para 16: What the framework must contain

What it says: The framework must include at least (APS 210 para 16):

  • (a) a statement of the ADI's liquidity risk tolerance, approved by the Board;
  • (b) the liquidity management strategy and policy, approved by the Board;
  • (c) operating standards (for example policies, procedures and controls) for identifying, measuring, monitoring and controlling liquidity risk in line with the risk tolerance;
  • (d) the funding strategy, approved by the Board; and
  • (e) a contingency funding plan.

In everyday words: There are five building blocks: how much risk we accept, how we plan to manage liquidity, the day-to-day rules, how we fund ourselves, and the emergency plan. We sit mainly in block (c): the procedures that measure and monitor.

What you do about it: Hold a map of which of our procedures and reports serve each building block. For block (c) the daily LCR, monthly NSFR and their controls are ours. For blocks (a), (b), (d) and (e) we supply data when asked and check that our limits and triggers match what the Board has approved.

Evidence we keep: The five framework documents with approval dates, and our procedure set.

Learn more: Document 03 (Your role, the team and the working year); Document 12 (Controls, incidents and escalation); Guide Ch 8 (Governance).

APS 210 para 17: Board duties on people and annual review

What it says: The Board must ensure that (APS 210 para 17):

  • (a) senior management and other relevant personnel have the necessary experience to manage liquidity risk; and
  • (b) the liquidity risk management framework and the liquidity risk management practices are documented and reviewed at least annually.

In everyday words: The right people, and written down, checked at least once a year.

What you do about it: Complete your training and competency sign-off (document 14). Make sure our procedures are reviewed and re-dated at least once a year and that changes are recorded. Support the annual review by listing any control weaknesses and process changes during the year.

Evidence we keep: Training and competency records; version-controlled procedures with the annual review date; the Board's annual framework review minute.

Learn more: Document 12 (Controls, incidents and escalation); Document 14 (Exercises and assessments); Guide Ch 8 (Governance).

APS 210 para 18: Board reviews liquidity reports

What it says: The Board must review regular reports on the ADI's liquidity position and, where necessary, information on new or emerging liquidity risks (APS 210 para 18).

In everyday words: The directors must actually look at the numbers regularly, and be told early about anything new that could cause trouble.

What you do about it: Produce the data for the Board report on time and accurately: the LCR, NSFR, their trends, buffers against appetite and early-warning flags. Include a clear short commentary when a number moves or a trigger is hit.

Evidence we keep: The Board liquidity report pack for each meeting, with our signed supporting working papers.

Learn more: Document 10 (Monitoring beyond the two ratios); Guide Ch 8 (Governance).

APS 210 para 19: What senior management must do

What it says: Senior management must, at a minimum (APS 210 para 19):

  • (a) develop a liquidity management strategy, policies and processes in line with the Board-approved liquidity tolerance;
  • (b) ensure the ADI maintains sufficient liquidity at all times;
  • (c) decide the structure, responsibilities and controls for managing liquidity risk and for overseeing the liquidity positions of all legal entities, branches and subsidiaries in the jurisdictions where the ADI is active, and set these out clearly in the liquidity policies;
  • (d) ensure the ADI has adequate internal controls to protect the integrity of its liquidity risk management processes;
  • (e) ensure stress tests, contingency funding plans and holdings of liquid assets are effective and appropriate for the ADI;
  • (f) establish reporting criteria stating the scope, manner and frequency of reporting for each recipient (such as the Board, senior management and the asset and liability committee), including who is responsible for preparing each report;
  • (g) set the procedures and approvals for exceptions to policies and limits, including escalation procedures and follow-up actions for breaches of limits;
  • (h) closely monitor current trends and market developments that may bring significant, unprecedented and complex challenges, so that the liquidity management strategy can be changed in good time; and
  • (i) continuously review information on liquidity developments and report to the Board regularly.

In everyday words: Senior managers run the machine. They write the policies, make sure there is enough liquidity, set who does what, make sure controls work, decide who gets which report and how often, decide how exceptions are approved, watch the outside world, and keep the Board informed.

What you do about it: Limb (f) names the preparer of each report, and that is often us. Check that our production schedule matches the written reporting criteria, and tell the controller if it does not. For limb (g), follow the written escalation steps when a limit is breached and keep a record. For limb (d), keep our own controls (reconciliations, review, change control) working and evidenced.

Evidence we keep: The reporting criteria matrix; our delivery log showing each report was produced on time; the exceptions and breaches register with approvals.

Learn more: Document 03 (Your role, the team and the working year); Document 12 (Controls, incidents and escalation); Guide Ch 8 (Governance).

APS 210 para 20: Understanding links between risks

What it says: Senior management and the Board must be able to show a thorough understanding of the links between funding liquidity risk and market liquidity risk, and of how other risks (credit, market, operational and reputation risks) affect the ADI's overall liquidity risk management strategy. Funding liquidity risk is the risk that an ADI may not be able to meet its financial obligations as they fall due. Market liquidity risk is the risk that liquidity in financial markets, such as the market for debt securities, may reduce significantly (APS 210 para 20).

In everyday words: Two ways to get stuck. Funding liquidity risk: the money we owe falls due and we cannot pay. Market liquidity risk: we try to sell assets to raise cash but the market is too thin, so we get a poor price or no buyer. The two feed each other, and other risks such as bad loans, a system failure or damage to our reputation make both worse.

What you do about it: When you comment on a ratio move, explain it in terms of both: did funding change (outflows) or did the asset buffer change (value or eligibility)? Highlight where a fall in market prices cut the value of our liquid assets. Include a line on external market conditions in the monthly pack if Liquidity Risk asks.

Evidence we keep: Movement analyses and ALCO papers that show this reasoning.

Learn more: Document 02 (Liquidity from zero); Document 10 (Monitoring beyond the two ratios); Guide Ch 1 (Funding liquidity risk and market liquidity risk).

Liquidity risk management framework

APS 210 para 21: Liquidity risk tolerance

What it says: The liquidity risk tolerance defines how much liquidity risk the ADI is willing to take on. It must be documented and appropriate for the ADI's operations, strategy and role in the financial system (APS 210 para 21).

In everyday words: The bank decides in writing how much liquidity risk it is prepared to live with. Our internal limits, such as an LCR of at least 120% (Example Bank convention), come from this.

What you do about it: Keep a copy of the current Board-approved tolerance statement and the limits derived from it. Check each limit in our reports against the latest approved version before every monthly pack.

Evidence we keep: The approved tolerance statement and the limit table showing the approval date.

Learn more: Document 10 (Monitoring beyond the two ratios); Guide Ch 8 (Governance).

APS 210 para 22: Annual review of tolerance

What it says: The liquidity risk tolerance must be reviewed at least annually, to reflect the ADI's financial condition and funding capacity (APS 210 para 22).

In everyday words: The tolerance is not set once and forgotten. It is checked at least every year against how the bank is actually doing.

What you do about it: Before the annual review, prepare a summary of the year: how often the LCR and NSFR came close to the appetite levels, the lowest and average values, and any breaches. This helps the Board judge whether the tolerance is right.

Evidence we keep: The annual summary we supplied, and the Board minute recording the review.

Learn more: Document 03 (Your role, the team and the working year); Guide Ch 8 (Governance).

APS 210 para 23: Tolerance must allow survival of a prolonged stress

What it says: In setting the tolerance, the Board and senior management must make sure it lets the ADI manage its liquidity position in a way that it can withstand a prolonged period of stress (APS 210 para 23).

In everyday words: The limits must be tight enough that the bank could get through a long bad patch, not just a day or two.

What you do about it: Supply stress test results and survival-horizon data when asked (see APS 210 paras 62 to 66), so the Board can judge whether the tolerance is tough enough.

Evidence we keep: Stress test results linked to the tolerance review papers.

APS 210 para 24: Tolerance states the trade-off between risk and profit

What it says: An LCR ADI must maintain an adequate level of unencumbered high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) to meet its liquidity needs for a 30 calendar day period under a severe stress scenario, in accordance with Attachment A. APS 210 para 61: APRA may require a higher NSFR

What it says: APRA may require an LCR ADI to keep a higher minimum NSFR where APRA considers it appropriate, including if it has concerns about the ADI's funding or liquidity risk profile or the quality of its liquidity risk management (APS 210 para 61).

In everyday words: APRA can raise the NSFR bar for a bank it worries about.

What you do about it: As for the LCR: act only on a written requirement (APS 210 para 8), update limits and templates, and tell the controller.

Evidence we keep: APRA's written requirement and the dated change to limits.

Learn more: Document 09 (Producing the NSFR, end to end); Guide Ch 2 (APRA's powers under the standard).

Stress testing

APS 210 para 62: Regular stress tests

What it says: In addition to the requirements in (APS 210 paras 52 to 56), an LCR ADI must run stress tests on a regular basis for a variety of short-term and protracted (long-running) institution-specific and market-wide stress scenarios, individually and in combination. The purpose is to identify sources of potential liquidity strain and make sure current exposures stay in line with the ADI's liquidity risk tolerance (APS 210 para 62).

In everyday words: Ask "what if?" regularly. What if only we get into trouble? What if the whole market does? What if both, for a day or for months? Then check we are still inside our own risk limits.

What you do about it: The scenarios are designed and run by Liquidity Risk. We supply the starting position data, with the same classification as the LCR and NSFR, and reconcile it to the signed numbers. We also help check that the LCR's own severe stress assumptions are applied correctly (see document 05).

Evidence we keep: The data extracts we gave to Liquidity Risk; the reconciliation of that data to the signed LCR.

Learn more: Document 10 (Monitoring beyond the two ratios); Guide Ch 1 (Stress: idiosyncratic, market-wide and combined).

APS 210 para 63: Using stress test outcomes

What it says: The stress test outcomes must be used to adjust the ADI's liquidity management strategy, policies and positions, and to develop effective contingency plans to deal with liquidity stress events (APS 210 para 63).

In everyday words: A stress test is only useful if the results change what the bank does.

What you do about it: No action for our team beyond supplying data; owned by Liquidity Risk, Treasury and the Board. Ask Liquidity Risk how results change limits, and update the limits in our reports when they change.

Evidence we keep: Records showing which limit changes came from which stress test.

Learn more: Document 10 (Monitoring beyond the two ratios); Guide Ch 8 (Governance).

APS 210 para 64: Level 1, Level 2 and business lines

What it says: Stress tests must let the ADI analyse the effect of stress scenarios on its Level 1 and Level 2 liquidity positions, and on the liquidity positions of individual business lines (APS 210 para 64).

In everyday words: Test the whole bank, the whole group, and each business line separately.

What you do about it: Make sure the position data we give can be cut by business line as well as in total. For Example Bank Level 1 equals Level 2 (see APS 210 para 3).

Evidence we keep: Business line mapping tables used in our data extracts.

Learn more: Document 07 (Data and classification); Document 10 (Monitoring beyond the two ratios).

APS 210 para 65: Documenting, reporting and using stress test results

What it says: An LCR ADI's stress test scenarios and related assumptions must be well documented and reviewed together with the results. Results, vulnerabilities and any resulting actions must be reported to, and discussed with, the Board and APRA. Results must be built into the ADI's strategic planning process and its day-to-day risk management, and must be explicitly considered when setting internal limits (APS 210 para 65).

In everyday words: Write down the scenarios and assumptions, report results to the Board and to APRA, and use them in planning and in setting limits.

What you do about it: The report to APRA is a notification, owned by Liquidity Risk and senior management (see document 11). We make sure the data behind it is accurate and signed. Keep the data version used for each stress test.

Evidence we keep: Stress test documentation; Board and APRA reports; data version records.

Learn more: Document 10 (Monitoring beyond the two ratios); Document 11 (Returns to APRA and public disclosure); Guide Ch 8 (Governance).

APS 210 para 66: Stress tests feed the contingency funding plan

What it says: An LCR ADI must decide how to include the results of stress tests when assessing and planning for potential funding shortfalls in its contingency funding plan (APS 210 para 66).

In everyday words: If the stress test shows a gap, the emergency plan must say how to fill it.

What you do about it: No action for our team beyond data; owned by Liquidity Risk and Treasury. If a stress test result produces a shortfall number, check that it uses the same position data as our signed figures.

Evidence we keep: Cross-reference between stress test results and the plan's funding sources.

Learn more: Document 10 (Monitoring beyond the two ratios); Document 12 (Controls, incidents and escalation).

Local operational capacity

APS 210 para 67: Foreign ADI annual LOC assessment

What it says: A foreign ADI must assess its local operational capacity (LOC), meaning its ability to liquidate assets and make or receive payments without help from staff outside Australia. It must do this at least annually and give the results to APRA on request (APS 210 para 67).

In everyday words: Could the Australian branch of an overseas bank keep working if its overseas head office staff were cut off? Only foreign ADIs must test this.

What you do about it: No action for our team; this applies to foreign ADIs only. Example Bank is locally incorporated.

Evidence we keep: Not applicable to Example Bank.

Learn more: Guide Ch 2 (Foreign ADI: branch versus subsidiary).

APS 210 para 68: LOC scenario

What it says: In its LOC assessment, a foreign ADI must at least consider a scenario combining time zones, different public holidays and an offshore operational risk event, under which it would operate, including making and receiving payments, for at least three business days without help from staff outside Australia. It may assume related-party operations within Australia continue to work normally (APS 210 para 68).

In everyday words: The test must cover three business days of working alone, with awkward time zones and holidays.

What you do about it: No action for our team; applies to foreign ADIs only.

Evidence we keep: Not applicable to Example Bank.

Learn more: Guide Ch 2 (Foreign ADI: branch versus subsidiary).

APS 210 para 69: APRA may add requirements after an LOC assessment

What it says: APRA may impose additional requirements on a foreign ADI if its LOC assessment highlights issues about its ability to liquidate assets and make or receive payments without help from staff outside Australia (APS 210 para 69).

In everyday words: If the test shows weakness, APRA can add rules.

What you do about it: No action for our team; applies to foreign ADIs only.

Evidence we keep: Not applicable to Example Bank.

Learn more: Guide Ch 2 (Foreign ADI: branch versus subsidiary).

Exceptional Liquidity Assistance

APS 210 para 70: Exceptional Liquidity Assistance

What it says: An ADI under severe liquidity stress may ask the Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) for Exceptional Liquidity Assistance (ELA). Before approaching the RBA, the ADI must notify APRA immediately of its liquidity concerns and its intention to request ELA. It must provide any information APRA or the RBA asks for about its ELA request. It must have the operational capability to provide information with its application for ELA, or within other timeframes APRA sets (APS 210 para 70).

In everyday words: ELA is the central bank's emergency lending to a bank that has run out of other options. Tell APRA first, then be ready to hand over detailed data fast. The July 2025 version added the requirement to be operationally able to provide information.

What you do about it: Be ready to produce a complete, reconciled, as-at-today data set (balance sheet by product and counterparty, unencumbered asset list with valuations, funding maturities, LCR and NSFR) at short notice, possibly within hours. Test this readiness at least once a year, for example during the contingency funding plan test (APS 210 para 51). Never send data to APRA or the RBA yourself; senior management decides.

Evidence we keep: The ELA data pack template and test results with timings; records of any request and what we supplied.

Learn more: Document 11 (Returns to APRA and public disclosure); Document 12 (Controls, incidents and escalation); Guide Ch 2 (Exceptional Liquidity Assistance).

Adjustments and exclusions

APS 210 para 71: APRA may adjust or exclude requirements

What it says: APRA may adjust or exclude a specific requirement of the standard in relation to an ADI or authorised NOHC (APS 210 para 71). The footnote refers to subsection 11AF(2) of the Banking Act (see footnote 1 below).

In everyday words: APRA can give one bank a tailored version of a rule, or lift a rule for it. It does this in writing (APS 210 para 8).

What you do about it: If any adjustment applies to Example Bank, record it in the register of APRA decisions and apply it exactly as written. Never assume an adjustment applies to us because it applies to another bank.

Evidence we keep: APRA's written adjustment or exclusion and the dated change to our procedures.

Learn more: Document 11 (Returns to APRA and public disclosure); Guide Ch 2 (APRA's powers under the standard).

Previous exercise of discretion

APS 210 para 72: Relying on a past exemption

What it says: An ADI must contact APRA if it wants to rely, for the purposes of complying with APS 210, on a previous exemption or other exercise of discretion by APRA under an earlier version of the standard (APS 210 para 72).

In everyday words: An old permission does not carry over by itself. Before relying on one, ask APRA.

What you do about it: List any old APRA permissions the team still relies on (for example, a past classification or concession). Raise each with the controller, who will contact APRA. Until APRA confirms, do not rely on it.

Evidence we keep: The list of past permissions and the correspondence with APRA confirming or ending each one.

Learn more: Document 11 (Returns to APRA and public disclosure); Guide Ch 2 (APRA's powers under the standard).
